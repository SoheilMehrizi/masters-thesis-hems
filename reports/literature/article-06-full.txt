Abstract
Day-ahead dispatch of integrated energy systems (IESs) is commonly solved by mixed-
integer linear programming when objectives, constraints, and forecasts are fully specified.
In practice, however, operators often express temporary preferences in natural language,
and day-ahead forecasts inevitably deviate from realized operation. These two conditions
make a fixed optimization interface difficult to use without additional modeling effort.
This paper proposes DARC (Decompose-Act-Repair-Critique), a constraint-diagnostic LLM
agent framework for day-ahead dispatch of campus-level microgrid-type IES with under-
specified operating requirements. DARC combines three language modules, namely a
Decomposer for temporal structure, a Resolver for numerical schedule generation, and a
Critic for root-cause diagnosis, with deterministic repair, constraint checking, and metric
evaluation. In all main experiments, the Decomposer is instantiated by its determinis-
tic rule-based variant for reproducibility, so the reported results reflect a loop with two
LLM modules (Resolver and Critic). The Projector repairs candidate schedules where
possible, while the Checker supplies mathematical facts that ground the Critic’s feedback.
DARC does not model forecast uncertainty through sets or scenarios; robustness to forecast
deviation is pursued operationally, through margin-aware repair and checker-grounded
revision, and is assessed empirically. Experiments on a campus-level microgrid IES testbed
show that DARC can incorporate natural-language preferences, improve realized feasibility
under forecast noise relative to both a point-forecast MILP and an interval-robust MILP
baseline in terms of strictly feasible configurations, at higher operating cost, and produce
diagnostic feedback that is robustly more useful than an ungrounded LLM diagnoser under
judge models from three families, and at least as useful as checker facts alone, with its ad-
vantage concentrated in actionable revision guidance. DARC is therefore positioned not as
a replacement for MILP , robust formulations, or model predictive control on fully specified
problems, but as a complementary interface for operational settings where preferences and
forecasts are not completely formalized.

1. Introduction
As low-carbon transition and campus energy digitalization continue to advance,
distributed photovoltaics (PV), battery storage, combined heat and power (CHP) units,
and electrified heating devices are increasingly coordinated through unified energy-
management platforms. Energy hubs and multi-carrier optimal power flow provide early
modeling foundations for integrated energy systems (IESs) [1,2]. Mancarella [3] reviewed
multi-energy-system concepts and evaluation models, while Mohammadi et al. [4] summa-
rized the development of the energy-hub model. Compared with single-carrier electricity
dispatch, day-ahead IES dispatch must simultaneously coordinate grid transactions, bat-
tery charging and discharging, CHP operation, electric boilers, and thermal storage. Its
difficulty arises from the joint effects of electricity–heat coupling, inter-temporal storage
dynamics, and device-level safety limits.
When objectives, constraints, and exogenous forecasts are fully formalized, MILP and
MPC remain mainstream and verifiable tools for IES dispatch. Zhang et al. [5] reviewed
modeling and solution methods for integrated electricity–heat operation, and Wu et al. [6]
proposed a multi-time-scale economic MPC framework that responds to forecast updates
and state changes through rolling optimization. Constrained MPC has a mature theoretical
basis [7] and has been applied to microgrid and risk-aware operation problems [8,9]. This
paper therefore does not position LLMs as replacements for MILP or MPC on fully specified
mathematical models. Instead, it focuses on operational information that is not yet fully
formalized but still affects dispatch decisions.
Such under-formalized information commonly appears in two forms. The first is
operator preference. Requirements such as maintenance windows, asset-life management,
low-carbon operation, or peak-period output suppression are often expressed in natural
language during operation. They can be translated after the fact into inequalities, soft
objectives, or counting constraints, but this process requires manual modeling and is
difficult to scale to changing operational expressions. The second is forecast deviation.
Day-ahead load and PV forecasts inevitably differ from realized profiles, and a schedule
optimized for point forecasts may violate power balance, state-of-charge (SOC) bounds,
or terminal conditions on the execution day. Robust optimization characterizes the trade-
off between conservativeness and economic performance [10], and has been used in IES
planning and hybrid AC/DC microgrid energy management [ 11,12]. However, these
methods usually require uncertainty to be represented as sets, intervals, distributions, or
risk measures, and cannot directly process temporary operating preferences expressed in
free text.
LLMs provide a possible bridge between natural-language requirements and struc-
tured decision workflows. Retrieval-augmented generation enables language models to
use external memory [ 13], while ReAct and Toolformer show that LLMs can combine
language reasoning with external actions or tool calls [ 14,15]. In the energy domain,
Majumder et al. [ 16] discussed the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in the electric-
energy sector; Antonesi et al. [17] and Zhang et al. [18] further reviewed opportunities and
reliability challenges for LLM-enabled energy systems. Existing applications include inter-
pretable building control [19], optimization with unstructured building-energy data [20],
distribution-system state estimation [21], and virtual-power-plant analysis [22]. However,
day-ahead dispatch differs from question answering, explanation, or state recognition. It
requires 24 h numerical schedules that satisfy electricity–heat balance, device capacities,
SOC dynamics, and terminal constraints at every time step. If LLMs are introduced into
IES dispatch, their outputs must therefore be coupled with deterministic repair, constraint
checking, and feedback grounded in mathematical facts.

Based on these considerations, this paper proposes DARC (Decompose-Act-Repair-
Critique), a constraint-diagnostic LLM agent framework for day-ahead dispatch of campus-
level integrated energy microgrids under natural-language preferences and forecast un-
certainty. DARC uses LLMs for temporal-structure interpretation, numerical candidate
generation, and root-cause diagnosis, while deterministic programs perform repair, fea-
sibility verification, and metric evaluation. We state the scope of the claims explicitly.
Architecturally, each ingredient of DARC has clear precedents: delegating computation
to executable programs follows program-aided reasoning [ 23], iterative revision under
feedback follows self-refine-style loops [24,25], and repairing candidate actions onto the
feasible region parallels action projection in safe reinforcement learning [26]. The contri-
bution claimed here is therefore not the invention of this pattern but its transfer to and
instantiation for day-ahead IES dispatch with hard physical constraints, where the feedback
signal must be grounded in checker-computed mathematical facts rather than in model
self-evaluation. With respect to forecast deviation, DARC does not construct uncertainty
sets or scenario trees; committed plans are repaired and verified against realized profiles,
and the resulting feasibility improvement is an empirical property evaluated against both
point-forecast and interval-robust MILP baselines (Section 5.3). The main contributions are:
1. A domain-specific instantiation of the decompose–generate–project–critique pattern
for day-ahead IES dispatch. DARC partitions modules by function (temporal-structure
decomposition, numerical action generation, deterministic repair, and root-cause
diagnosis) rather than by device boundary, so that arithmetic and physical-consistency
checks are not delegated solely to linguistic reasoning.
2. Unlike generic LLM-as-judge feedback loops, DARC’s Critic separates the determinis-
tic constraint assessment, which is computed by a checker rather than the LLM, from
the LLM’s role of root-cause attribution. The resulting feedback signal therefore car-
ries not only the list of violated constraints but also a structured root-cause diagnosis
and concrete revision instructions. Judged evaluation, replicated with judges from
two additional model families, shows that checker grounding is load-bearing—the
ungrounded diagnoser collapses under every judge—and that the two-layer design is
at least as useful as checker facts alone in aggregate, with a judge-dependent margin,
while consistently leading in the actionability dimensions (revision specificity and
physical consistency) that feed the revision loop (Section 5.4).
3. Across three independent random seeds, DARC reduces operator-preference violations
relative to a preference-blind MILP baseline when preferences are supplied as free
text. Under realized forecast noise, it attains more strictly feasible configurations than
both the point-forecast MILP (16/27 vs. 0/27) and an interval-robust MILP baseline
(16/27 vs. 11/27), while the robust baseline achieves lower cost regret; we therefore
position DARC as a complementary interface rather than a replacement for robust
formulations. We report operating cost together with feasibility metrics to characterize
the trade-off introduced by the natural-language interface. All main experiments run
the rule-based Decomposer variant, i.e., two LLM modules in the loop (Section 4.2).

2.1. Optimization-Based Dispatch and the Limits of Complete Formalization
The foundations of IES dispatch can be traced to the energy-hub concept and multi-
carrier optimal power flow. Geidl and Andersson [ 2] formulated optimal power flow
across multiple energy carriers, and Geidl et al. [1] introduced the energy hub as a system
abstraction for multi-energy conversion. Mancarella [ 3] and Mohammadi et al. [ 4] later
reviewed multi-energy-system concepts, evaluation models, and energy-hub modeling
developments. These studies address the representation of multi-energy coupling and
provide the basis for the electricity–heat balance, storage dynamics, and device-limit
constraints considered in this paper.
Building on this foundation, MILP , MPC, and other mathematical programming
methods have long served as core tools for IES day-ahead dispatch. Zhang et al. [ 5]
reviewed modeling and solution methods for integrated electricity–heat operation. Alabi
et al. [ 33] further discussed optimization, forecasting, and machine learning within a
unified IES decision-making perspective. In the MPC literature, Mayne et al. [7] established
foundational results for constrained MPC, Parisio et al. [ 8] applied MPC to microgrid
operation, Hans et al. [9] introduced risk-averse MPC for uncertain islanded microgrids,
and Wu et al. [6] extended economic MPC to multi-time-scale IES operation. For objectives
and constraints that have already been formalized, these methods provide interpretable
and verifiable dispatch solutions.
Forecast errors and renewable fluctuations have motivated robust and stochastic
formulations. Bertsimas and Sim [10] characterized the price of robustness; Dong et al. [11]
proposed a hybrid robust-interval method for IES planning; and Fu et al. [12] used robust
optimization for hybrid AC/DC distribution systems with microgrid clusters. These
studies show that uncertainty can be incorporated into optimization models through sets,
distributions, or risk measures. By contrast, operator preferences are often expressed as
temporary text, with semantic ambiguity and contextual dependence. If each preference
must be manually converted into constraints or objective terms, the responsiveness and
scalability of the dispatch workflow are limited.

Reinforcement learning provides another dispatch paradigm by learning policies
through environment interaction rather than solving a complete optimization problem
for every operating day. Vázquez-Canteli and Nagy [27] reviewed reinforcement learning
for demand response; Ruelens et al. [28] controlled thermostatically controlled loads with
batch reinforcement learning; Bahrami et al. [29] applied deep reinforcement learning to
distribution-network demand response; and Cao et al. [ 30] optimized storage arbitrage
while accounting for battery degradation. Because energy-dispatch actions must respect
power limits, SOC bounds, and device constraints, safe and constrained reinforcement learn-
ing has become important. Garcia and Fernandez [34] surveyed safe reinforcement learning,
Achiam et al. [35] proposed constrained policy optimization, Berkenkampet al. [36] studied
safe model-based learning with stability guarantees, and Brunke et al. [37] reviewed safe
learning from control to robotics. Closer to energy dispatch, Pham et al. [26] introduced an
optimization layer for action correction, Wang et al. [31] proposed a physics-informed safe
RL approach for multi-energy microgrids, and Hou et al. [32] developed constraint-aware
RL for energy scheduling. DARC follows the design principle of candidate generation,
feasibility repair, and constraint verification, but it does not train an RL policy. Instead, it
uses an LLM to interpret operator preferences, generate candidate schedules, and explain
the root causes of constraint violations.
2.2. LLM Agents and Verifier-Grounded Feedback
Recent work on LLM agents has provided task decomposition, tool use, and feedback-
revision mechanisms from the language side. Wei et al. [38] showed that chain-of-thought
prompting elicits multi-step reasoning, and Kojima et al. [ 39] showed that zero-shot
prompts can also induce reasoning. Zhou et al. [40] decomposed complex problems into
easier subproblems, while Wang et al. [41] improved reasoning stability through multiple
reasoning paths. At the agent level, Yao et al. [14] proposed ReAct, Schick et al. [15] showed
that models can learn tool use, and Tree of Thoughts organized intermediate reasoning into
searchable candidates [42]. These studies indicate that LLMs are better suited to intermedi-
ate roles such as task decomposition, candidate generation, and tool orchestration than to
unverified direct decision-making.
Iterative-feedback methods further support this positioning. Madaan et al. [24] pro-
posed Self-Refine, in which a model critiques and revises its own output; Shinn et al. [25]
transformed external feedback into verbal memory for subsequent attempts; Park et al. [43]
demonstrated the role of memory, reflection, and planning in generative agents; and Shen
et al. [44] used an LLM as a controller to orchestrate external models. For numerical rea-
soning, Gao et al. [23] proposed program-aided language models, delegating computation
to executable programs. A related line of work translates natural-language optimization
problems directly into solver-ready formulations, from the NL4Opt competition [ 45] to
agentic modeling systems such as OptiMUS [ 46] and Chain-of-Experts [ 47]; Section 5.7
discusses how this formalize-then-solve interface relates to, and can be combined with,
DARC. When these ideas are applied to IES dispatch, the key requirement is that feedback
must be grounded in physical constraints. Power balance, device capacities, and SOC limits
are determined by equations and parameters, and should not be replaced by the subjective
judgment of a language model. Ruan et al. [48] and Majumder et al. [16] also emphasize
reliability and safety challenges when applying general-purpose LLMs in power systems.
DARC therefore does not let the Critic directly judge schedule quality; instead, the Critic
reads checker-computed constraint assessments and organizes violations, evidence, and
possible root causes into feedback for the next revision.
A complementary line of work concerns retrieval-augmented generation. Lewiset al. [13]
combined parametric language models with non-parametric external memory for knowledge-

2.3. LLMs in Energy Systems: Applications and the Remaining Gap
As LLM capabilities have expanded, energy applications have moved from general
question answering toward task-specific engineering workflows. Majumder et al. [ 16]
examined the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in the electric-energy sector, highlighting
opportunities for decision support while cautioning that general-purpose models do not
directly satisfy power-system reliability requirements. Antonesi et al. [17] systematically
reviewed transformers and LLMs across the energy sector, and Zhang et al. [18] summarized
their opportunities, challenges, and future roles. At the application level, Zhang and
Chen [19] combined Shapley values with LLMs to explain building-control decisions;
Xiao and Xu [ 20] proposed an LLM-based multi-agent framework for building-energy
optimization with unstructured data; Li et al. [ 21] used LLM-based in-context learning
for distribution-system state estimation; and Wang et al. [22] reviewed large AI models in
virtual power plants.
Overall, existing LLM-energy studies demonstrate clear value in knowledge retrieval,
unstructured-data processing, interpretation, and specialized estimation tasks. Most of
them, however, focus on explanation, recognition, or decision support rather than exe-
cutable long-horizon dispatch. Day-ahead IES dispatch poses a different challenge because
it requires numerical decisions that remain consistent with power balance, storage states,
device limits, and terminal conditions. As Table 1 indicates, model-driven optimization is
strong under complete formalization, reinforcement learning offers experience in candidate
generation and safety correction, and LLM agents provide language interaction and tool
orchestration. A practical interface for the setting considered here must combine free-text
input, long-horizon numerical scheduling, and hard physical-constraint verification. This
gap motivates DARC: LLMs interpret preferences, generate candidate schedules, and ex-
plain root causes, while the Projector and Checker perform repair, verification, and metric
computation. The feedback process is therefore supported by mathematical facts rather
than model self-evaluation.

4. Method: The DARC Framework
4.1. Overview
DARC (Decompose-Act-Repair-Critique) is a constraint-diagnostic LLM agent frame-
work for IES day-ahead dispatch. Its core design principle is strategies by LLMs, physics by
programs: LLMs handle temporal-structure understanding, numerical action generation,
and error root-cause diagnosis, while deterministic programs handle constraint repair,
feasibility verification, and metric computation. The Resolver is the only LLM module that
emits numerical dispatch variables; every other LLM module operates over symbolic or
textual representations. The Decomposer has both an LLM-based and a rule-based instanti-
ation sharing the same output schema; as stated in the abstract, the main experiments use
the rule-based variant (Section 4.2), so the empirical results in Section 5 certify a loop with
two active LLM modules (Resolver and Critic).

4.2. Stage I: Temporal Structure Decomposition
The Decomposer transforms the raw forecast x (and optional preference text π) into a
structured decision map m = Dθ(x, π) that provides a temporal skeleton for downstream
numerical generation. Its output contains no power values—only structural priors.
The economics and feasibility of an IES dispatch are typically determined by a small
number of critical periods and cross-carrier coupling relationships rather than by uniform
treatment of all 24 h. If the Resolver must independently discover these structures from
raw numerical tables on every call, its reasoning burden and error probability both increase.
The Decomposer factors this structural reasoning out of the Resolver’s job.

4.3. Stage II: Numerical Schedule Generation
The Resolver Rθ is the only LLM module that directly outputs numerical dispatch
variables. It consumes the day-ahead forecast x, the decision map m from the Decomposer,
and (optionally) the natural-language preference π, and emits a raw plan:
˜u = Rθ(x, m, π). (14)
DARC uses a single Resolver that simultaneously observes electrical, thermal, storage,
and grid variables rather than partitioning generation across multiple device agents. The
rationale is that IES dispatch difficulty arises primarily from inter-device coupling: CHP
simultaneously alters electrical and thermal supply; the electric boiler converts electrical
load into thermal supply; battery charge/discharge affects PV absorption and peak-hour
grid purchase. If multiple device agents generate independently, merged plans readily
exhibit local rationality but global imbalance.
When π is non-empty, the Resolver receives the natural-language preference as addi-
tional context in its prompt. The Resolver is instructed to interpret the preference and reflect
it in the output schedule but is not provided with any constraint enforcement mechanism.
The resulting compliance is therefore a prompt-conditioned approximation rather than a
hard guarantee—a fundamental property discussed in Section 5.7.
Because LLMs are unreliable for long-sequence numerical consistency, ˜u may contain
array-length errors, SOC drift, or power-balance violations. Therefore, ˜u is never used as
the final schedule and must pass through the Projector.
4.4. Stage III: Deterministic Repair Projection
The Projector is a deterministic repair layer that converts LLM dispatch output into
physically checkable candidate schedules:
u = P ( ˜u, x, C). (15)
The Projector is neither a MILP solver nor a cost optimizer: it does not search the
full feasible domain, and it does not generate optimal solutions independent of the LLM
strategy. Instead, it functions as a repair decoder, converting an LLM-proposed operating
mode into a physically checkable candidate plan. This separation enables the strategic
quality of the LLM’s output to be assessed independently of its arithmetic accuracy.
The Projector does not guarantee that every repaired candidate is strictly feasible.
Coupled limits or a poor proposed operating mode may leave residual violations; the
Checker remains the final authority on feasibility.
The Projector executes deterministic repairs in the following order: (1) device variable
bound clipping; (2) CHP ramp-rate correction; (3) thermal balance closure with forward TES
SOC correction; (4) TES terminal SOC repair; (5) battery charge/discharge mutual exclusion,
SOC bounds, and terminal SOC correction; (6) grid buy/sell closure of electrical balance
with mutual exclusion enforcement. The guiding principle is to preserve the LLM-proposed
operating mode wherever possible, intervening only to repair hard constraint violations.
4.5. Stage IV: Math-Grounded Diagnostic Critic
The Critic is the key module distinguishing DARC from generic LLM-agent pipelines.
It is organized into two layers: a deterministic mathematical assessment and an LLM-based
root-cause diagnosis conditioned on that assessment.

The Critic supplies the mathematical facts a together with the forecast x to the LLM,

first, and the decisions that should be preserved. The diagnostic feedback is therefore
actionable: it reports not only the violation but also its inferred cause and a suggested
correction. The LLM does not compute operating costs and does not judge constraint
satisfaction; it performs root-cause attribution and generates corrective suggestions based
on the deterministic facts provided to it. The output format is detailed in Appendix C.
4.6. Iterative Revision and Best-So-Far Selection
DARC employs closed-loop iterative revision. Given round- i candidate ui and di-
agnosis di, if the diagnosis determines that the plan meets quality thresholds, iteration
stops; otherwise, the diagnosis is fed back to the Resolver, which regenerates the schedule
conditioned on the prior plan and the diagnostic instructions:

4.8. Optional Extension: Case-Library Strategy Transfer
When an offline corpus of MILP-solved historical days is available, DARC supports an
optional retrieval-augmented strategy-transfer module inserted between the Decomposer
and the Resolver. Given a historical profile set {xj}Nj=1, MILP is solved offline to obtain ref-
erence dispatches umilpj ; from each solution, we extract a device operating-mode summary
gj (per-period charge/discharge/idle/high/low labels) and compressed numerical-scale
anchors qj (per-period averages, total throughput), forming a case library
L = {(xj, gj, qj, ϕj)}Nj=1. (21)
At inference time, top-k similar cases Kx = TopKj sim(x, xj) are retrieved by normalized
profile distance. Their operating-mode summaries and compressed numerical anchors are
formatted as structured strategy context for the Resolver. The current-day MILP solution is
never accessed at inference time; only historical solutions enter the library.
We emphasize that this extension isoptional: the main experiments in Section 5 run the
core pipeline (Algorithm 1) without case-library retrieval, and the ablation in Section 5.6
shows that the case library contributes modest economic guidance but does not materially
change feasibility behavior. Implementation details of the extension are provided in
Appendix B.

5.3. Forecast Robustness
For each day-type and seed, a noisy forecast is generated by applying multiplicative
Gaussian noise ε ∼ N(1, σ2) to PV , electric load, and thermal load; prices are observed
exactly. Each method solves on the noisy forecast, and the committed plan is realized against
the true profile via the deterministic projector. We sweep σ ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, covering
typical industry forecast-error magnitudes for short-horizon distributed photovoltaics and
demand. Realized cost is compared against the per-day oracle, defined as an MILP solved
on the true profile. The design yields nine independent observations per (method, σ) cell.
Because a point-forecast MILP is not designed to absorb forecast error, we addition-
ally benchmark MILP-robust, a box-uncertainty (Soyster-style) robust counterpart that
represents the standard margining response to forecast deviation within the optimiza-
tion paradigm [10,11]. MILP-robust solves the same model against the worst corner of
a per-hour box uncertainty set—electric and thermal loads scaled by (1 + γσ) and PV
by (1 − γσ)—and additionally tightens all battery and TES SOC bounds, including the
terminal windows, by a back-off of min(0.05, γσ), so that realized state drift stays inside
the true bounds. The conservativeness parameter is γ = 1 in Table 5; γ ∈ {0.5, 1.5} is
reported as a sensitivity check in the text. Rolling-horizon MPC [6–9] responds to the same
deviation through intra-day recourse; because every method in this experiment commits
to a single day-ahead plan that is then realized, an MPC comparison would change the
information structure of the task, and we leave it outside the present protocol.

stated plainly: DARC’s raw generate-and-repair reliability on tightly coupled days is not
sufficient for unsupervised use. Every one of these residuals is flagged by the deterministic
Checker, so the operational interface must retain the Checker as a hard gate, with fallback
to the MILP solution whenever the problem is fully specified—which is precisely the
positioning of Section 5.7.
5.6. Architectural Ablations
Two ablations relative to the full DARC pipeline are reported in Table 8.DARC w/o
critic replaces the Critic loop with a single-shot generation; strict feasibility collapses from
8/12 to 2/12 and mean residual violation rises from 18.9 kWh to 220.8 kWh. The math-
grounded Critic feedback loop is therefore the load-bearing component of the architecture.
DARC w/o cases removes the optional similar-day retrieval module; feasibility is unchanged
and the cost difference is modest, indicating that the case library, when available, provides
limited economic guidance but is not required for feasibility. The heuristic projector row
(a fixed-rule dispatcher passed through DARC’s projector) achieves 0/12 feasibility, con-
firming that the LLM-based reasoning layer contributes substantively beyond what the
deterministic projector alone delivers.
5.7. Discussion
On the fully specified cost-minimization task, MILP remains the rational choice.
DARC’s contribution is the two settings that MILP cannot ingest directly: natural-language
operator preferences and noisy forecasts. Because both enter as prompt context rather than
hard constraints, the resulting compliance is a prompt-conditioned approximation rather
than a guarantee. Operating cost is therefore reported as part of the feasibility–economy
trade-off rather than as a standalone optimization claim. Robust and stochastic MILP
variants address forecast noise under additional modeling assumptions but do not provide
a route to ingest natural-language preferences; DARC’s contribution is the unified prompt-
driven interface, not the per-setting numerical optimum. Section 5.3 now quantifies this
positioning directly: once a well-chosen uncertainty margin is supplied, MILP-robust is
the stronger answer on cost and mean violation, while DARC attains more strictly feasible
configurations without any uncertainty parameterization. The two occupy different points
on the specification-effort axis rather than competing on a single leaderboard.
An alternative division of labor: formalize-then-solve. A natural alternative dele-
gates less to the language model: an LLM translates the operator’s text into constraints
and objective terms, and a conventional MILP solves the resulting model, as pursued by
NL4Opt, OptiMUS, and Chain-of-Experts [45–47]. We consider this design complemen-
tary rather than competing, and often preferable when preferences are crisp, hard, and
expressible in the solver’s algebra: the hand-engineered optimizer-pref rows in Table 4
represent exactly the upper bound of such a pipeline, achieving full compliance at a
2.3–2.4% cost premium. Three limitations define the setting where DARC adds value.
First, mis-formalization is silent: an incorrectly translated constraint yields a schedule that
is confidently optimal for the wrong problem, and nothing inside the solver reports the
discrepancy; DARC instead verifies the final numerical schedule directly against system
physics, keeping the failure surface observable regardless of how the preference was inter-
preted. Second, some operator preferences are soft or under-specified (“avoid stressing the
transformer in the morning if it does not cost too much”), admitting no unique inequal-
ity; a prompt-conditioned Resolver can trade such intent off without forcing a premature
formalization. Third, the two designs compose: a formalizer could hand crisp inequalities
to the Projector as hard constraints while soft intent remains prompt context, with the
https://doi.org/10.3390/en19163817

Checker–Critic loop unchanged. We regard this hybrid as the most promising engineering
path and leave it to future work.
The main threats to the present evidence are the three-seed sample size, the single
LLM family (DeepSeek-V4-Flash), and the single-bus electrical and aggregated thermal
model. A fourth threat, the LLM judge in Section 5.4 sharing the diagnoser’s model
family, was addressed directly during revision. Three protocol features already limit
stylistic self-preference (the judge receives the ground-truth root cause, scores against an
anchored six-dimension rubric, and is blinded to diagnoser identity); in addition, the
evaluation harness used in this study decouples the judge provider from the diagnoser
provider, and the scoring was replicated with two cross-family judges on an identical
regenerated diagnosis batch (Table 7). The replication preserved the naive-diagnoser
gap and the full Critic’s lead in the actionability dimensions under every judge, while
the aggregate full-versus-facts margin proved judge-sensitive; the corresponding claims
in the abstract, contribution list, and Section 5.4 have been softened accordingly. An
ablation that feeds facts-only feedback into the closed revision loop, isolating how much
the diagnosis layer’s actionability matters operationally, is a natural follow-up we leave
to future work. None of these alter the directional findings but each constrains the scope
of the quantitative claims; broader cross-family and cross-network replication is deferred
to future work.
6. Conclusions
This paper has proposed DARC, a constraint-diagnostic LLM agent framework
that complements MILP-based day-ahead dispatch of campus-level integrated energy
microgrids in two operationally common settings that the standard solver cannot ingest
directly: natural-language operator preferences and noisy day-ahead forecasts. Experi-
ments on a campus-level microgrid IES testbed, run with the rule-based Decomposer
variant (two LLM modules in the loop) and evaluated across three independent random
seeds, show that DARC reduces preference violations relative to a preference-blind
MILP baseline without manual constraint engineering, and that under industry-typical
forecast noise, its plans achieve strict feasibility on 16 of 27 (day, noise, seed) configu-
rations against the realized profile while the point-forecast MILP achieves 0 of 27 and
an interval-robust MILP baseline achieves 11 of 27 at roughly half of DARC’s cost re-
gret. The math-grounded Critic, which pairs a deterministic constraint checker with
an LLM-based root-cause diagnoser, dominates an LLM-only diagnoser on a judged-
quality rubric under judges from three model families; relative to checker facts alone,
its judged advantage concentrates in revision specificity and physical consistency—the
actionability that feeds the revision loop—while the aggregate margin over facts alone is
judge-dependent (Section 5.4). On the fully specified, noise-free task, DARC remains
behind MILP (8/12 vs. 12/12 strictly feasible), with failures concentrated on the ther-
mally tight Winter-peak day (Section 5.5); the deterministic Checker gate is therefore an
integral part of the interface rather than an optional safeguard.
The architectural principle behind these results is to bind LLM reasoning to determin-
istic repair and verification: physical balance is repaired by a projector rather than by the
LLM, and the Critic’s feedback signal originates in checker-computed facts rather than in
LLM self-evaluation. The reported operating costs characterize the trade-off introduced by
the natural-language interface and should be interpreted jointly with feasibility.
Future work. Three directions remain open. First, the prompt-conditioned natural-
language interface yields partial compliance for hard preferences; a structured preference
channel that can express hard inequalities while preserving the natural-language entry
point would extend the practical envelope. Second, the present results use a single LLM

family (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) and a limited number of random seeds; cross-family replica-

extensions. Third, the testbed uses a single-bus electrical model and an aggregated ther-
mal model; a network-aware Projector that exposes power-flow and pipe-temperature
constraints to the same Critic loop would test whether the architecture generalizes to
distribution-level IES dispatch.
