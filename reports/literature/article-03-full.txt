fault detection and diagnosis: Application, evaluation and operational

H I G H L I G H T S
• A Diagnostic Bayesian Network for whole-HVAC system fault detection and diagnosis was developed.
• The DBN uses both knowledge-based and data-driven methods for parameter modeling.
• DBN performance was evaluated using historical data and on-site AHU fault experiments.
• Despite sensor uncertainty, it achieved a classification rate of 87.8% for AHU fault experiments.
• DBN application during HVAC operation was demonstrated by diagnosing a suspected real-world fault.

A B S T R A C T
Automated fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) can support building energy performance and predictive mainte­
nance by leveraging the vast amounts of data generated by building management systems. However, most work 
on FDD for HVAC systems focuses on individual components, neglecting complex system interactions and the cas­
cading effects of component faults. Diagnostic Bayesian Networks (DBN) offer a particularly promising approach 
for whole-building FDD due to their robustness, flexibility and scalability. This paper presents the development 
of a DBN for whole-HVAC FDD and its application in an office building in the Netherlands. The DBN uses aggre­
gated representations of all major building systems to detect which faulty components need further investigation 
and uses knowledge-based and data-driven methods for parameter estimation. The DBN’s performance and the 
effects of input parameter assumptions are assessed using historical data as well as experimental data obtained by 
introducing simultaneous faults into the air handling unit (AHU) controls. Although fault injection was restricted 
to AHU controls due to safety concerns, the model architecture represents all major HVAC systems and simultane­
ously diagnoses their faults. The results using historical data identified persistent sensor-related anomalies in the 
building, as well as possibly associated system and control faults. Despite these sensor issues, the evaluation using 
experimental data showed that the DBN achieved a classification rate of 87.8% for AHU faults while maintaining 
a low false alarm rate (3.8%). However, the miss rate was high, exceeding 25% in all cases. Finally, the DBN is 
demonstrated through its application to diagnose a suspected fault during operation. 
1 . Introduction
Modern building systems are complex, with numerous components 
and interactions between systems, buildings and occupants. Through 
sensors deployed throughout the heating, ventilation and air condi­
tioning (HVAC) system, building management systems (BMS) collect 
vast amounts of data, which potentially allow real-time monitoring 
of building operation and system performance optimization. However, 
the complexity arising from the coupling and interactions between 
components and controls makes it difficult to maintain fault-free op­
eration [1]. Therefore, buildings continue to suffer from energy waste, 
uncomfortable indoor environments and poor air quality due to malfunc­
tioning components and inadequate controls. Component degradation

Automated fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) offers a promising so­
lution to this problem by leveraging building automation data to detect 
faults in physical systems, diagnose their causes and potentially pro­
vide prognostic maintenance for degrading systems [ 2]. A number of 
methods for FDD in building systems can be found in the literature, in­
cluding HVAC-level energy efficiency, chillers, air handling units (AHUs) 
and sensors. Most research has focused on individual components, and 
in particular AHUs, chillers, sensors, vapor compression systems, and 
variable refrigerant flow systems, which account for more than 80% of 
published literature in the field [1]. Chillers and AHUs have been iden­
tified as the energy-using components in an HVAC system that are most 
likely to cause serious and costly problems [ 4], which might explain 
their predominance in building system FDD.
Real-world HVAC systems, however, typically comprise multiple, 
highly coupled subsystems that need to be operated and controlled si­
multaneously [ 5]. Single-component FDD methods might neglect the 
interdependencies between different components, leading to inadequate 
fault diagnosis in practice [ 6]. Faults occurring in one subsystem may 
propagate and influence other subsystems, leading to false alarms in 
those other subsystems [ 7]. Therefore, ensuring fault-free operation in 
whole building HVAC systems requires an integrated approach that can 
overcome these limitations. However, a review on FDD for building sys­
tems revealed that only 12% of all studies in the literature focused on 
the whole building system [8].
Among the proposed FDD methods in the literature, Diagnostic 
Bayesian Networks (DBNs) are particularly promising for whole build­
ing HVAC system FDD due to their robustness, flexibility and scalability 
[9]. DBNs draw upon a Bayesian statistical approach, in which the prob­
ability of faults is calculated according to the occurrence of symptoms. 
Compared to other methods, DBNs can address a number of different 
and potentially simultaneous faults [ 1], which is especially relevant at 
the whole HVAC system scale.
Despite their potential advantages, the application of DBNs to whole 
building HVAC systems has so far been limited [9]. This is in part due to 
the difficulty of modeling complex, highly integrated building systems. 
Furthermore, the complexity of the whole building HVAC system makes 
it difficult to obtain labeled fault data in the real world, which further 
complicates the evaluation of DBN performance in practice. The use of 
DBNs for whole building HVAC systems, including all generation and 
distribution systems, their validation using fault data collected through 
experiments in building systems, and their evaluation using real-world 
operational data have not been demonstrated in the literature.
1.1 . Scope of this paper
This paper presents the development of a DBN for a whole building 
HVAC system, as well as its testing, partial validation and application 
in a real case study building. Our methodology uses generic represen­
tations of individual systems within the building (boiler group, chiller 
group, hydronic groups, ventilation groups, and end user groups), such 
that the DBN can determine which subcomponents need to be further 
investigated by operators or by their own FDD methods where avail­
able. As part of the development of the model, both knowledge-based 
and data-driven methods for the definition of the DBN input parameters 
are tested and compared.
The DBN was developed for a case study building in the Netherlands 
and subsequently tested, partially validated and applied to investigate a 
suspected fault during operation. First, the DBN is used to detect faults 
during a two-year period (2022–2023) using historical data to demon­
strate that the DBN structure represents and actively diagnoses all major 
HVAC systems simultaneously. The performance of the DBN is then par­
tially tested using data from fault experiments carried out in the case 
study building’s AHUs during six days in March–April 2024. A sensitiv­
ity analysis is also carried out to verify the effects of input parameters 
on the model’s performance. While the experiments were restricted to 
AHU controls due to safety concerns, the model architecture represents

1.2 . Contribution of this paper
The work presented in this paper differentiates itself from previous 
research in a few ways. First of all, the focus on the whole HVAC sys­
tem, including heating, cooling and ventilation systems, has rarely been 
seen in DBN literature. For example, other papers focusing on interde­
pendencies in building systems have focused on specific system couples, 
such as heating and ventilation [ 6] or cooling and ventilation [ 7,10]. 
Furthermore, the development of our DBN combines knowledge-based 
and data-driven methods, as opposed to purely knowledge-based ap­
proaches (e.g., [ 11]). Unlike other works in the literature, we further 
carry out both qualitative evaluation and partial quantitative evalua­
tion of our model’s performance. The use of normal building operation 
as well as on-site experimental data provides insight into the challenges 
and limitations of DBNs in real-world applications, as opposed to the use 
of model-based data for DBN development [5]. Thus, this paper demon­
strates the application of DBNs to a real whole-building HVAC system, 
accounting for all system interactions, and its evaluation through both 
multi-year historical data and experimental fault injection under real 
operating conditions.
2 . Background
2.1 . FDD for whole building HVAC systems
Most works on whole-building FDD aim to detect anomalies in build­
ing energy demand data as potential symptoms for faulty building 
systems. Among these works, clustering and decision trees are the most 
popular techniques for anomaly detection at whole-building level [17]. 
Pattern recognition techniques, clustering and artificial neural ensem­
bling networks have also been used to detect outliers and anomalies in 
building energy consumption [18,19]. However, such methods only fo­
cus on the whole building level, without providing any information on 
which systems might be leading to the detected anomalies.
Other authors have proposed methods to detect faults in specific 
building systems based on whole-building energy consumption data. For 
example, principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation analysis 
[20,21] and recursive deterministic perceptron (RDP) neural networks 
[22] have been used for these purposes. Feature-based PCA along with 
weather and schedule-based pattern matching has also been proposed 
to differentiate normal and abnormal operation in a whole building sys­
tem [23,24]. However, this method is unable to isolate the root cause of 
cross-level faults and requires detailed analysis when various fault symp­
toms are observed [ 7]. Statistical Process Control (SPC) with Kalman 
filtering based on gray-box models has also been proposed to detect sud­
den and gradual faults in a whole-building HVAC system [25]. While the 
method is promising in terms of scalability and replicability, it has only 
been developed for two specific building systems and no validation with 
real data has been provided.
A key limitation of these fully data-driven methods is the need for 
labeled “normal” vs. “faulty” data, which can be difficult to collect in 
real-world applications. For example, while the most common faults 
for chillers have been well documented, it is hard to obtain labeled 
data for all common faults in the field [ 26]. Some recent advances in 
data-driven FDD seek to address this practical challenge by combining 
limited labeled samples with unlabeled data. In particular, data aug­
mentation techniques such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) 
and variational autoencoders (VAEs), and semi-supervised learning ap­
proaches have been applied to address the issues of limited labeled 
data and data scarcity. These methods have been successfully applied 
at the component scale, such as for AHUs [27,28], refrigeration systems 
[29,30] and rooftop units [ 31]. However, these methods still exhibit 
important limitations in practical HVAC FDD applications. Synthetic 
samples are constrained by the distributions learned from existing ob­
servations, while semi-supervised learning approaches primarily exploit 
abundant unlabeled operational data and remain dependent on a small 
amount of labeled “faulty” data. Consequently, both approaches offer 
limited capability to generalize to unseen faults and changing oper­
ating conditions, especially for highly interconnected whole-building 
HVAC systems. Therefore, applications in whole-building HVAC FDD 
are less common and achieve much more modest performance due to 
the complex interactions that arise at this scale [31].
Other challenges in real-world applications include insufficient sen­
sors, poor sensor accuracy, non-standardized sensor installation and 
control logic, missing data and an imbalance between faulty and fault-
free training data [ 32]. As a result, most data-driven FDD techniques 
in the literature use data from laboratory experiments or simulations 
to validate their methods, while most works using real building data 
only focus on building-scale energy anomalies [ 17]. However, labora­
tory experiments on single systems and simulations might not be able to 
replicate real-world conditions in highly integrated, large-scale HVAC 
systems. Furthermore, fully data-driven methods can have low inter­
pretability [4] and neglect valuable information in building operation, 
such as expert knowledge from facilities management and the perception 
and feedback of building occupants [33].
2.2 . DBNs for whole building systems
Diagnostic Bayesian Networks (DBN) are probabilistic graphical 
models in which faults and symptoms are represented as nodes in a net­
work connected by edges representing the causal relationships between 
faults and symptoms (Fig. 1). Fault nodes typically have two states (fault 
present or absent), whereas symptom states may also be binary or indi­
cate the strength of the evidence of the symptom (e.g., strong, medium, 
weak). Prior probabilities for each fault and conditional probabilities 
of a symptom being detected given the presence of a given fault are 
assigned based on either expert knowledge or historical data. By apply­
ing Bayes’ theorem, posterior probabilities for the presence of fault 𝐹𝑖
given the presence of symptom 𝑆𝑗 can be calculated from the prior and 
conditional probabilities:

real-world applications, as building professionals tend to be suspicious 
of FDD methods when they are not able to understand the model in­
ference mechanism [36]. Additionally, most FDD methods in the field

Furthermore, since they are probabilistic models, DBNs are robust to un­

features given the complexity of whole building HVAC systems. Finally, 
DBNs are expandable, as a model can be built from DBN submodels and 
can be set up using aggregated DBN models from a DBN model library 
[13]. This scalability can allow DBNs to be easily adapted as new systems 
are installed or if old systems are replaced.
Despite these advantages, limited work has been done on DBNs for 
whole building systems, as summarized in Table 1 . Work on whole-
HVAC DBNs often reduces the scale of the problem by focusing on 
specific system couples rather than on the whole HVAC system. For 
example, Chen et al. [7] developed a discrete Bayesian Network (DisBN) 
approach for diagnosing and isolating cross-level faults in a VAV HVAC 
system. Their work aimed to address the difficulty of obtaining prior 
probability distributions and conditional probability tables as required 
by Bayesian Networks. However, they specifically focused on nine dif­
ferent cross-system faults affecting the chiller and AHU systems rather 
than the whole HVAC. [6] proposed a multiple-model approach account­
ing for both HVAC subsystem interdependencies and changing modes of 
operation. In their approach, individual DBNs were developed for each 
mode of operation, using a combination of expert knowledge and his­
torical data, and were implemented on an HVAC system consisting of 
a zone to be conditioned, a boiler, pump, radiator, and an AHU. By fo­
cusing on specific modes of operation, however, they focused only on 
specific system couples rather than accounting for all interdependen­
cies. Furthermore, seasonal DBNs cannot detect simultaneous operation 
of the heating and cooling systems, which is one of the most common 
faults in commercial and educational buildings [38].
Another key difficulty is DBN evaluation, which is complicated by 
the lack of labeled fault data in highly integrated building systems. Some 
authors therefore evaluate their methods qualitatively by analyzing his­
torical data and assessing the feasibility of the fault isolation results 
[16]. For example, Taal and Itard [11] and Taal and Itard [13] devel­
oped a whole-HVAC DBN for a case study building in the Netherlands 
and demonstrated their methodology by analyzing their method’s ca­
pabilities for detecting various symptoms. These works demonstrate the 
potential for DBNs to be developed at different scales for different stages 
of fault isolation; however the performance of the DBN is not evaluated 
through quantitative metrics.
Other authors instead use simulations to introduce faults in a vir­
tual test bed for quantitative assessment [ 14]. For example, Verbert 
et al. [6] demonstrated their approach through two simulated faults. 
Similarly, Huang et al.  [5] present a fully data-driven method to de­
velop DBNs for cross-level fault diagnosis and isolation and evaluate it on 
a simulated HVAC system. In their simulated evaluation, their method 
performed similarly to methods incorporating expert knowledge while 
decreasing network complexity. However, the authors acknowledge that 
their methodology, like most data-driven approaches, is influenced by 
factors such as data volume, data quality and data information. These 
issues are exacerbated in real-world applications.
In summary, the literature on DBNs for HVAC system FDD shows a 
lack of works focused on the whole building and its interactions, with 
some works focusing on specific system couples (e.g., heating and venti­
lation, cooling and ventilation) rather than on the whole HVAC system. 
Furthermore, there is a lack of works using real-world data for valida­
tion, with most works using simulation results due to the difficulty of 
accessing labeled fault data. Finally, while the use of purely data-driven 
approaches in FDD is promising, their applicability beyond the specific 
boundary conditions related to the training data used can be limited 
[17]. Combining knowledge-based and data-driven approaches in DBNs 
provides a viable approach to improve the robustness and accuracy of 
whole-HVAC system FDD.
3 . Case study
In this paper, we developed a DBN for a whole HVAC system, eval­
uated it qualitatively and quantitatively, and tested it during operation 
in a case study seven-floor office building in the Netherlands ( Fig. 2). 
The building mainly houses office space, with large atria and open hall­
ways, as shown in a typical floor plan of the building. The building is 
equipped with two boilers, one chiller and two AHUs, both equipped 
with cooling coils and heat recovery wheels, but only one of which has 
a heating coil. The building’s operation is split into three main zones: 
East, West and Atrium. Each zone corresponds to one hydronic distri­
bution group, which supplies hot water to the radiators in each zone. 
Cooling is delivered by a chiller to the AHUs, which both supply all 
three zones.
According to the building’s control documents, heating systems are 
available when the outdoor temperature is below 20 ◦C, while cooling 
systems are available when it is at least 14 ◦C. These overlapping operat­
ing temperatures can potentially lead to simultaneous operation of the 
heating and cooling systems in spring and autumn. The hydronic sys­
tems are controlled in order to maintain a desirable supply temperature 
as a function of the outdoor air temperature and the demand for heating 
in the building.
Air is supplied to each office by variable air volume (VAV) systems 
that are controlled based on CO 2 measurements at the outlet of each 
room. During normal operation, the air distribution systems provide 
the minimum amount of ventilation necessary as long as CO2 measure­
ments stay below 400 ppm. The volume of air supplied is increased as 
CO2 increases, with maximum airflow provided when CO 2 levels ex­
ceed 1400 ppm. Cooling is supplied only by the ventilation systems 
and no thermostat is available in the offices. Building occupants do

The HVAC systems are equipped with temperature, pressure and

Mass flow and airflow sensors have higher noise content and measure­

excluded from HVAC systems. For example, ASHRAE Guideline 36 [41],

minimum recommended sensors. However, as a result, energy balance 
symptoms in the system cannot be detected. In this paper we therefore 
focus primarily on operational state and occupant comfort symptoms, 
which can be detected by the typical minimum sensor installation in 
buildings to support operational comfort.
4 . Methodology
The procedure for developing the DBN is shown schematically in 
Fig. 3. The first step involves defining the scope of the problem and 
systems to be considered. In the second step, the dependencies be­
tween faults and symptoms in the system are defined and conceptualized 
graphically in a Bayesian Network. Subsequently, the parameters re­
quired by the model, namely the prior and conditional probabilities 
for fault detection, are defined. Finally, we introduce the fault isolation 
methods and the metrics used for evaluation.
4.1 . Problem formulation
A DBN including all possible faults and symptoms in every sub­
component of the whole building HVAC system can quickly become 
impractically complex. In order to reduce the scale of the problem, we 
use aggregated system definitions (e.g., AHU, boiler, chiller), as has 
been done by other authors [ 6,20], without including faults in their 
sub-components. The advantage of such an approach is that it allows 
subsystem-specific methods, possibly developed by manufacturers, to be 
applied to trade components. Furthermore, it allows for subcomponent-
level methods to be updated or replaced if changes in the building’s 
subsystems are introduced over time. Fig. 4 shows the aggregated sys­
tem boundaries for two generator modules (boiler and chiller) and two 
air modules (AHU1 and AHU2).
The resulting aggregated system P&ID for the case study building is 
shown in Fig. 5. The two boilers are shown as the Boiler group, which 
supplies the three distribution groups and AHU1. The chiller delivers 
cooling to the two AHUs, which both supply all three zones and are 
controlled by CO2 sensors located in each room.
4.2 . Structure modeling
In developing the DBN structure, we first define all possible faults in 
the HVAC system, and then extract the symptoms that would arise from 
each fault from the P&ID.

4.2.1 . Fault definition
Each aggregated building system in Fig. 5  is assigned a generic 
component fault, such as “AHU1 fault”. Each system also has its own 
associated control strategy, and thus a generic control fault is also as­
signed to each (e.g., “AHU1 Controls” fault). Every sensor used to define 
a symptom in the DBN is also connected to a sensor fault represent­
ing possible bias or drift in the building. Finally, “Zone control” faults 
are included to represent inadequate use of room-level manual controls, 
namely the ability of occupants to adjust the radiators and open the 
windows. These are included in order to allow the incorporation of occu­
pant behavior into the diagnosis method in future studies. The resulting 
faults are shown as purple nodes in Fig. 6, while the complete list of 
faults along with their definitions and sample subcomponent faults is 
summarized in Appendix A.
4.2.2 . Symptom definition
Symptoms are defined by assessing which sensors downstream from 
any given faulty piece of equipment would be affected by the fault. Every 
sensor shown in Fig. 5 is considered a potential source of evidence of a 
fault in the building system. The resulting list of symptoms along with 
their definitions is summarized in Appendix B.

expected value by more than a threshold 𝜀𝑖. For example, a boiler supply 
temperature symptom is detected if during a given timestep:

4.2.3 . Symptom detection thresholds
Symptom detection thresholds are typically defined using domain 
knowledge and expert experience, but can also be derived from histori­
cal data [32]. In this paper, we considered both: on one hand, we tested 
fixed thresholds based on expert knowledge and building documenta­
tion; on the other, we assessed the use of thresholds based on historical 
data.
The fixed thresholds used in the first case, based on literature and 
building documentation, are summarized in Table 2 . Cooling system 
temperature symptom thresholds are lower than those for the hot water 
circuits due to their stronger effect on cooling system performance [13]. 
Finally, due to the need for accurate measurements for all building con­
trols, temperature sensor deviation thresholds were the most stringent. 
Here, we assumed temperature sensor deviation thresholds to be 50% 
lower than the corresponding system thresholds.
General knowledge-based assumptions can provide a good initial es­
timate and a comparable benchmark for DBNs in different buildings 
in the absence of further information. However, control strategies can 
change over time, such that these initial assumptions might not be 
representative of a system’s current state.
In order to avoid these deviations between expected and actual 
building operation, thresholds can also be assigned based on histori­
cal data. For example, Li et al. [35] set thresholds equal to three times 
the standard deviation of the fault-free historical data. While the use of 
data-driven thresholds might better represent actual building operation, 
it can also hide the effects of sensor bias in historical data. Therefore, 
the performance of data-driven thresholds based on different standard 
deviations is compared to reference literature values to assess the ef­
fects of different thresholds on fault isolation performance. In our study, 
we tested three different data-driven thresholds and compared them 
to the fixed, knowledge-based reference values: 𝜀𝑖 = 0.5⋅𝜎 𝑖, 𝜀𝑖 =𝜎 𝑖

4.2.4 . Causal relationships
Having identified all possible faults and symptoms, their causal re­
lationships are translated into a DBN, as shown in Fig. 6. Even though 
the faults are defined at the aggregated system level, the resulting DBN 
is complex and highly interconnected, with a total of 43 fault nodes, 50 
symptom nodes and 177 connecting edges. For readability, sensor faults 
are grouped as one node in this figure, although in reality each sensor 
fault is an individual node. Similarly, zone-level faults are grouped for 
readability. The relationship between symptoms and control faults is de­
fined based on the documentation for the building automation systems. 
For example, a CO2 sensor fault will lead to an AHU control symptom, 
since the AHUs are controlled by the CO 2 sensors in each room in the 
building.
4.3 . Parameter modeling
Once the DBN structure and relationships between fault and symp­
tom nodes have been defined, the DBN parameters need to be assigned. 
These are, namely: the fault node prior probabilities, which define the 
frequency of a given fault in building systems ( 𝑃(𝐹 𝑖) in Eq. (1)); and 
the conditional probabilities that define the likelihood that a symptom 
is detected when a given fault occurs (𝑃(𝑆 𝑗 |𝐹𝑖)). The model parameters 
considered for each of the evaluation cases for the DBN presented here 
are summarized in Table 3.
4.3.1 . Prior probabilities
Fault node prior probabilities define the frequency of a given fault 
occurring in a building system. Most real-world BMS data do not in­
clude labels indicating faulty and fault-free building system operation. 
Therefore, assigning the probability of a given fault occurring in a data-
driven way is difficult, so prior probabilities are more often estimated 
by experts [9].
In order to verify the effect of model parameter assumptions on the 
fault isolation performance of DBNs, we carried out a sensitivity analysis 
of prior probabilities. We considered all possible combinations of the 
following prior probabilities [34]:
• Component fault probability: 0.5%, 2%, 5%, 10%
• Control fault probability: 1%, 5%, 10%
• Sensor fault probability: 0.5%, 2%, 5%, 10%
Fault detection was carried out for each combination of the above 
probabilities and the effects on three performance metrics were assessed.
4.3.2 . Conditional probabilities
Conditional probabilities are the likelihoods of a symptom being 
present given that a fault is present and are usually contained in condi­
tional probability tables (CPT). As previously discussed regarding prior 
probabilities, however, there are generally insufficient reference data to 
define CPTs. Furthermore, a significant challenge is the exponential in­
crease in the number of parameters in the CPTs as the number of parent 
nodes increases [43]. Studies have shown that it is unreliable to directly 
generate the conditional probabilities for each state of the evidence node 
when there are more than four fault nodes [7].
In order to overcome these issues, studies often use Noisy-OR and 
Noisy-MAX gates to reduce the number of parameters that have to be 
estimated [10]. By assuming symptom independence given the parent 
faults, Noisy-OR and Noisy-MAX gates reduce the number of parameters 
required to specify a CPT from logarithmic to linear [44]. In this paper, 
all faults and symptoms were defined as binary variables (“present” vs. 
“absent”) and therefore the Noisy-OR gate was used to define all CPTs.
Here, again, three different scenarios were considered in order to 
assess the performance of the DBN under different assumptions. In the 
first case (“Uniform”), a uniform conditional probability that the child 
nodes will be false if a parent node is false of 95% is assumed [ 13]. In 
addition, a 𝐿𝐸𝐴𝐾 probability (i.e., the probability that a symptom is 
detected when the corresponding fault is absent) of 0% was assumed.

4.4 . Fault isolation and DBN evaluation
The final step in the FDD process is the diagnosis of the faults leading 
to the observed symptoms. In order to do so, the DBN structure and 
model parameters are implemented using the Discrete Bayesian Network 
model in pgmpy, a Python package for causal inference and probabilistic 
inference using Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and Bayesian Networks 
[46]. Given the symptom detection evidence obtained from the BMS 
data, the resulting fault probabilities are calculated by exact inference 
using the Variable Elimination algorithm in pgmpy. A fault is assumed 
to be present if the posterior probability is at least 50%.
The data collection frequency in the BMS system changes from sensor 
to sensor. Before symptom detection could take place, these data were 
resampled to match the lowest temporal resolution in the dataset, that 
is, a 10-minute frequency was used for all measurements. For numerical 
variables, the data were resampled by taking the mean of higher fre­
quency measurements, whereas for categorical variables the mode was 
used. Control signals were only recorded when their status changed;

4.4.1 . Detection and diagnosis timescale
Symptom detection and fault isolation can be carried out at differ­
ent timescales. Here, three different symptom detection timescales were 
considered: subhourly detection (i.e., using all samples in the dataset, 
at 10-minute frequency), hourly and daily. The use of all samples in the 
dataset can easily lead to false positives due to transient effects such as 
when systems are starting up and have not yet reached their setpoint. 
In order to avoid false positives, hourly and daily symptom detection 
strategies were defined. In the “hourly mode” case, the mode of all 
subhourly symptom states was taken as the hourly symptom state (i.e., 
for a given hour, if a symptom was present more often than it was ab­
sent, the symptom was considered present in that hour). For the second 
hourly detection case (“hourly thresholds”), symptom detection thresh­
olds were selected, whereby a symptom was only considered present if 
it was detected for two timesteps in a row, at least three times during 
that hour [45]. The same thresholds were used for daily detection (“daily 
thresholds”). Similarly, fault isolation at hourly and daily timescales was 
tested. Fault isolation was ultimately carried out on an hourly basis, as it 
is a timescale short enough for building operators to take action in real-
time applications, but also long enough to minimize transient effects for 
the faults considered.
4.4.2 . DBN evaluation
The DBN’s ability to isolate faults is evaluated, partially validated, 
and tested on a real-world application ( Table 3). The first evaluation 
is carried out using historical data from the years 2022–2023 to detect 
long-term trends in the building’s HVAC systems. This data was also 
used to define the data-driven thresholds presented in Section 4.2.3. In 
the absence of fault labels, it is impossible to verify the performance of 
the DBN using this data, but this analysis can demonstrate whether the 
model can diagnose different faults and shed light on the most frequent 
faults in the case study building.
The second evaluation is then carried out using data from exper­
iments carried out in the building’s AHUs in 2024. These data are 
used for partial model validation and to assess the performance of the 
whole-HVAC DBN in AHU fault isolation using labeled data. The ex­
periments and performance metrics used are described in the following 
subsections.
Finally, in the operational testing, a real-world application of the 
DBN during a suspected fault is demonstrated. In early September 2024, 
a surge in occupant complaints prompted facility management to in­
vestigate a potential issue with the ventilation systems in the building. 
The DBN was run for the period 1–10 September 2024 in order to ver­
ify whether the model was able to detect this issue and isolate its root 
cause.
4.4.3 . Fault experiments for performance assessment
The whole-HVAC system DBN’s performance was tested using experi­
mental data collected over six days in 2024. Different fault combinations 
were introduced into the controls for different subcomponents in each 
AHU module, as summarized in Table 4. In each of the fault cases shown, 
the normal operation of the AHUs was modified via the systems’ con­
trols to mimic a specific fault in the ventilation systems. However, since 
faults were injected via the control systems and control signals were used 
during symptom detection, all faults were detected as control faults.
Due to restrictions from the building operator and safety concerns, 
faults could only be introduced in the AHUs. Fault injection in other 
components, such as boilers and chillers, could therefore not be tested. 
Nevertheless, the experimental data are useful in verifying the DBN’s 
ability to detect AHU faults in different subcomponents and to isolate 
the faults in individual AHUs. Furthermore, the experimental data can 
be used to assess the cascading effects of AHU malfunction on other 
systems and whether the DBN can successfully identify the AHUs as the 
root cause of symptoms detected in other systems.
4.4.4 . Assessment metrics
In this research, we selected three of the most common performance 
metrics used to evaluate DBNs for building systems [47]. The first is the 
Classification Rate (𝐶𝑅), also known as accuracy [ 48–50], correct rate 
[51] and correct diagnosis rate [38]: 
𝐶𝑅= 𝑇 𝑃+𝑇 𝑁𝑇 𝑃+𝑇 𝑁+𝐹 𝑃+𝐹 𝑁 (5)
where 𝑇 𝑃 is the number of true positives, 𝑇 𝑁 is the number of true 
negatives, 𝐹 𝑃 is the number of false positives and 𝐹 𝑁 is the number 
of false negatives. This metric gives an indication of the model’s overall 
performance, combining its ability to correctly detect faulty and normal 
behavior. Therefore, the higher the CR, the better the network performs.
The second metric is the Miss Rate ( 𝑀𝑅), also known as missed 
detection rate [52] and false negative rate [38]: 
𝑀𝑅= 𝐹 𝑁𝑇 𝑃+𝐹 𝑁 (6)
This metric provides information on whether the DBN is unable to cor­
rectly diagnose faults in the system. A high 𝑀𝑅 would lead to faults in 
a building system not being addressed in a timely manner, leading to 
inadequate system performance.
Finally, we consider the False Alarm Rate ( 𝐹 𝐴𝑅), also called false 
alarm ratio [26] and false positive rate [38]: 
𝐹 𝐴𝑅= 𝐹 𝑃𝐹 𝑃+𝑇 𝑁 (7)
In contrast to the MR, this metric assesses whether the DBN raises nonex­
istent issues during normal behavior. A high 𝐹 𝐴𝑅 would therefore imply 
that maintenance might be sent to check systems during normal perfor­
mance. Over the long run, this can lead to real detected faults being 
ignored under the assumption that a false alarm is being raised.
In addition to these metrics common in DBN evaluation, we have 
considered three further metrics commonly used in data-driven FDD 
applications. These are Precision ( 𝑃 𝑟), F1-score ( 𝐹1) and Balanced 
Accuracy (𝐵𝐴), which are calculated as follows:
𝑃 𝑟= 𝑇 𝑃𝑇 𝑃+𝐹 𝑃 (8)
𝐹1 = 2⋅𝑃 𝑟⋅(1 −𝑀𝑅)𝑃 𝑟+ (1 −𝑀𝑅 ) (9)
𝐵𝐴= 12 ( 𝑇 𝑃𝑇 𝑃+𝐹 𝑁 + 𝑇 𝑁𝑇 𝑁+𝐹 𝑃 ) (10)
Another common metric in data-driven FDD is Recall, which is simply 
equal to (1 −𝑀𝑅) , so it is omitted from our discussion.

5.2 . Partial experimental evaluation
The DBN’s performance was tested by carrying out fault detection 
on a dataset collected during fault experiments in the building’s AHUs. 
Due to the aforementioned constant issues with sensors in the building, 
the majority of the detected faults are sensor-related. Therefore, in or­
der to correctly assess the component and control faults in the DBN, 
the results presented in the rest of this section exclude sensor faults. 
If AHU faults can be accurately diagnosed, then DBN can be expected 
to perform acceptably in spite of the building’s malfunctioning sensor 
infrastructure.
5.2.1 . DBN performance evaluation
Table 5 shows the performance metrics for the different symptom 
threshold assumptions and conditional probability cases summarized in 
Table 3. Each possible combination of component, sensor and control 
fault prior probabilities was tested, and the resulting range of results 
for each performance metric is shown as the mean and standard de­
viation for each metric. Given that labeled fault data is only available 
for the AHUs, true positives and false negatives were only calculated for 
faults in the AHU controls themselves. Furthermore, simultaneous faults 
in different AHUs were simply counted as concurrent individual faults. 
Therefore, if during a simultaneous fault only one of the two faults is 
detected, one true positive and one false negative will be assigned. Given 
the level of aggregation of this DBN at system level, simultaneous faults 
in different subcomponents are considered as a single fault in the AHU.
In general, the choice of prior probabilities for component and con­
trol faults barely has any impact on the performance of the DBN. The 
effect is strongest for the P&ID-weighted scenario, which in any case is 
the worst performing of the three scenarios. Therefore, the exact val­
ues selected for the prior probabilities do not appear to have a strong 
influence on the fault isolation capabilities of the DBN in most cases. 
The reason for this is that although prior probability assumptions do af­
fect the predicted posterior fault probabilities, the effect is not strong 
enough to change the ranking of the assigned faults. Therefore, despite 
the changes in posterior probabilities, the most likely fault in each case 
remains the same. Thus, the diagnosis accuracy of the DBN is not af­
fected by the range of prior probabilities assumed, which is consistent 
with previous findings in the literature [7,33,34].
The uniform and weighted conditional probability cases perform sim­
ilarly well in terms of CR, with the uniform case performing slightly 
better in terms of MR and slightly worse in terms of FAR. The P&ID-based 
case performs worst due to the assumption that the closest system to a 
given symptom is most likely to cause it. Therefore, symptoms caused by 
cascading effects from a fault in one system were incorrectly assigned 
to the system closest to the sensor that detected the symptom. In the 
final version of the DBN, the uniform conditional probability case was 
selected due to its better performance in terms of CR and MR while 
maintaining an adequately low FAR.
Regarding prior probabilities, the symptom threshold of three stan­
dard deviations appears to be too large for this DBN. This threshold 
achieves the lowest FAR but also the highest MR, indicating that the 
threshold is not stringent enough and therefore allows at least 50% of 
faults to go undetected. The other symptom thresholds perform simi­
larly in terms of both CR and MR, with the fixed, uniform thresholds 
performing worst in terms of FAR.
All versions of the model achieve CRs of more than 80%, which is 
well above the 70% threshold presented by Wang et al. [48]. The DBN’s 
diagnostic performance is better than reported 51–66% rates for an FDD

adequate performance with higher uncertainty and without the need for 
labeled data for training.

achieved in three of the cases is still comparable to single-system FDD 
approaches in the literature [38,52]. The MR is likely to be strongly af­

faulty. On the other hand, the fact that over 70% of the AHU faults could 
still be detected in spite of the high degree of uncertainty in the sensor 
readings is an indication of the method’s robustness to uncertainty in 
the input data. The performance of the DBN in this paper is expected to 
improve if maintenance and calibration of the sensor infrastructure in 
the building are carried out.
The FAR is fairly low for most scenarios, with two thirds of the 
tested cases achieving the 5% threshold specified by other authors 
[26,52]. This means that the DBN does not raise incorrect faults, which 
would otherwise lead to unnecessary control and maintenance efforts by 
facility management.
Since the metrics are calculated only for systems for which labeled 
data was available (AHU1 and AHU2), the experimental data was not 
dominated by true negatives from other building systems, and therefore 
Precision remains high for all cases. In particular, the version of the 
DBN with uniform conditional probabilities and data-driven symptom

a balanced accuracy of 83.5%. This combination therefore achieves the 
most balanced performance in terms of correctly detecting faults and 
not raising false alarms, achieving the best performance in three of the 
six metrics considered.
5.2.2 . Fault isolation by subcomponent fault type
Fig. 10 shows hourly fault detection results from the selected version 
of the model (Table 3) for each of the days when an experiment was car­
ried out. Since the DBN uses control signals to detect whether a system 
is operational or not, the introduced faults are all detected as control 
faults. This is consistent with reality, as the faults introduced in the ex­
periments were indeed introduced through the control systems, not by 
introducing physical damage into the subcomponents themselves.
Two main competing fault diagnoses are observed. During exper­
iments F–H (air pressure setpoint faults), an “East hydronic control” 
fault is detected instead of AHU1 control. The same happens during 
experiments N–O (supply temperature setpoint faults), when an “East 
hydronic” fault is also detected. The second major competing fault is 
“Boiler”, which is detected instead of “AHU1 control” both at the start 
of experiment R and during experiments T–U (simultaneous HCV and 
HRW control faults). This is due to an unexpected cascading effect on 
the building control systems, where the experiments carried out in the 
AHU caused the boiler temperature to increase ( Fig. 11). Since AHU2 
does not include a heating component, this effect is only observed for 
AHU1. The same happens during the aforementioned simultaneous fault 
experiments T–U, when a boiler fault is detected instead of an AHU1 
control fault for a number of time steps.
These cascading effects cannot be directly explained based on the 
building’s control logic and demonstrate some of the difficulties of FDD 
in highly coupled real-world HVAC systems. Furthermore, they cannot 
immediately be categorized as false positives, since the cause for these 
cascading effects is unknown and might indeed be related to a pre-
existing fault in these systems and their controls. Nevertheless, in order 
to quantitatively assess DBN performance during each individual exper­
iment, we consider any fault diagnosis made during a false negative as 
a false positive diagnosis in the other system. In other words, in order 
to calculate TP, FP, TN and FN, we consider three diagnoses: “AHU1 
control” fault, “AHU2 control” fault or a fault in any other component. 
Therefore, in experiment E we have two correct diagnoses (“AHU1 con­
trol” and “AHU2 control”), but for fault F we have one correct diagnosis 
and one false positive (“AHU2 control” and “East hydronic control”).
The resulting performance metrics by subcomponent fault experi­
ment type are summarized in Table 6. While Table 5 evaluates detection 
at AHU level using the entire monitoring period as ground truth (ex­
periment running vs. not), this is not directly transferable to individual 
subcomponents, since (i) attributing an AHU-level detection to a specific 
subcomponent outside its own experimental window is ambiguous; and 
(ii) using “no experiment” periods as the negative class for every sub­
component would repeatedly count the same true negatives for different 
experiments. Table 6 therefore restricts the evaluation window for each 
subcomponent fault to the runs of that specific experiment, and rede­
fines the confusion matrix accordingly: a true positive is a run where an 
AHU control fault flag is raised (correct detection); a false negative is 
a run where no fault is flagged (missed detection); a false positive is a 
run where the anomaly is instead attributed to a different component 
(misattribution); and a true negative is a run where no other component 
is falsely flagged. Consequently, the metrics in Table 6 characterize both 
detection sensitivity and attribution correctness within each experiment, 
rather than overall false-alarm behavior during fault-free operation. By 
comparing the detection of “AHU1 control” and “AHU2 control” faults 
to any other fault in the system, we do not have a large number of con­
sistently negative results for faults that are not detected, and therefore 
the number of positives and negatives is very balanced.
The DBN performs well for faults in the heating and cooling compo­
nents, namely the HRW (faults C–E, AHU2 faults I–J and Q) and HCV 
(faults I–K in AHU1). For the selected parameter combination, these 
faults are consistently detected in every experiment, achieving a CR of 
100%. On the other hand, the model struggles with supply fan faults, 
where fault A was detected only in AHU1 and fault B was detected in nei­
ther. Return fan faults in AHU2 (R–U), on the other hand, are correctly 
detected, with a CR of 87.5% due to a simultaneous “Atrium hydronic” 
diagnosis.
The DBN also performs very well for setpoint symptoms in AHU2 
(faults F–H and M–P) but struggles to detect the same faults in AHU1. 
This is especially noticeable for air pressure setpoint symptoms (F–H), 
for which the CR is only 50%, with an “East hydronic control” fault 
detected instead of AHU1. As discussed previously, this is not necessarily 
an actual misdiagnosis, but it is considered one here for this comparison. 
For temperature setpoint faults, on the other hand, the DBN achieves a 
high CR of 83.3%, with an “AHU1 control” fault mostly detected, though 
“East hydronic control” is detected once more.
Regarding fault isolation during simultaneous faults, the model per­
forms very well in detecting simultaneous faults in AHU1 on 31 March, 
but poorly on 1 April, resulting in a CR of 70%. Given that both AHUs 
supply the entire building, differentiating between faults in each of them 
can be challenging. In particular, when considering the aggregated sys­
tem level, the effects of certain faults might cancel each other out, 
which might help to explain why the DBN performs better for certain 
simultaneous fault combinations than others.

allowed the DBN to focus on indoor environmental quality-related symp­

shows the potential for occupant feedback to be incorporated into FDD. 
Given the large number of faulty sensors encountered in the building, 
occupant complaints provided a key source of information for facili­

FDD can therefore help to ensure desired IAQ conditions are maintained 
[54]. Such feedback can provide valuable complementary information 
for DBNs, but while its implementation has been proposed before [55], 
no such implementation can be found in the literature on whole-HVAC 
system FDD.
6.1 . Limitations and ongoing work
The DBN presented here covers the whole HVAC system, including 
all major components and their operation. Historical evaluation of the 
DBN results allowed us to assess general DBN performance and highlight 
the most frequent faults in the building systems over time. However, in 
the absence of labeled data, no performance metrics could be calculated 
during the period 2022–2023. We partially addressed this issue by intro­
ducing fault experiments in the building’s systems in order to assess DBN 
performance, though faults could only be introduced in the AHUs due 
to restrictions from the building operator and safety concerns. Further 
experimental work should therefore be carried out on the various com­
ponents of the HVAC systems in order to provide holistic validation of 
the DBN performance.
Another limitation of this approach is its reliance on building doc­
umentation, P&ID diagrams and control documents. The construction 
of whole-HVAC DBNs is time-consuming, requiring domain knowledge 
and detailed analysis of the existing systems. Although the overall 
framework used in this paper to define faults, symptoms, and their 
relationships is applicable to a wide range of HVAC systems, its im­
plementation in a new HVAC system still requires a moderate level of 
manual effort. First of all, the specific list of symptoms and faults con­
sidered depends on the available sensor infrastructure and the specific 
HVAC configuration. While the use of data-driven and literature-based 
parameters can help minimize the case study-specific inputs needed, 
the expected operating ranges and overall control logic depend on the 
target HVAC system, which means the specific fault-symptom relation­
ships, probability parameters, and thresholds must be adjusted to reflect 
the characteristics of the target HVAC system. Therefore, the required 
effort depends on the similarity between the source and target HVAC 
systems, with adaptation being considerably simpler for systems shar­
ing comparable configurations. In order to overcome this limitation, 
DBN libraries containing reusable fault, symptom and causal relation­
ship modules have been developed to support practitioners in adapting 
DBNs to new case studies, thereby improving the practical transferability 
of these approaches [42]. Additionally, further work should be carried 
out on the development of automated methods for DBN construction. 
Previous work on the use of large language models [56], computer vision 
[57] and deep learning approaches [58] has shown promising results in 
recognizing P&IDs automatically.
Analyzing the historical data demonstrated a large number of sensor 
faults in the case study building. Such consistent issues in the build­
ing sensing infrastructure raise significant uncertainty about any FDD 
method’s ability to detect faults in the building. For example, the use 
of data-driven thresholds based on sensor measurements that show un­
usual behavior can lead to biased symptom detection thresholds. Given 
the absence of fault-free historical data to define baseline thresholds, 
we compared the performance of the DBN using different data-driven 
thresholds based on different standard deviations in the historical data 
as well as literature-based reference values. The fault isolation results 
nevertheless carry additional uncertainty due to the faulty sensors used 
for symptom detection.
Despite this uncertainty, the DBN presented here was able to achieve 
a CR of 87.8% for the experimental faults introduced into the AHU 
controls. This finding confirms DBNs’ ability to deal with contradictory 
information and uncertainty. Nevertheless, this uncertainty might have 
contributed to the DBN’s difficulty in isolating fan faults and pressure 
setpoint faults. As a result, the MR remained high, with a minimum 
of 27.5% achieved during the sensitivity analysis. For the specific case 
study presented here, significant maintenance work should be carried 
out on the sensors in the building. Such an overhaul could be coupled 
with an upgrade of the building’s sensing infrastructure to incorporate 
the types of flow rate sensors needed to detect energy performance 
symptoms in the building.
In the absence of reliable sensors, the case study presented here 
stressed the importance of occupant feedback as a valuable complemen­
tary source of information in FDD. In subsequent work, the incorporation 
of building occupant feedback as a symptom and behavior as a potential 
fault in this whole-building DBN is being explored. This paper therefore 
represents an initial step towards FDD methods that can support both 
building system performance and occupant comfort.
7 . Conclusions
This paper presented the development of a DBN for a whole building 
HVAC system and its testing and partial validation in a real-world case 
study building in the Netherlands. The DBN focuses on cross-level fault 
diagnosis at the aggregated system level, accounting for all generator, 
distribution and end-use modules in the building.
The results of fault detection based on the long-term historical data 
indicate that the network is able to detect the most likely faulty compo­
nents at whole building HVAC scale. In particular, significant issues with 
the building’s sensing infrastructure, and CO2 sensors in particular, were 
detected. The evaluation of the DBN using data from AHU fault experi­
ments conducted on-site demonstrated that the DBN was able to achieve 
a classification rate of more than 85% in spite of the high degree of un­
certainty created by the sensing infrastructure. While false alarms were 
not a significant issue (FAR less than 5%), the miss rate was above 25% 
for all cases. As in other works in the literature, the choice of prior proba­
bilities proved to have a relatively minor impact on the results, while the 
“uniform” conditional probability case and data-driven symptom detec­
tion thresholds were found to perform best. The implementation of the

This study represents one of the few DBN implementations in 
the literature that include all major HVAC systems in the building. 
Furthermore, rather than utilizing purely qualitative evaluations of the 
results or quantitative evaluations based on simulated data, the DBN 
was tested using both historical and experimental data. The model ac­
tively diagnoses all major HVAC systems simultaneously, even though 
experimental validation was limited to AHU controls due to safety con­
cerns. In spite of the significant faults found in the building’s sensor 
infrastructure, the DBN was able to achieve an adequate fault detection 
performance and diagnose multiple simultaneous faults in the AHUs. 
Thus, this work advances the development of validated whole-building 
HVAC system FDD using DBNs.
