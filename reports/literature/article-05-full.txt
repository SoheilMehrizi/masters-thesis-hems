ABSTRACTS
Intelligent detection and diagnosis methods have shown great potential in identifying faults of building energy 
systems. However, practical applications remain limited due to its reliance on prompt engineering, the developed 
models, and a lack of interpretability. To address these challenges, this study develops an agentic fault detection 
and diagnosis (AFDD) framework that integrates the AI agent, ontology, and generalized diagnosis rules. The 
ontology can describe the knowledge of buildings and control logic. The generalized diagnosis rules can diagnose 
the fault of the actuator, sensor, load, plant in other control loops, and setpoint. And the AI agent combined with 
ontology and diagnosis rules generates interpretable diagnosis reports and suggestions. The AFDD framework is 
validated on a real-world heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) system. The result shows that the 
framework achieves information extraction for building energy system and supply air temperature control loop 
and realizes ontology extension for the supply chilled water temperature. All faults for the supply air sensor, 
temperature control valve, cooling capacity, supply chilled water temperature and unreasonable setpoint can be 
automatically diagnosed without misjudgment using data form one hour, one day, to one week. The diagnosis 
report including the fault number, breakdown, and detailed analysis and operation and maintenance suggestions 
are generated. In summary, the AFDD framework enhances automation, interpretability, and decision-making for 
building energy systems. It provides a new perspective for the next-generation intelligent diagnosis method.
1. Introduction
As one of the most resource-intensive sectors, the building industry is 
responsible for approximately 40 % of global primary energy con-
sumption and over 30 % of total carbon emissions. This offers consid-
erable potential for energy conservation and carbon reduction [1,2]. 
Building energy systems are an essential part of large public buildings, 
which consume significant energy [3,4]. In recent years, the rapid 
advancement of sensor, communication, computing, and control tech-
nologies has positioned automatic control as one of the effective stra-
tegies for optimizing energy efficiency [5]. Through the real-time 
monitoring and intelligent adjustment of operational parameters, 
automatic control systems can decrease energy consumption while 
increasing indoor environmental comfort [6]. At present, widely 
implemented automatic control strategies encompass open-loop control 
[7], closed-loop control [8], proportional–integral–derivative (PID) 
control [9], and model predictive control [10,11]. Automatic control 
enhances system stability and responsiveness compared to traditional 
manual operation or simple time-based scheduling. It facilitates dy-
namic optimization in response to fluctuating loads, changing climatic 
conditions, and varying user demands, further advancing overall energy 
performance.
However, the practical implementation of automatic control strate-
gies continues to face several challenges, including sensor failures, 
actuator stuck, higher load, unreasonable setpoint, and negative influ-
ence from other control loops [12–14]. For instance, Li et al. [15] 
discovered that adjusting the temperature control valve resulted in the 
control fault when the error of the supply air temperature was +2 ◦C, at 
which the control loop of the PID reached a stable time lag exceeding 
20.6 % . Similarly, Verhelst et al. [16] reported that faults in tempera-
ture sensors may induce economic losses in control performance ranging 
from 7 % to 1000 %. Consequently, fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) 
for control loops has become increasingly important in building energy

systems [17–19]. By monitoring and analyzing operational conditions, 
FDD facilitates the timely identification and localization of potential 
faults. This prevents abnormal increases in energy consumption and 
enhances system stability.
Many researchers have proposed different FDD approaches for 
building energy systems and control loops to facilitate automatic fault 
identification, isolation, and maintenance. The FDD approaches can be 
classified into three categories: model-based approaches [20,21], 
data-driven approaches [22,23], and knowledge-based approaches [24,
25]. Model-driven approaches are used to develop mathematical models 
that characterize the system’s behavior. Based on these models, residual 
sequences are generated and analyzed against predetermined thresholds 
to identify fault conditions. The effectiveness of this approach is closely 
tied to the accuracy of the system model, necessitating a certain level of 
domain expertise for practical implementation. With the development of 
the big data technology, data-driven approaches have attracted more 
and more attention. Data-driven approaches rely on many operational 
data and employ data mining techniques to analyze system performance 
for fault diagnosis, including principal component analysis, support 
vector machines, neural networks, and deep learning. While data-driven 
approaches provide significant advantages in terms of detection and 
ease of implementation, their performance is largely dependent on the 
accuracy and quality of data collected from system sensors and equip-
ment. Moreover, knowledge-driven approaches leverage causal models, 
detailed system descriptions, or expert knowledge to construct diagnosis 
rules for fault detection. These approaches do not depend on the quality 
of measurement and can be highly effective; however, they often require 
a substantial accumulation of expert knowledge, which can be both 
time-consuming and labor-intensive.
Recently, with the ongoing refinement of intelligent building oper-
ation and maintenance standards by international organizations such as 
ASHRAE, alongside the adoption of cutting-edge technologies, an arti-
ficial intelligence (AI) agent driven FDD approach has provided new 
ways for intelligent diagnostics and adaptive operation of various sys-
tems [26–28]. The AI agent is powered by large language models (LLMs) 
such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) by OpenAI and 
Gemini by Google [29]. It has demonstrated the ability to comprehend 
complex concepts, engage in sophisticated reasoning, and integrate in-
formation across multiple domains [30–32]. LLMs in the fault diagnosis 
industry possess domain knowledge like domain experts, enabling them 
to diagnose faults and anomalies based on users’ prompts and analyze 
data like domain experts [33]. For example, Lannger et al. [34] utilized 
the sequential processing capabilities of LLMs to handle time-series data 
from HVAC systems. And the models were further fine-tuned using many 
labeled input data to achieve binary or multiclass fault classification. 
Zhang et al. [35] applied GPT to diagnose common faults in AHUs, 
chillers, and variable refrigerant flow air conditioners. They could 
provide interpretable texts explaining the reason behind their diagnosis 
results. To further enhance diagnosis accuracy, Zhang et al. [36] 
designed a self-correction strategy for LLMs that automatically generates 
an optimized training dataset based on labeled data to improve the 
LLM’s performance. Additionally, a data augmentation method was 
proposed to adapt and optimize the training dataset, enabling iterative 
development of high-performance LLMs. Moreover, Li et al. [28] diag-
nosed the fault of the virtual model of return temperature of chilled 
water in building digital twins based on 10 consecutive residuals greater 
than the threshold.
These approaches have propelled the progress and advancement of 
FDD. However, when applied to real-world building energy systems, 
several limitations need to be considered: 
(1) Existing FDD approaches relied on user prompt to provide 
building information and control logic, which increased the time 
and cost required for system deployment and development.
(2) System-specific knowledge or black-box models were employed 
to achieve FDD for the sensor or equipment. However, the control 
loop including the sensor, actuator, load, setpoint, and plant was 
neglected, which reduces the performance of operation and 
maintenance.
(3) Previous FDD methods focused on diagnosis effect using various 
evaluation indexes, decision-making processes behind responses 
were not interpretable.
Hence, this study develops an agentic fault detection and diagnosis 
(AFDD) framework that combines the ontology, generalized FDD rules, 
and the AI agent, achieving advancements in automated cross-scenario 
generalizability, result interpretability, and practical decision support 
capability. This work presents a novel solution for efficiently operating 
and maintaining building energy systems and promoting energy con-
servation and carbon reduction. Moreover, the effectiveness of the AFDD 
framework in the real building energy system has been further validated 
through the discussion of the running time and field experiments.
The main contributions of this study are as follows: 
(1) Ontology is developed by leveraging the Brick schema and its 
extensions for control logic to describe the knowledge of building 
energy systems and control loops to reduce the required time and 
cost for providing this information.
(2) Generalized FDD rules are proposed based on by incorporating 
operational and maintenance experience, design instructions, 
expert knowledge, and historical data to diagnose the sensor, 
actuator, load, setpoint, and plant in real building energy systems 
with high accuracy and applicability, which achieves manage-
ment of key equipment and points.

2. Agentic fault detection and diagnosis
2.1. Framework and composition
The agentic fault detection and diagnosis (AFDD) framework is 
developed by integrating the ontology, generalized diagnostic rules, and 
an AI agent to enable interpretable and automated diagnosis for building 
energy systems. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the AFDD framework adopts a 
multi-component collaborative architecture, comprising six key com-
ponents: expert, user, AI agent, ontology, database, and FDD tool.
Expert: Experts contribute domain knowledge and engineering 
experience to the AI agent through knowledge engineering to avoid 
hallucinations. Knowledge engineering is defined as the process of 
providing information and domain knowledge to support understanding 
of the context in which fault detection and diagnosis are performed and 
analyzed, ultimately optimizing prompt engineering for more accurate 
and intelligent system reasoning. The knowledge engineering includes 
the expert instructions and tool explanations [37]. The expert in-
structions can enrich the AI agent with system knowledge beyond its 
pre-trained data, such as role descriptions, result analysis, and virtual-
ization. It can be input to the AI agent using textual form. The tool ex-
planations are the description of the FDD tool, encompassing the 
purposes, rules, performance evaluation, etc. It is expressed through 
Python scripts and texts, which effectively help prevent AI agents from 
generating erroneous outputs.
User: Users initiate queries and requests based on operational needs 
and receive diagnosis results and services. They can also provide feed-
back, which is relayed to experts for further system refinement. This 
mechanism helps to reduce operational errors and supports more precise 
detection and diagnosis.
Ontology: The ontology provides a unified, structured, and semantic 
representation of building system metadata and control logic. It supplies 
system information to support the AI agent and FDD tool, enabling 
automated and interpretable diagnosis.
Database: The database contains data of building energy system, 
including historical data and real-time data. Historical data can realize 
the development of virtual model and lay the foundation for the reali-
zation of the FDD tool. During implementation, real-time data can be

2.2. Ontology
Serving as the backbone of building energy systems, the ontology 
enables unified, machine-interpretable modeling of equipment, points, 
locations, and control logic across multiple subsystems, such as heating, 
ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC), lighting, and beyond. 
Ontology is developed by leveraging the Brick schema and its extensions 
for control logic [38], as illustrated in Fig. 2.
At its core, the Brick schema defines three primary object classes: 
equipment, point, and location. These three classes are interconnected 
through standardized relationships, including "isPointOf", "isLocatedIn", 
and "feeds" [39,40]. For example, a point (such as a temperature sensor) 
can be linked to a specific equipment or location using "isPointOf". A 
clear semantic connection is established between the physical asset and 
its associated data point. Table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of

2.3. Generalized diagnosis rules
Generalized FDD rules are developed by incorporating operational 
and maintenance experience, system configuration, and historical data 
to diagnose the actuator, sensor, load, plant in other control loops, and 
setpoint. During diagnosis, it evaluates whether the control objective is 
satisfied by analyzing the difference between setpoints and measure-
ments. If a difference greater than the threshold is detected, a sequential, 
rule-based diagnostic process is initiated for each control loop compo-
nent including the sensor, actuator, load, plant in other control loops, 
and setpoint, as shown in Fig. 3. 
• Fault A: Sensor fault in the target control loop
When the difference between the actual measurement and the set-
point exceeds the threshold, the AFDD further evaluates the difference 
between the measurement and the virtual model output. If this 
difference exceeds the predefined threshold, the sensor fault is identi-
fied; otherwise, the sensor is considered normal. Note that virtual model 
output is obtained using data-driven methods, with inputs such as 
equipment power consumption, frequency, or control signals. They are 
assumed to be fault-free during operation. Additionally, if the sensor 
measurement can be guaranteed to be fault-free, it may also be used as 
an input. 
• Fault B: Actuator fault in the target control loop
If the sensor fault is excluded, the AFDD further analyzes changes in 
actuator output such as valve position, damper position, frequency. If 
the actual value between the maximum and minimum threshold and the 
actuator response is abnormal (e.g., response is not timely), an actuator 
fault is determined; otherwise, the actuator is considered normal. 
• Fault C: Load issue in the target control loop

Suppose neither sensors nor actuators exhibit fault, but the target 
plant’s load (e.g., terminal cooling or heating capacity) still fails to meet 
control requirements. In that case, the AFDD determines the load issue, 
which may be related to operation and management. 
• Fault D: Plant fault in other control loops
The AFDD further analyzes the plant output variable in other control 
loops. This output variable can be obtained using the correlation anal-
ysis algorithm. If any difference between the output variable and the 
setpoint exceeds its threshold, a potential fault is localized to variables 
or equipment; otherwise, the plant is considered normal. 
• Fault E: Setpoint unreasonable in the target control loop
If all entities within the target loop are identified as normal, the fault 
will be attributed to the unreasonable setpoint.
Generalized diagnosis rules integrate thresholds with ontology, 
enabling efficient and interpretable identification of multi-source and 
multi-level faults in complex building energy systems. The specific 
system configuration can flexibly extend and adapt them to ensure that 
the AFDD maintains high practical engineering scalability.
2.4. FDD tool
By synthesizing the expert knowledge, engineering experience, his-
torical measurement data, and the structured relationships embedded 
within the ontology, the FDD tool formulates multi-level logical 
reasoning for each control loop. It involves the code, ontology, and 
diagnosis rules. The code is developed to assist virtualization because it 
may be challenging to generate satisfactory virtualization results by 
relying only on knowledge engineering. During implementation, the 
FDD tool is executed based on the following steps. First, the ontology is 
loaded to identify and retrieve relevant equipment, points, and control 
loop entities within the target building energy system. Second, 
comprehensive queries (e.g., via SPARQL) are executed to obtain all 
objects and associated attributes linked to the control loop, leveraging 
the formal relationships. Third, the system imports operational datasets 
by the AI agent for the database and diagnosis thresholds based on 
system configuration, the quantitative basis for subsequent inferential

pump, chilled water pump, cooling water pump, cooling tower, supply

3.2. Ontology for the HVAC system
To enable structured and semantic management of the target HVAC 
system, this study adopts an ontology modeling approach that integrates 
both system operation’s physical and logical aspects. As illustrated in 
Fig. 5, the Brick schema encompasses a wide range of equipment (e.g., 
the heat pump, cooling tower, supply fan), points (e.g., temperature 
sensors, differential pressure sensors, valve positions), and locations (e. 
g., chilled water loop, cooling water loop). Meantime, the Brick schema 
leverages a set of formalized relationships, such as "isPointOf", "isLoca-
tedIn", "hasPoint", and "feeds", to semantically connect points with 
equipment and locations. Each equipment node is meticulously anno-
tated with its associated input and output points. For example, the 
chilled water pump is linked to the chilled water loop via "hasPart", and 
to its speed and power measurements via "hasPoint". This ontology- 
based modeling enables rapid information retrieval, intuitive visuali-
zation, and seamless knowledge transfer to the AI agent to enhance the 
generalizability and scalability of the AFDD.
Building upon the Brick schema, the ontology is extended with 
explicit representations of control logic to accommodate advanced 
diagnostic requirements. As depicted in Fig. 6, five control loop ontol-
ogies are constructed, each structured around the five essential control 
entities, including setpoint, controller, actuator, plant, and sensor, 
which are interconnected by standardized relationships such as "isIn-
putOf", "adjusts", etc. For example, entities such as the supply air tem-
perature setpoint, controller (PID), actuator (temperature control 
valve), plant (air handling unit), and sensor are instantiated and con-
nected in the supply air temperature control loop. Each of these entities 
is mapped to the corresponding physical equipment and real-time 
operational data point. It preserves the control loop’s functional struc-
ture and its integration with the HVAC system. This control ontology can 
be readily extensible to other loops, such as indoor temperature, static 
pressure, and various differential pressure loops to support consistent 
and scalable modeling across diverse operational scenarios.
3.3. Generalized FDD rules for the HVAC system
Upon completion of the ontology modeling, the generalized FDD 
rules are developed. In the operation and maintenance of the target 
HVAC system, the complex system structure, changeable working con-
ditions, and significant data noise often limit the interpretability of 
purely model-based or data-driven FDD approaches. Rule-based FDD 
approaches, grounded in operation and maintenance experience, design 
instruction, expert knowledge, assisted codes, and historical data, create 
inferable diagnosis rules. This approach offers high interpretability and 
strong generalizability. And it renders suitable for the HVAC system 
characterized by multiple sensors, equipment, and control loops. The 
rules involve two key aspects: (1) translating engineering experience 
and expert knowledge into multi-level, executable logical rules. For 
example, if the absolute difference between the measurement and set-
point is less than the threshold, the supply air temperature setpoint is 
reasonable; otherwise, the problem may be from other entities, and (2) 
determining thresholds that reflect normal system operation based on 
relevant references, system and control configurations, and operational 
experience.
As illustrated in Fig. 7, this study defines the diagnosis rule using the 
supply air temperature control loop as an example, comprising the 
following specific steps:
First, the AFDD judges whether the difference between the supply air 
temperature measurement and its setpoint falls within a ± 1 ◦C 
threshold [42]. If the difference is within this range, the control loop is 
deemed to be operating normally.
Second, if the difference exceeds the threshold, the underlying causes 
are further investigated, encompassing the supply air temperature 
sensor, temperature control valve, cooling capacity, supply chilled water 
temperature, and supply air temperature setpoint. 
Fault A: Supply Air Temperature Sensor Fault
This study generates the virtual model output using a Multilayer 
Perceptron (MLP) algorithm using data from June 1 to June 30, 2022, 
and the ratio of training and testing data is 80 % and 20 %. The 
hyperparameters is configurated with batch size 32, epochs 20, and 
hidden layers 2 (128–64). The loss function is the mean squared error, 
and optimizer is Adam. The RMSE is 0.32 ◦C for model accuracy. The 
return chilled water temperature and mixed air temperature are the 
input variables, as shown in Eq. (1). Throughout the diagnosis, it is 
assumed that these two sensors are fault-free to ensure the reliability of 
the virtual model output.
The AFDD determines the sensor fault by comparing the actual 
measurement and the virtual model output. If the difference is less than 
the ±1 ◦C threshold from the modeling result and experience, the sensor 
is considered normal; otherwise, the sensor is faulty.

3.4. Implementation of the AFDD for the HVAC system
The AI agent integrates with the developed ontology and FDD rules 
to achieve the AFDD in the HVAC system. The AFDD uses a multi- 
component collaborative architecture as shown in Fig. 8. Centered on 
the AI agent, the architecture integrates expert knowledge engineering, 
user prompts, the FDD tool, database, and ontology to realize full- 
process intelligent and automatic diagnosis. GPT-4o powers the AI 
agent during implementation. LangChain will be employed to link Py-
thon with the GPT-4o [43]. LangChain is a software development kit 
that facilitates the creation of applications using the GPT-4o, offering 
various possibilities for application development. The specific steps are 
as follows:
First, experts give knowledge engineering including expert in-
structions and tool explanations to develop the domain-specific AI 
agent, as shown in Table 3. Expert instructions provide an overview for 
the entire diagnostic process. It enables the AI agent to determine which 
tools to invoke and what results to generate. Otherwise, tool in-
terpretations require the AI agent to produce the codes and interpretable 
diagnostic reports based on datasets and generalized FDD rules, thereby 
helping users better understand the system’s operational status. These 
knowledge engineering is structured and injected, enhancing GPT-4o’s 
understanding and reasoning capabilities for the target HVAC system 
and reducing hallucinations.
Second, users can give the AI agent information queries and diag-
nosis requests according to their needs, involving the information 
extraction, ontology extension, and fault diagnosis. Various cases are 
designed as follow:

4.5. Discussions for the application
To implement the AFDD in practical the target HVAC system, it is 
essential to discuss the running time and field experiment. The running 
time can reflect the efficiency of the AFDD. Real-time can be achieved 
for the entire control loop only when the running time is shorter than the 
data acquisition interval. Additionality, the field experiment can be 
conducted using real-time data to validate the effectiveness of the AFDD 
in response to user queries and its integration with the building man-
agement system. The following provides a detailed discussion.
4.5.1. Running time
The running time of the AFDD under varying amounts of diagnostic 
data is illustrated in Fig. 14. The results indicates that as the data volume 
increases, the running time also rises significantly. Notably, when 
diagnosing a full day’s data (660 data), the system requires 473.3 s, 
which may adversely impact operational efficiency. However, consid-
ering that the diagnosis time per data point is 43.87 s and the data 
sampling interval is one-minute, real-time diagnosis will be possible. In 
summary, the target HVAC system can transmit real-time data to the 
AFDD. This enables diagnosis of the sensor, actuator, load, setpoint, and 
plant based on user prompts.
4.5.2. Field experiment
Fig. 15 illustrates the field experiment of the AFDD at a specific time 
point during August 2025. In this scenario, the user provides diagnostic 
queries to the AFDD based on the developed ontology, diagnosis rules, 
and data to execute diagnosis across the supply air temperature, tem-
perature control valve, cooling capacity, supply air temperature 
setpoint, and supply chilled water temperature. The AFDD is run using 
Python scripts integration with the AI agent via LangChain. During the 
diagnostic process, users operate the AFDD by providing their personal 
OpenAI API key. This ensures that all information is stored securely 
within the user’s own account, thereby safeguarding data privacy, and 
preventing information leakage. In the building management system 
interface, each related variable is paired with an indicator light: green in 
normal, red in fault states. This visual design allows users to identify 
fault points clearly without further analysis. For example, when the 
difference between the measurement and setpoint is 0.8 ◦C below the 
threshold, the supply air temperature control loop is determined to be 
normal, and all indicator lights display green. The building energy sys-
tem achieves efficient O&M using the AFDD.
5. Conclusions
In this study, the AFDD that integrates the AI agent, ontology, and 
generalized FDD rules is developed and validated in a real-world HVAC 
application. The proposed framework enables automated, scalable, and 
interpretable fault diagnosis in complex building environment. Case 
studies demonstrates that the system can extract information and extend 
the control loop for the ontology, accurately identify, and trace the 
sources of faults under various operational scenarios and generate 
diagnosis reports and suggestions for operation and maintenance. These 
reduce the workload and expertise required of facility managers. The 
following conclusions are drawn: 
(1) By developing the ontology, the AFDD can extract the building 
information and control logic. Furthermore, when the user wants 
to increase new control loop, it can be automatically extended in 
a standardized format to reduce development and deployment 
time and costs.
(2) By integrating the generalized diagnosis rules, the AFDD can di-
agnose all faults for the sensor, actuator, load, setpoint, and plant 
in other control loops without misjudgment. Additionally, the 
real-time diagnosis is achieved by interacting with the actual 
building energy system through API key and user’s prompt.
(3) By using the AI agent, the AFDD can automatically generates 
intuitive and easily understandable interpretable report from 
diagnosis to decision-making. This reduces the requirement for 
specialized domain knowledge among operation and mainte-
nance personnel and enhances the reliability of fault diagnosis 
results.
In the future, automatic generation for ontologies and diagnosis rules 
should be developed using the AI agent. Building energy systems have 
become increasingly complex and diverse in practical operation and 
maintenance. Ontologies and diagnosis rules will contain many equip-
ment and points, and manual development will reduce the automation 
level of building management. The autonomous development by the AI 
agent based on architectural drawings and design specifications con-
stitutes a significant avenue for future research. Otherwise, the AFDD 
framework combines with services in building digital twins should be 
considered. As a component of digital twins, services can integrate 
multi-source databases to enable prediction, diagnosis, and calibration 
of physical entities and virtual models. A promising research direction 
lies in combining the developed AFDD with digital twins to facilitate the 
diagnosis of virtual models, virtual sensors, and control logic.
