ABSTRACT
As residential energy systems become increasingly electrified and coordination-intensive, home energy man-
agement systems (HEMS) must operate economically while remaining aligned with evolving user-authorized 
comfort and service boundaries. Existing personalization approaches rely on questionnaire initialization, fixed 
preference parameters, or indirect behavioral corrections, limiting alignment under changing needs and the use 
of runtime natural-language feedback for long-horizon calibration. This paper proposes a large language model 
(LLM)-enabled human-in-the-loop framework that converts user utterances into authorization-aware, contract- 
constrained, machine-checkable events for downstream control and preference updating. The LLM is a contract- 
constrained semantic interface, not a direct controller. Explicit intents support bounded execution and direct 
profile correction, whereas vague feedback requires option negotiation and user confirmation before bounded 
updating. A Contract-Informed Preference Update (CIPU) mechanism combines structured interaction evidence 
with projection and trust-region stabilization, while model predictive control remains the safety-constrained 
execution layer. In simulations, the proposed method reduces normalized preference distance by 22.50% and 
complaints by 74.46% relative to Survey MPC across three role-conditioned profiles. On a controlled 188-utter-
ance language-interface benchmark, the proposed interface achieves 100% structured-event exact match and a 
0% parser-level false direct-execution rate. Closed-loop ablations show 59.40% fewer complaints than the 
explicit-only variant, while event-log auditing of the no-contract-gate variant yields a 36.90% event-interface 
mismatch rate after gate removal. Under mid-horizon preference drift, final preference mismatch is reduced 
by 44.09% on average and up to 71.43% relative to Survey MPC.

1. Introduction
1.1. Background and motivation
As end-use electrification deepens, the operational complexity of the 
energy transition is increasingly shifting from the traditional supply side 
to end-use energy systems. The IEA reports in Electricity 2026 that 
global electricity demand grew by about 3% year-on-year in 2025 and is 
projected to increase by around 3.6% per year on average during 
2026–2030 [1]. In parallel, Energy Efficiency 2025 reports that build-
ings account for roughly 30% of global energy demand, with residential 
buildings contributing about 70% of building energy use [2]. Under this 
trend, homes are evolving into small multi-device energy systems in 
which critical loads such as HVAC and electric vehicle (EV) charging 
must be continuously coordinated under time-varying prices, weather 
conditions, and user routines. U.S. EIA residential end-use statistics 
further show that space cooling and space heating remain among the 
major household electricity end uses [3]. Accordingly, home energy 
management systems (HEMS) can no longer be viewed as static sched-
uling tools. Their practical effectiveness increasingly depends on 
whether automated control decisions can remain aligned with residents' 
evolving comfort, service, and cost-related requirements during sus-
tained operation.
A fundamental challenge is that the controllability of critical residential 
loads is not determined by device physics alone. It is more appropriately 
understood as user-conditioned operational flexibility, jointly shaped by 
comfort tolerance, temporary tasks, behavioral preferences, and willing-
ness to accept control actions. Consequently, system-level coordination 
demand does not automatically translate into callable household control 
authority. Stable coordinability can be preserved only if HEMS can 
continuously perceive, confirm, and maintain user-authorized controllable 
boundaries over time. This makes sustained user alignment not an auxiliary 
feature, but a core requirement for practical residential energy coordina-
tion. The urgency of this requirement is reinforced by the fact that, ac-
cording to the IEA's Global Energy Review 2025, electricity use in buildings 
increased by more than 600 TWh in 2024, accounting for nearly 60% of the 
global electricity-demand increment that year, with air-conditioning 
growth as an important driver [2].
In real home settings, however, such boundaries are often context- 
dependent, evolving, and difficult to express through fixed parameters 
alone. Users may need to communicate immediate requests, nuanced 
comfort corrections, or gradual shifts in operating preference, and these 
signals are naturally expressed in language rather than in predefined nu-
merical forms. Prior work has demonstrated the feasibility of natural- 
language interfaces in related cyber-physical and IoT settings [4]. This 
makes natural language a practical and low-friction channel for conveying 
high-level user intent, but it also creates a critical interface mismatch: user 
utterances are semantically rich yet unstructured, whereas downstream 
control and optimization require consistent, machine-checkable inputs. 
Moreover, runtime language feedback often mixes different control se-
mantics, including directly executable instructions and longer-horizon 
preference evidence. Without a robust interface that can separate and 
stabilize these signals, short-term expressions may interfere with long-term 
preference calibration, weakening sustained alignment between control 
actions and user needs. A central question for modern HEMS is therefore 
how natural-language feedback can be transformed into stable, verifiable, 
and reusable evidence for both real-time execution and long-horizon 
preference updating.
1.2. Related work and motivation for this study
Existing HEMS personalization studies have mainly incorporated user 
needs in two ways. One line parameterizes user needs through comfort 
ranges, preference weights, or control-related settings and then embeds 
these quantities into scheduling or optimization models. For example, Liu 
et al. developed a data-driven, uncertainty-aware preference modeling 
pipeline and incorporated the identified preference levels into multi- 
objective HEMS scheduling [5], while Ren et al. combined comfort eval-
uation with reinforcement learning-based decision making to address 
control under uncertain household parameters [6]. Another line adapts 
preferences during operation using indirect feedback signals, such as 
thermostat overrides or online behavioral traces. Elehwany et al. learned 
preferences from thermostat override behavior [7], and Sha et al. contin-
uously updated control-relevant models through online learning [8]. 
Taken together, these studies have moved HEMS from one-shot setup to-
ward dynamic adaptation. However, by representing user needs primarily 
as preset parameters or by inferring them indirectly from sparse behavioral 
evidence, they remain limited by a relatively low-bandwidth feedback 
loop. As a result, they are not well suited to absorb semantically rich run-
time language feedback as explicit, reusable evidence for sustained closed- 
loop calibration.
To make such semantically rich runtime feedback usable by HEMS, 
the system must first identify the user's intent and determine whether 
the utterance should trigger control-related processing. Wu et al. 
developed SCADA-NLI, which combines hierarchical intent classifica-
tion with semantic parsing to translate natural-language queries and 
control instructions into structured intermediate commands, achieving 
96.5% intent-recognition accuracy and 88.47% instruction-parsing ac-
curacy [9]. Larson et al. constructed an evaluation dataset covering 150 
intents across 10 domains and showed that benchmark classifiers per-
formed well on supported intents but struggled to identify out-of-scope 
queries [10]. These studies show that conventional intent recognition 
and scripted parsing pipelines can effectively map language into 
machine-readable representations when task classes and semantic 
boundaries are specified in advance. In sustained HEMS operation, 
however, the same temperature or SOC value may denote a requested 
target, a current measurement, a historical record, or a candidate option, 
while some utterances express only an adjustment direction and others 
contain no current control request. Covering these cases with predefined 
rules or fixed intent labels requires the relevant expressions and se-
mantic roles to be specified beforehand, limiting their ability to process 
open-form runtime feedback.
To interpret such open-form expressions, large language models 
(LLMs) have opened new possibilities for natural-language interaction in 
building and energy systems. In building operation and maintenance, Li 
et al. mapped natural-language queries to database access and question- 
answering responses using LLM-based methods [11], while Chen et al. 
developed a domain-adapted large-model framework for building en-
ergy system O&M with integrated domain knowledge [12]. At a broader 
methodological level, LLMs have also been explored for building load 
forecasting [13] and for more general building energy management 
tasks [14]. Human-in-the-loop mechanisms have likewise been explored 
through user confirmation, overrides, and feedback correction in 
building- and control-related settings. However, while LLMs are effec-
tive for query assistance, advisory support, and local interaction, a 
fundamental tension remains between the non-deterministic, open- 
ended nature of language generation and the strict feasibility re-
quirements of constraint-based control. This tension becomes especially 
important when moving from read-oriented assistance to closed-loop 
control participation. In such settings, unconstrained language outputs 
cannot be directly treated as executable control signals. What is still 
missing is a constrained intermediate representation that can transform 
mixed-semantic runtime feedback into stable, verifiable, and reusable 
evidence for both real-time execution and long-horizon preference 
calibration. This unresolved interface problem directly motivates the 
contract-constrained framework developed in this paper.

(1) First, a stable mechanism for long-horizon preference cali-
bration during sustained HEMS operation is still missing. 
Existing HEMS personalization methods have progressed in both 
preset parameter modeling and in-operation adaptation, yet user 
needs are still commonly treated as initialization priors or updated 
only indirectly through sparse behavioral traces. For critical resi-
dential loads, however, coordinability is jointly conditioned by 
comfort tolerance, temporary tasks, and willingness to accept 
control actions. User-authorized controllable boundaries there-
fore evolve during operation rather than remaining fixed at system 
startup. Although parts of this dynamic have been recognized, 
current approaches still lack an online mechanism that can 
continuously maintain and recalibrate these boundaries over time.
(2) Second, runtime natural-language feedback has not yet 
formed a stable intermediate representation for closed-loop 
control. Large language models have made natural-language 
interaction increasingly accessible in building and energy appli-
cations, but most existing studies still use language primarily for 
query answering, suggestion generation, or local task support. 
From the perspective of sustained HEMS operation, semantically 
rich runtime feedback must first be transformed into a consistent, 
verifiable, and reusable intermediate form before it can serve as 
valid control and updating evidence. Such a stable interface layer 
is still largely missing.
(3) Third, mixed-semantic feedback still lacks a unified but 
differentiated closed-loop treatment. In practical home set-
tings, runtime feedback often combines directly executable re-
quests with longer-horizon preference evidence and expressions 
that should not trigger a new control action. These signals carry 
different meanings for control execution and profile updating, yet 
existing approaches still do not separate them clearly enough. As 
a result, short-term supervisory expressions may interfere with 
long-term preference correction, while longer-horizon preference 
signals may be reduced to local command handling. This se-
mantic conflation weakens long-run alignment with evolving 
user requirements and increases interference between real-time 
execution and subsequent preference calibration.
1.4. Contribution
To address these gaps, this paper proposes a human-in-the-loop 
closed-loop framework for sustained HEMS operation that enables 
runtime natural-language feedback to enter control and preference 
updating through a stable and machine-checkable interface. Rather than 
using the large language model as a generic assistant, we position it as a 
contract-constrained language interface between user expressions and 
downstream control. The methodological focus lies in the controlled 
transformation of language feedback into reusable interaction evidence 
and online preference calibration, while MPC remains the constrained 
execution layer. The main contributions are as follows. 
(1) First, we establish a contract-constrained language interface 
for closed-loop HEMS. This interface transforms user utterances 
into structured, machine-checkable interaction events that 
distinguish directly executable requests, negotiation-required 
preferences, and expressions that should trigger no new action, 
thereby bridging the mismatch between semantically rich lan-
guage input and constraint-based downstream control. As a 
result, runtime language feedback no longer remains at the level 
of transient dialogue, but can be incorporated into the HEMS loop 
through a stable intermediate representation.
(2) Second, we develop a differentiated human-in-the-loop 
handling mechanism for mixed-semantic feedback. Explicit 
requests and vague preference feedback are allowed to enter the 
same closed loop through distinct pathways: directly executable 
signals support bounded execution and stronger profile 
correction, whereas less specific signals are processed through 
interpretable negotiation with user confirmation before bounded 
updating. Expressions that contain no current control request 
remain outside these active pathways. This design separates 
short-term execution needs from long-horizon calibration re-
quirements and reduces their mutual interference.
(3) Third, we propose the Contract-Informed Preference Update 
(CIPU) mechanism for online long-horizon preference cali-
bration under preference drift. By combining structured 
interaction evidence with contextual information, bounded pro-
jection, and trust-region stabilization, CIPU continuously updates 
the preference profile while limiting the effect of transient intent 
expressions. This CIPU-based updating design, together with its 
stability-oriented analytical support, enables more stable prefer-
ence tracking and better long-run alignment between user needs 
and control outcomes than one-shot preference setting or ap-
proaches relying only on sparse behavioral evidence.
1.5. Paper organization
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 pre-
sents the HEMS modeling framework and MPC formulation. Section 3 
introduces the contract-constrained language interface, differentiated 
handling of mixed-semantic feedback, and the CIPU-based online pref-
erence calibration method. Section 4 presents the benchmark, language- 
interface and mechanism ablation, and drift-tracking studies and dis-
cusses the corresponding results. Section 5 concludes the paper.
2. Control-oriented HEMS modeling and MPC formulation
This section starts from the overall control problem of the home 
energy management system and establishes a unified modeling foun-
dation for the subsequent analysis. In this study, the HEMS is formulated 
as a closed-loop decision-making system that links environmental 
sensing with device-level actuation, as shown in Fig. 1. It continuously 
integrates indoor and outdoor states, weather conditions, electricity 
prices, and user requirements, and coordinates key residential loads 
under the joint constraints of device capabilities, operational limits, and 
service objectives [15]. From this overall perspective, HVAC and EV 
charging are selected as the main modeling targets, since both exhibit 
significant energy impacts, strong temporal coupling, and service attri-
butes closely related to user experience, thereby capturing the core 
characteristics of multi-device coordination at the household level. On 
this basis, this section develops unified models of building thermal dy-
namics, HVAC execution mechanisms, and EV charging dynamics, and 
formulates the HEMS optimization and control problem within an MPC 
framework.
2.1. Building thermal state evolution model
This study adopts a 3R2C resistance–capacitance (RC) model to 
describe building thermal state evolution. A lumped-parameter RC 
network is used to represent heat transfer between indoor air and the 
outdoor environment, between indoor air and the building thermal 
mass, and between the thermal mass and the outdoor environment.
Let Ta denote indoor air temperature and Tm denote the aggregated 
building thermal mass temperature. The 3R2C model consists of two 
coupled energy-balance equations: the air node exchanges heat with the 
outdoor environment and the thermal mass, while the mass node ex-
changes heat with both the air node and the outdoor environment. The 
net HVAC thermal input Qhvac is applied to the air node. Solar radiation 
gains and internal heat gains are treated as external disturbances acting 
on either the air node or the mass node.

2.3. Electric vehicle charging dynamics model
The electric vehicle (EV) subsystem is modeled as a controlled dy-
namic process jointly determined by the state of charge and connection 
availability, and only home charging is considered in this study. Let sk∈
[0, 1] denote the state of charge (SOC) at time step k and let ck∈{0, 1}
indicate whether the EV is physically connected to the home charger. In 
this study, ck is exogenously determined by a given daily travel 
schedule: ck = 0 when the vehicle is away from home, and ck = 1 after it 
returns and is plugged in.
When the vehicle is connected, a binary charging action uevk is used to 
indicate whether charging is activated, with charging proceeding at a 
fixed effective rate. The SOC dynamics can be written as

Here Ecap denotes the battery capacity, Pch denotes the nominal

ensuring that SOC does not change due to charging when the EV is away 
from home. This dynamic model provides the basis for EV service con-
straints and charging scheduling in the subsequent HEMS control 
formulation.

2.5. MPC solution and device-level execution
To solve the finite-horizon constrained control problem established 
in Section 2.4, this paper adopts model predictive control (MPC) as the 
closed-loop execution mechanism [16]. At each decision instant, MPC 
solves the optimization problem using the latest state measurements and 
updated forecasts, implements only the first control action, and then 
repeats the same procedure at the next instant based on newly observed 
states and refreshed predictions. This receding-horizon mechanism en-
ables the controller to continuously update decisions under the com-
bined effects of multi-device constraints, time-varying electricity prices, 
and weather disturbances, and is therefore well suited for coordinated 
closed-loop control in HEMS.

3. Contract-constrained human-in-the-loop method
Section 2 has established the building and device models, opera-
tional constraints, and the MPC-based closed-loop execution foundation 
of the HEMS, thereby defining the feasible control space for subsequent 
decision-making. Building on this foundation, this section presents a 
contract-constrained human-in-the-loop method for incorporating run-

Within this method, the language layer is responsible for constrained 
interaction parsing and event generation, and it connects to the subse-
quent control and update modules through a structured interface [18]. 
Given a user utterance yk and its contextual information, the language

and then forms the structured interaction event ek through predefined 
contract mapping [19]. The system then checks the completeness and 
validity of the event and routes it into different handling pathways ac-
cording to the intent type. For explicit intents, the system gives priority 
to the user's short-term request under operational constraints and

3.1.2. Intent types and domains
To reflect realistic human–system interaction, each user utterance yk 
is first classified into three intent types: typek∈{explicit, vague, none}. 
An explicit intent corresponds to a clear request that can be directly 
grounded in the control layer. A vague intent conveys a preference di-
rection, adjustment tendency, or service need, but does not yet specify a 
unique executable target. A no-intent utterance contains no current 
control intent and therefore provides no actionable evidence for 
execution or preference updating. Correspondingly, domaink is used to 
identify the scope addressed by the utterance. For explicit intents, 
domaink∈{HVAC, EV}, indicating direct control requests for the HVAC 
or EV subsystem. For vague intents, domaink = ∅ denotes a global 
cost–comfort trade-off preference, whereas domaink = EV denotes EV- 
related service urgency or charging tendency that has not yet been 
expressed as a numerical target. For no-intent utterances, domaink = ∅. 
This design is consistent with the HVAC and EV subsystems considered 
in this study and preserves a unified interface for future extension to 
additional devices.
Explicit intents are used to express user-specified device-level tar-
gets. In the HVAC domain, such intents typically appear as direct 
temperature-setting requests. In the EV domain, they usually appear as 
explicit charging targets, which are represented in this study as target 
SOC values and then passed to subsequent execution and updating 
procedures. Vague intents carry a broader range of preference infor-
mation and mainly cover three cases. The first is a global cost–comfort 
trade-off preference, such as a request to save more electricity cost or to 
place more emphasis on comfort. The second is a comfort-adjustment 
tendency, such as a desire for a cooler or warmer environment 
without a specified magnitude. The third is EV-related service urgency 
or charging tendency, such as a request for faster or slower charging 
without a direct numerical target. All vague intents are first routed to the 
negotiation path. When directional information is underspecified, as in 
“I feel uncomfortable,” the system first resolves the direction and then 
determines the adjustment magnitude. No-intent utterances include 
greetings, chit-chat, unrelated questions, measured-state reports, his-
torical descriptions, and other energy-related statements without a 
current control intent. These utterances do not trigger preference 
updating or new execution overrides.
For example, “Set the indoor temperature to 24◦C" is explicit because 
it specifies one directly executable numerical target. “Please make the 
room a little cooler” is vague because it expresses a current adjustment 
direction without a unique target. “The display currently shows 24◦C" is 
none because the numerical value describes a measured state rather than 
a requested target.
The above intent types jointly determine the subsequent routing and 
update behavior. A no-intent utterance keeps the current preference 
profile pk unchanged, and the control layer continues to execute the 
baseline MPC policy. An explicit intent produces a directly executable 
target and enters the immediate execution path. A vague intent first goes 
through negotiation and confirmation, and then influences pk in a 
bounded manner. In this way, runtime language feedback is consistently 
separated into direct execution signals, negotiable preference evidence, 
and non-actionable expressions, which provides a clear semantic basis 
for the contract-constrained structured event defined in the next 
subsection.

These candidate plans are presented to the user in a concise and 
contrastive form together with contextual signals such as the current 
electricity price snapshot. After the user confirms ζk, the preference 
update module applies a bounded update to pk based on (ωk, ζk, xk) with 
direction and magnitude determined by the confirmed level. The control 
layer then executes the MPC solution under the updated profile, so that 
the negotiated choice is reflected in subsequent optimization and actu-
ation. For an underspecified comfort request, the system may first use 
ωk = comfortDir to determine the direction (‘cooler’ or ‘warmer’), and 
then use ωk ∈{coolLevel, warmLevel} to determine the magnitude. For 
vague EV urgency feedback, the system uses ωk = evLevel to negotiate 
the service acceleration level and maps the confirmed result to a 
bounded correction of the EV target entry in pk.

3.2. Contract-informed preference update (CIPU)
Building on the contract-constrained event interface established in 
Section 3.1, this study further proposes the Contract-Informed Prefer-
ence Update (CIPU) mechanism to convert contract-compliant interac-
tion events into online preference-profile updates. CIPU operates on the 
learnable preference state pk defined in Eq. (17), and combines it with 
the structured event ek and the runtime context xk to generate the 
updated preference profile pk+1. Thus, the input of CIPU consists of the 
current online preference profile, a contract-compliant interaction 
event, and the runtime context, while its output is the updated prefer-
ence profile. CIPU does not update the physical model, MPC solver, fixed 
control-layer penalty coefficients, device constraints, or safety settings. 
Through intent routing, bounded branch-specific updating, projection 
onto an admissible parameter domain, and trust-region stabilization, 
explicit feedback and confirmed vague feedback enter the same 
preference-update process through different update pathways. This 
design enables the system to continuously absorb interaction evidence 
during long-term operation and provides the algorithmic basis for sub-
sequent preference calibration and preference-drift re-tracking.

4. Experiments and discussion
This section evaluates the proposed human-in-the-loop preference 
calibration framework through controlled experiments. The experi-
ments address three questions. First, under the same physical model, 
operational constraints, and solver settings, can online interactive cali-
bration further improve user alignment compared with fixed preference 
initialization? Second, how do the major components of the language 
interface contribute to structured-semantic recovery and final authori-
zation, and what roles do intent-specific routing and contract-event 
gating play in the closed-loop process? Third, when the role- 
conditioned reference preference target changes during operation, can 
the proposed method continue to use new natural-language feedback to 
correct the online preference profile and reduce the mismatch to the 
updated reference target?
To answer these questions, three groups of experiments are con-
ducted. Study 1 provides a benchmark comparison across three user 
profiles and four control methods. Default MPC denotes a baseline 
without user-specific calibration, using only default preference param-
eters. Survey MPC denotes a static personalization method that obtains 
an initial preference profile from a pre-operation questionnaire and 
keeps it fixed during operation. GT-parameter MPC denotes an ideal 
reference in which the controller directly uses the predefined reference 
preference profile parameters. The proposed method starts from 
questionnaire-based initialization and continuously updates the prefer-
ence profile through runtime natural-language feedback and CIPU. This 
comparison evaluates the overall advantage of online preference cali-
bration over non-personalized control and one-shot personalized 
initialization. Study 2 conducts mechanism ablations and language- 
interface evaluation. Study 2 A compares Explicit-only, Vague-only, 
and the full proposed method to analyze the complementary roles of 
explicit feedback and vague feedback in preference calibration. Study 2B 
removes the contract-based event gates to examine the effect of a 
structured and verifiable interface on closed-loop stability, with 
interaction-event logs further used to quantify interface mismatch after 
gate removal. Study 2C conducts a component-level comparison on a 
shared frozen benchmark to examine the contributions of LLM-based 
semantic parsing, the current-control-intent semantic rule in the 
prompt, structured-schema consistency, and deterministic final autho-
rization. Study 3 introduces mid-horizon preference drift and examines 
whether the system can keep absorbing interaction evidence and re- 
track the user preference after drift shocks, including shifts from a 
balanced preference toward cost-sensitive, comfort-sensitive, and multi- 
stage preference patterns.
All closed-loop experiments are conducted under a unified simula-
tion environment and MPC execution framework. All compared closed- 
loop methods share the same physical model, external data, operational 
constraints, safety bounds, and solver settings. Their differences mainly 
lie in preference initialization, interaction pathways, and preference- 
update mechanisms. The building and device parameters, weather and 
electricity-price data sources, user-profile settings, control parameters, 
and experiment procedure are described in Section 4.1. The evaluation 
metrics include electricity cost, energy consumption, normalized L1 
preference distance, complaint count, and comfort-range ratio. Elec-
tricity cost and energy consumption measure economic performance 
and energy use. The normalized L1 preference distance, defined in Eq. 
(41), quantifies the mismatch between the online preference profile and

4.1. Experimental setup and evaluation metrics
All closed-loop experiments use the 3R2C building thermal model, 
HVAC execution model, EV charging dynamics model, and MPC 
execution framework established in Section 2. The compared closed- 
loop methods share the same physical model, external inputs, opera-
tional constraints, safety bounds, prediction horizon, and solver settings. 
Their differences mainly come from preference initialization, interaction 
pathways, and online preference-updating mechanisms.
The building thermal parameters are configured according to 
German residential building typology standards [23]. The 3R2C model 
includes the air-node thermal capacitance, the building thermal-mass 
capacitance, and the heat-transfer coefficients between indoor air and 
outdoors, between indoor air and thermal mass, and between thermal 
mass and outdoors. These parameters describe the heat exchange among 
indoor air, building thermal mass, and the outdoor environment. HVAC 
parameters include the maximum heating thermal power, maximum 
cooling thermal power, and the coefficients of performance in heating 
and cooling modes [24]. EV parameters include battery capacity, 
nominal charging power, and charging efficiency [25]. The EV-related 
settings follow representative German household charging equipment 
and low-voltage grid-connection requirements [26]. Table 2 summarizes 
the building, HVAC, EV, controller-safety, and objective-bound settings.
The controller safety settings are consistent with the soft-constrained 
MPC formulation in Section 2.5. Indoor temperature is subject to hard 
safety bounds, while slack variables are used to handle a small number 
of potentially infeasible cases, with a fixed slack penalty coefficient 
limiting safety-bound violations. This setup follows common feasibility- 
protection practice in constrained MPC for building energy control [27]. 
On the objective side, the comfort-bandwidth and cost-weight bounds 
are fixed admissible intervals that cover the initial profiles mapped from 
the questionnaire in Appendix A. The temperature-deviation penalty, 
EV-service penalty, and smoothing penalty are fixed control-layer pa-
rameters in all experiments. CIPU updates only the learnable preference- 
profile entries defined in Eq. (17), namely the cost-related preference 
weight, comfort-center temperature, EV target SOC, and comfort 
bandwidth.
The external inputs include weather data and time-varying elec-
tricity prices. Weather data are taken from real German meteorological 
data for 2025 [28], and electricity prices are taken from German elec-
tricity market data for 2025 [29]. The raw data are processed through 
missing-value handling, outlier cleaning, timestamp alignment, and 1 h 
resampling before being passed to the simulator. The control interval 
and simulation step are both set to 1 h, and both the MPC prediction 
horizon and control horizon are set to 24 h. Each closed-loop experiment 
window contains 720 time steps, corresponding to 30 days of operation. 
January, April, and July are selected as representative winter, 
transition-season, and summer scenarios.
The user-side experiment follows a controlled role-play protocol. 
Three volunteers respectively play a balanced user, a temperature- 
sensitive user, and a cost-sensitive user. Motivated by user- 
segmentation [30] and preference-modeling studies in residential en-
ergy management, these role-conditioned user types are designed to 
cover typical preference orientations, including balanced cost–comfort 
trade-off, stronger thermal-comfort priority, and stronger cost-saving 
tendency. Each user type is associated with a predefined reference 
preference profile, which represents the preference target of that role in 
the experiment. Before the experiment starts, each user obtains an 
observable initial preference profile through a lightweight question-
naire. The questionnaire items, deterministic parameter mapping, pre-
defined reference profiles, and questionnaire-initialized profiles of the 
three user types are given in Appendix A. During the experiment, par-
ticipants provide natural-language feedback according to their assigned 
roles and the runtime information shown in the dialogue interface, while 
the numerical values of the reference profiles are not disclosed to them. 
Survey MPC uses the questionnaire-initialized profile and keeps it fixed 
during closed-loop operation. The proposed method starts from the same 
questionnaire-initialized profile and continuously updates the prefer-
ence profile through runtime structured interaction events and CIPU.
The language layer uses a locally deployed open-source Qwen3-14B 
model [31]. All LLM-based experiments use the same decoding config-
uration, with temperature set to 0.0, top_p set to 0.95, top_k set to 20, 
max_new_tokens set to 32,768, and thinking mode disabled by setting 
enable_thinking = False. These settings remain fixed across user profiles, 
seasonal scenarios, and LLM-based comparison methods to reduce 
generation variability and maintain consistent structured semantic 
parsing.
The language interface is constrained by a fixed System Prompt and 
User Prompt. The System Prompt specifies the task scope, intent-class 
boundaries, semantic-field definitions, numerical-target admission 
rules, and the prohibition against directly generating device-control 
commands. The User Prompt provides the current user utterance and 
relevant session context and requires the model to return the parsing 
result in the predefined format. The complete System Prompt and User 
Prompt are provided in Appendix C.
Model outputs follow a fixed JSON-formatted structure consistent 
with Section 3.1.3 and are checked for field exclusivity, conditional 
emptiness, data types, and value bounds. Records that violate the schema 
are rejected or conservatively downgraded. The language model only 
converts user utterances into structured semantic records; it does not 
directly generate device commands or bypass the contract gates and MPC 
execution layer. In the dialogue interface, users can observe indoor 
temperature, outdoor temperature, current electricity price, accumu-
lated electricity cost, accumulated energy consumption, and EV status, 
and then provide natural-language feedback based on these observations.

4.3.3. Study 2C: component-level language-interface evaluation
Study 2C conducts a component-level controlled comparison of the 
language interface on the same frozen 188-utterance benchmark and 
expert-reference records (see Table 6). All LLM-based configurations use 
the same Qwen3-14B base model, decoding configuration, and input 
utterances. Five configurations are evaluated: Rule-based, No-Current- 
Control-Intent Prompt Rule, No-Schema-Consistency, LLM-Based Final 
Authorization, and Proposed.
Rule-based replaces LLM-based semantic parsing with keyword 
matching, expression templates, and numerical matching rules while 
retaining the same structured-event representation and downstream 
authorization rules. No-Current-Control-Intent Prompt Rule retains the 
same LLM, the remaining prompt rules, structured-schema constraints, 
and deterministic authorization mechanism, while removing only the 
rule that determines whether the current speaker expresses a valid 
present or future control-related intent. No-Schema-Consistency retains 
the complete LLM-based semantic parsing process and prompt-level 
semantic rules while removing conditional emptiness, cross-field de-
pendencies, field exclusivity, and domain-target consistency re-
quirements. LLM-Based Final Authorization retains the complete 
upstream semantic parsing and structured semantic records of Proposed, 
but replaces deterministic final authorization with LLM-based selection 
among direct execution, negotiation, and no action. Proposed retains the 
complete prompt-level semantic rules, structured-schema consistency 
constraints, and deterministic final authorization mechanism.
Rule-based achieves 67.55% Intent Accuracy and EEM, 71.56% NTR, 
12.41% FDER, and 67.55% RA. Its errors are mainly associated with 
context-dependent distinctions between state information and requested 
control targets. For example, “The air-conditioning panel still shows 
24◦C; I am just mentioning it” reports the current state, whereas “Set the 
air conditioner to 24.0◦C" specifies an explicit control target. This result 
reflects the role of LLM-based semantic interpretation in distinguishing 
open-form expressions with similar surface information but different 
contextual meanings.
No-Current-Control-Intent Prompt Rule reduces Intent Accuracy, 
EEM, and RA to 87.23%, while NTR remains at 100.00% and FDER is 
1.38%. The errors mainly involve state reports, historical descriptions, 
resolved conditions, and third-party comments being interpreted as 
current control-related intents. For example, “The car still has 40% 
battery left; no need to do anything for now” contains an SOC value but 
does not express a current control request. This result reflects the role of 
the current-control-intent rule in defining the boundary between 
contextual information and currently valid control-related intent.
No-Schema-Consistency achieves 81.38% Intent Accuracy, 68.09% 
EEM, 68.81% NTR, 23.45% FDER, and 81.38% RA. Removing condi-
tional emptiness, field exclusivity, cross-field dependencies, and 
domain-target consistency allows structured records to contain field 
combinations that do not satisfy the predefined schema relationships. 
For example, “Charge a bit faster” expresses a charging-direction pref-
erence without specifying a unique SOC target, while “It is a little hot 
now; make it cooler” expresses a cooling direction without specifying a 
unique temperature target. This result reflects the role of schema con-
sistency in forming internally consistent structured semantic records.
LLM-Based Final Authorization retains 100.00% Intent Accuracy, 
EEM, and NTR and 0.00% FDER, while RA decreases to 84.04%. Because 
the upstream semantic parsing and structured records are unchanged 
from the Proposed configuration, the decrease occurs at the final 
authorization stage. For example, “It is a little hot now; make it cooler” 
and “Charge faster now” are correctly identified as vague requests 
without unique executable numerical targets and should enter negoti-
ation, but LLM-based final authorization may assign a different handling 
path. This result reflects the role of deterministic authorization in 
maintaining consistent final routing.
Proposed achieves 100.00% Intent Accuracy, EEM, NTR, and RA, 
with an FDER of 0.00%. The 100% result was obtained on the present 
controlled benchmark comprising utterances from three role-playing 
volunteers, and its generalizability should therefore be understood 
within the experimental conditions and range of user expressions rep-
resented in this benchmark. Under the complete configuration, the 
prompt-level semantic rules define the intent and field semantics, the 
schema-consistency constraints maintain valid relationships among the

4.4. Study 3: dynamic preference tracking on the balanced user profile
Study 3 examines the re-tracking capability of online preference 
calibration when the user's reference preference profile changes during 
operation (see Table 7). The experiment starts from the balanced user 
profile and considers three mid-horizon drift patterns: Balanced → Cost, 
Balanced → Comfort, and Balanced → Cost → Comfort. The latent 
reference preference profile in each stage is taken from the predefined 
user profiles in Appendix A. The Balanced stage uses the latent reference 
preference profile parameters of the balanced user, the Cost stage uses 
those of the cost-sensitive user, and the Comfort stage uses those of the 
temperature-sensitive user. In each pattern, the latent reference pref-
erence profile is switched at predefined time steps, so that the system 
experiences preference-target changes within the same closed-loop run. 
This study examines whether, after preference switching, the controller 
can use later natural-language feedback to correct the preference profile 
and reduce the mismatch between the online profile and the new 
reference preference profile.
Fig. 6 shows the trajectories of normalized L1 distance under the 
three preference-drift patterns. At each switching point, all methods are 
affected by the drift shock, and the preference distance increases. The 
recovery process after drift differs clearly across methods. Default MPC 
and Survey MPC keep using their original preference settings after the 
preference changes, so their distances remain at high levels after the 
drift. The proposed method also shows a short-term distance increase 
after drift, but then continues to update the profile using new interaction 
feedback and shows a clear downward recovery. In the representative 
winter trajectory, under Balanced → Cost, the final normalized L1 dis-
tance of the proposed method is about 0.089, lower than 0.346 for 
Survey MPC and 0.463 for Default MPC. Under Balanced → Comfort, the 
corresponding values are 0.179, 0.270, and 0.521. Under the more 
complex Balanced → Cost → Comfort pattern, the proposed method still 
limits the final distance to 0.278, while Survey MPC and Default MPC 
remain at 0.396 and 0.646, respectively. These trajectories show that 
online calibration can reduce preference mismatch after drift, demon-
strating the adaptability of a continuously updated profile.
Fig. 7 further shows changes in feedback semantics and system re-
sponses after preference drift. The phase-level complaint statistics 
indicate that reference-profile switching changes the semantic compo-
sition of user feedback. In the Balanced → Cost case, baseline-stage 
complaints are more comfort-related, while complaints quickly shift 
toward energy-saving requests after entering the cost-sensitive stage, 
with 23 cost-saving complaints and 5 comfort-related complaints. In 
Balanced → Comfort, complaints shift from 10 cooler complaints in the 
baseline phase to 32 warmer complaints and 2 explicit HVAC requests in 
the comfort-shift phase. In the cycle setting, the sequence becomes even 
clearer as cooler → cost-saving → warmer. The local zoom-ins further 
show that these complaints trigger actionable responses rather than 
passive logging. In a cost complaint case, the short-window mean hourly 
electricity cost drops from about 1.38 EUR/h before complaint to about 
0.24 EUR/h after complaint. In a comfort complaint case, the mean 
indoor temperature over the pre-complaint steps is about 23.40 ◦C, 
below the new comfort-band lower bound of 26.05 ◦C, while the next- 
step post-complaint temperature reaches 26.16 ◦C and re-enters the 
updated comfort range. Therefore, preference drift changes both how 
users complain and how the system responds.
These results show that dynamic personalization depends on 
whether the preference profile can keep being updated after deploy-
ment. Survey MPC provides a better initial preference estimate than 
default control, but when the user's reference preference profile 
changes, its fixed profile gradually becomes outdated and keeps the 
post-drift preference distance high. The proposed method continuously 
receives natural-language feedback through the contract-constrained 
interface and converts the new complaint semantics after drift into 
bounded preference updates, allowing the system to move from the old 
preference stage to the new one. The Balanced → Cost case shows that, 
when the user shifts from balanced to cost-sensitive behavior, the system 
can use energy-saving feedback to reduce economic mismatch. The 
Balanced → Comfort case shows that, when the user shifts toward 
stronger comfort needs, the system can adjust temperature preference 
through comfort-related feedback. The multi-stage drift case further 
shows that this mechanism can maintain re-tracking capability after 
multiple preference switches. Runtime natural-language feedback can 
correct initialization mismatch and can also provide new calibration 
evidence after user preference changes, allowing the online preference 
profile to move back toward the changed reference preference profile.
4.5. Discussion
The three studies indicate that the central challenge of personalized 
HEMS control is the continuous maintenance of user-authorized 
controllable boundaries during long-term operation. Questionnaire 
initialization provides an initial preference profile, but it mainly reflects 
the user's judgment before system operation. As weather, electricity 
prices, travel schedules, and immediate comfort perception change, the 
user's actual needs continue to emerge through runtime feedback. 
Natural-language feedback therefore provides new calibration evidence

This long-term calibration process requires a stable interface be-
tween language feedback and control. Natural language can express 
immediate requests, vague preferences, and context-dependent dissat-
isfaction, but these expressions must be converted into structured evi-
dence that downstream control modules can check and use. The 
contract-constrained intent interface contains distinct functions for 
contextual semantic recovery, prompt-level intent specification, 
structured-record consistency, and final authorization. Study 2C sepa-
rates these functions through component-level comparisons and shows 
that different interface components affect different stages of language 
processing and routing. Study 2B further shows that removing contract- 
event gating increases inconsistencies between parsed records and 
downstream preference-updating or control modules. Accordingly, the 
language interface converts user expressions into structured semantic 
evidence and determines the corresponding handling path, while the 
event gates control whether the resulting records can enter downstream 
preference updating and control execution.
On top of this stable interface, mixed-semantic feedback must pre-
serve different update meanings. Explicit intents usually contain con-
crete temperature or charging targets and are suitable for fast local 
correction. Vague intents express directional tendencies in cost, comfort, 
or service urgency, and are more suitable for long-term preference 
calibration after negotiation and confirmation. The intent-path ablation 
results show that a single path can only use part of the feedback infor-
mation, while the dual-path mechanism can absorb both concrete re-
quests and vague tendencies. CIPU further converts these two types of 
events into bounded profile updates, allowing short-term execution 
needs and long-term preference calibration to coexist in the same HEMS 
loop.
The dynamic preference study further shows the value of preference 
calibration during long-horizon operation. When the user shifts from a 
balanced preference to a cost-sensitive or comfort-sensitive preference, 
the semantics of feedback also change, and the system must adjust the 
preference profile based on the new interaction evidence. Fixed-profile 
methods continue to operate under outdated assumptions after prefer-
ence drift, while online calibration converts post-drift language feed-
back into new update signals and reduces the preference distance again. 
This behavior is consistent with the theoretical analysis: preference drift 
enters the error recursion as an external disturbance, and continuing 
contract-compliant feedback drives the profile back toward the changed 
reference preference profile.
Overall, the experimental results show that the language interface, 
CIPU, and MPC undertake semantic parsing, preference correction, and 
constrained execution, respectively. Study 2C shows that LLM-based se-
mantic parsing, the current-control-intent prompt rule, structured-schema 
consistency, and deterministic final authorization contribute to different 
stages of semantic recovery and routing. Study 2 A and Study 2B further 
show that intent-path routing and contract-event gating affect how update 
evidence is used and whether it is valid. Study 3 shows that, when the 
reference preference changes, continuing language feedback provides new 
calibration evidence and drives the preference profile to re-track the 
changed target. Accordingly, changes in closed-loop performance mainly 
depend on whether interaction evidence is accurately parsed, appropri-
ately routed, and incorporated into preference updating in a bounded 
manner, while MPC continues to maintain physical constraints and device 
coordination under the updated preference parameters.

5. Conclusion
This paper proposes a human-in-the-loop preference calibration 
framework for home energy management. The framework converts 
runtime natural-language feedback into contract-constrained structured 
events, uses CIPU to continuously update the user preference profile, 
and relies on MPC to coordinate multiple devices under safety con-
straints. The paper first establishes the building thermal model, HVAC 
model, EV charging model, and MPC execution mechanism for closed- 
loop control. It then develops the contract-constrained intent inter-
face, the explicit/vague dual-path handling mechanism, and the CIPU- 
based online updating method. Finally, closed-loop benchmark com-
parisons, mechanism ablations, a language-interface benchmark, and 
dynamic preference studies are conducted to evaluate long-term user 
alignment, the effectiveness of key mechanisms, structured language- 
parsing capability, and preference-drift re-tracking. The results show 
that runtime interaction feedback can further correct the preference 
profile beyond one-shot questionnaire initialization, reduce long-term 
preference mismatch and repeated complaints, and pull the online 
profile back toward the reference preference profile after user prefer-
ence changes. The component-level language-interface benchmark 
further distinguishes the contributions of LLM-based semantic parsing, 
the current-control-intent prompt rule, structured-schema consistency, 
and deterministic final authorization. On the frozen 188-utterance 
benchmark, the proposed interface achieves 100.00% Intent Accuracy, 
EEM, NTR, and RA, with an FDER of 0.00%. The current validation is 
mainly based on controlled simulation and role-play experiments. The 
language-interface benchmark contains utterances from three role- 
playing volunteers and therefore does not provide independent valida-
tion across unseen users or broader natural-language distributions. 
Larger participant cohorts, independent user corpora, and validation in 
real household environments are required before broader conclusions 
about linguistic generalizability can be drawn. In addition, the current 
device scope mainly covers HVAC and EV charging, and the coordina-
tion effect after adding more flexible household loads remains to be 
further studied.
Future work will proceed in two directions. First, long-term 
deployment experiments will be conducted in real homes or prototype 
systems to examine the stability of the framework under real user 
feedback, device communication errors, and long-term operational 
disturbances. Second, the human-in-the-loop LLM interaction mecha-
nism will be further developed, and demand-response signals will be 
incorporated into the home energy management loop, so that user 
preferences, household-side flexibility, and grid-side regulation needs 
can be expressed and coordinated within a unified framework.
