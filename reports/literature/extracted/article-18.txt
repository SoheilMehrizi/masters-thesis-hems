A review on digital twin application in photovoltaic energy systems: 
challenges and opportunities

Abstract
As the global demand for sustainable energy solutions grows, photovoltaic (PV) power plants are increasingly vital, espe-
cially with the integration of innovative technologies like digital twins (DTs). Digital twin serves as dynamic digital replicas 
of physical assets, enhancing the monitoring, maintenance, and optimization of PV systems. This technology promises to 
revolutionize solar energy production by enabling more informed decision-making and operational efficiency. This review 
examines the integration of digital twin technology in PV systems, aiming to identify dominant trends, challenges, and 
opportunities. It involves a comprehensive analysis of existing literature, highlighting recent advancements and the evolv -
ing role of digital twin in enhancing the operational dynamics of solar power plants. The integration of digital twins in PV 
systems provides significant advantages, including improved performance monitoring and predictive maintenance. However, 
the adoption of this technology is hindered by challenges, such as high implementation costs and the lack of standardized 
procedures in the solar energy sector. Despite these obstacles, the potential for digital twin to enhance cost-efficiency, reli-
ability, and profitability in solar power operations is evident, with substantial growth projected in the digital twin market. This 
review underscores the transformative impact of digital twin technology on the solar power industry, suggesting that despite 
current challenges, the strategic implementation of digital twin could lead to significant improvements in the efficiency and 
reliability of photovoltaic systems. The study calls for further research into overcoming the barriers to adoption and explor-
ing innovative applications of digital twin technology in solar energy production, paving the way for more sustainable and 
efficient energy solutions.

1 Introduction
The increasing demand for clean energy has pushed pho-
tovoltaic (PV) power plants to the forefront of renewable 
energy solutions [1, 2]. However, effectively managing and 
maintaining these complex systems with traditional methods 
presents significant challenges. Digital twin (DT) technol-
ogy presents a revolutionary approach to overcome these 
limitations and transform solar power plant operations [3 , 
4]. The rapid growth of the renewable energy sector, cou-
pled with the adoption of digital twins, is reshaping solar 
energy generation. As forecasted by Bloomberg New Energy 
Finance, the projected increase of 56% in worldwide solar 
PV installations by 2023 indicates a rise in the adoption 
of renewable energy and signifies a significant change in 
the operational dynamics of solar power plants [5 ]. Digital 
twin, which can generate virtual replicas of actual assets, 
systems, and processes, holds the capacity to transform the 
solar power sector [6]. These digital representations provide 
unique and unmatched insights, making them a catalyst for 
optimizing operations and improving overall efficiency in 
solar power plants.
Integrating DT technology in photovoltaic (PV) power 
plants offers significant advantages and can effectively tackle 
diverse challenges. DT are exact digital representations of 
physical assets, offering operators a high level of control. 
This level of control enables them to make well-informed 
decisions based on the present circumstances and future 
objectives [3]. While digital twins offer potential advantages, 
their implementation poses challenges, including the high 
costs of implementation and maintenance and the absence of 
standardization in the solar industry. However, digital twins 
can potentially enhance cost reduction, efficiency improve-
ment, and profit increase for solar power plant operators 
through advancements in predictive maintenance, reliability, 
safety, design optimization, and yield optimization. They can 
expedite the detection of vulnerabilities in current systems 
and forecast potential issues, thus improving the efficiency 
of renewable energy sources and minimizing operational 
interruptions [7].
DTs can observe and assess the functioning of different 
elements in solar power plants, such as solar panels, invert-
ers, trackers, and supporting infrastructure [8, 9]. Operators 
can optimize performance by promptly identifying and rec-
tifying issues by comparing the virtual model's predictions 
with real-world data collected by sensors throughout the 
plant [6 ]. DTs can precisely identify the specific location 
and characteristics of issues within a solar PV plant, sim-
plifying the problem-solving process and minimizing peri-
ods of inactivity [10]. Furthermore, operators can remotely 
oversee and regulate solar PV plants using the digital twin 
technology, enhancing energy production, particularly for 
solar farms spread across different locations [ 11, 12]. DT 
technology integrates technologies, such as the internet 
of things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), and advanced 
analytics to create real-time, data-driven simulations of 
physical objects. This allows for informed decision-making 
based on the present circumstances and future objectives 
[11, 13]. DTs can additionally forecast the power generation 
of the solar power facility, aiding operators in minimizing 
expenses, enhancing effectiveness, and maximizing profits 
[14].
The DT market is expected to experience substantial

2  Fundamentals of digital twin
2.1  Definition
The concept of DT has evolved rapidly since its introduc-
tion in 2013 by Grieves and Vickers [18– 20]. There’s no 
single, universally accepted definition for DT due to its 
multifaceted nature. Researchers have explored DT from 
various angles, emphasizing aspects, such as models, data, 
connections, and functionalities. These diverse interpreta-
tions highlight the complex and ongoing development of 
digital twin technology. Table  1 summarizes various defi-
nitions of digital twins proposed by different researchers.
Early conceptualizations of DT include simulations 
integrating various data scales to represent a complete 
physical system, as proposed by NASA in 2012 [20– 22]. 
Additionally, researchers have defined DT as streamlined 
computational models for simulating real-world systems 
[23], digital representations focused on replicating opera -
tional states [ 24], and frameworks replicating specific 
aspects of physical entities in manufacturing [25].
Another perspective views DT as a data-driven virtual 
representation informed by information from the physical 
object or process, as defined by Mukherjee et al. [26].

2.2  Key components
Creating a DT involves three stages: constructing a rep-
resentation of the physical system or assets, generating a 
virtual replica of the system or model, and establishing 
connectivity between the virtual and physical systems, as 
shown in Fig.  2. Achieving the integration and interaction 
of all these sub-systems is necessary to perform absolute 
system modeling [ 8, 46]. Creating a DT involves three 
essential parts in the context of a photovoltaic energy 
system.
2.2.1  Physical system
The physical system in a photovoltaic (PV) energy field 
refers to the concrete, tangible entities and processes 
involved in solar power generation. It encompasses the 
complete ecosystem of a solar power plant, consisting of 
individual solar panels that capture sunlight and convert it 
into electricity. Inverters convert the generated direct cur -
rent (DC) into alternating current (AC) for residential and 
commercial use, and also contribute to system monitoring 
and optimization [47]. Additional components may include 
mounting structures, wiring, and battery storage units for 
enhanced system reliability.
Solar panels, the most prominent component of a PV 
system, are composed of semiconductor materials known as 
photovoltaic cells [48]. These cells produce electric power 
when they come into contact with sunlight. The effective-
ness, capability, and long-lasting nature of these panels are 
crucial factors in determining the overall performance of 
the PV system. Mounting structures securely position and 
tilt the solar panels for maximum sunlight exposure. Some 
structures incorporate tracking systems that automatically 
adjust the panels' orientation throughout the day to opti-
mize energy production. Battery storage units (optional) 
enable the accumulation of surplus energy produced during 
peak sunlight hours, which can subsequently be used during 
periods of reduced sunlight or nighttime. Monitoring and 
control systems collect data on various parameters, such 
as energy generation, system efficiency, and environmen-
tal conditions. They are vital in operational management, 
maintenance scheduling, and performance optimization 
[49].
2.2.2  Virtual model
The virtual model, the heart of digital twin technology, func-
tions as a comprehensive and data-driven digital replica of 
the physical PV system [38]. This model, built using data 
from the physical system and its surroundings, replicates the 
real-world asset's physical and operational characteristics 
[48, 50].
The process starts with gathering extensive data from the 
physical photovoltaic (PV) system using sensors to monitor 
parameters such as solar irradiance, panel efficiency, temper-
ature, and electrical output [51]. Environmental data, such as 
weather conditions, temperature variations, and geographical 
details, are also incorporated for a holistic understanding of 
the system's operational context. Advanced modeling tech-
niques, including 3D modeling, CAD representations, and 
intricate mathematical and physical models, are employed to 
construct the virtual model [52]. These models consider the 
interplay between components and environmental factors, 
ensuring an accurate representation of the physical system's 
dynamics.

The virtual model utilizes simulation and analysis tools

continuous data integration [54]. This allows for strategic 
scheduling of interventions, minimizing downtime and 
extending the system's lifespan. The model also serves as 
a controlled environment for experimenting with differ -

experimentation with new technologies, and development

A key characteristic of the virtual model is its dynamism. 
It constantly adapts and evolves with newly acquired data 
and insights. This adaptability is essential for ensuring accu-
racy throughout the PV system's lifespan as components age, 
environmental conditions change, and new technologies 
emerge [56]. The model's ongoing update mechanism guar-
antees synchronization with the physical system, accurately 
reflecting its current state and performance. Furthermore, 
the virtual model can incorporate machine learning (ML) 
algorithms and artificial intelligence (AI), progressively 
learning from past performance, adjusting to new circum-
stances, and gradually enhancing its predictive capabilities 
[57].
2.2.3  Data connection and analytics
The data connection serves as the essential communica-
tion channel within the digital twin framework, connect-
ing a PV energy system's physical and digital aspects [9 ]. 
Uninterrupted data transmission is crucial for the immediate 
coordination of the tangible object with its digital replica, 
guaranteeing the precision and significance of the digital 
twin [58].
2.2.3.1 Data connection A comprehensive array of sensors 
integrated into the physical photovoltaic system enables data 
connectivity. These sensors monitor various parameters, 
such as solar irradiance, panel temperature, energy output, 
and environmental conditions [59]. Data are transmitted to 
the digital twin platform through various technologies, such 
as wired and wireless networks, IoT protocols, and cloud-
based services [60–62]. This configuration ensures expe-
dited and protected data transfer, facilitating instantaneous 
updates and analysis. The data connection enables bidirec-
tional communication, allowing the digital twin to provide 
feedback that the physical system incorporates, promoting 
operational modifications and establishing maintenance 
timetables to enhance the PV system's performance [63].
By establishing a data connection, operators can continu-
ously monitor the PV system's performance and receive real-
time information regarding emerging issues [64]. The digital 
twin can predict system failures or maintenance require-
ments by examining data trends and patterns. It enables pro-
active interventions that minimize downtime and prolong the 
system's operational lifespan. The data connection allows for 
the simulation of various operational scenarios in the virtual 
model, enabling the identification of the most effective set-
tings and strategies that improve the efficiency and output 
of the PV system [ 65]. In the broader framework of smart 
grids and energy systems, the data connection enables the 
integration of the PV system's digital twin with other energy 
assets, thereby enhancing the efficiency of energy distribu-
tion and utilization [12].
2.2.3.2 Data analytics In the context of DTs, analytics 
pertains to the advanced processing, analysis, and inter -
pretation of data collected from the physical PV system 
and its digital replica [66]. The intelligence layer of the 
digital twin is responsible for processing raw data and 
generating meaningful insights using sophisticated ana-
lytical techniques [67]. The first step entails purifying, 
standardizing, and organizing the extensive data gath-
ered, readying it for subsequent analysis. This guaran-
tees precision and dependability in the generated insights 
[68].
Predictive analytics utilizes statistical models and 
machine learning algorithms to anticipate future condi-
tions and events, such as possible component failures or 
decreases in energy production. It allows for proactive 
measures to minimize risks. Analytics tools utilize histori-
cal and real-time data to detect patterns and inefficiencies, 
providing recommendations for improving energy output 
and system performance in different circumstances [69]. 
Advanced analytics evaluate the well-being and state of 
PV system parts, suggesting maintenance tasks to avoid 
unforeseen malfunctions and prolong the system's lifes-
pan. Machine learning algorithms acquire knowledge from 
past data to generate predictions or make decisions without 
requiring explicit programming for tasks [70]. Machine 
learning is applied in photovoltaic (PV) systems to predict 
energy production with precision, detect anomalies, and 
optimize operational configurations [71]. Artificial Intelli-
gence encompasses a broader range of technologies, includ-
ing machine learning, which aims to replicate intelligent 
behavior in machines. Artificial intelligence can automate 
intricate decision-making processes, improve the detection 
of faults, and offer advanced control strategies for photo-
voltaic operations [57]. Data visualization tools provide a

2.3  Technology enablers of digital twin in PV 
energy systems
In photovoltaic (PV) energy systems, digital twins enhance 
system efficiency, predict maintenance needs, and opti-
mize performance. The operation of digital twins revolves 
around three fundamental processes: data acquisition, 
modeling, and application. Data acquisition involves 
gathering relevant information, data modeling entails 
constructing digital representations based on this infor -
mation, and data application consists of using these digital 
constructs to derive actionable insights and make informed 
decisions. The key technologies enabling these processes 
include the internet of things (IoT), artificial intelligence 
(AI), extended reality (XR), and cloud computing as 
shown in Fig.  3.
2.3.1  Internet of things (IoT)
Digital twins rely heavily on the internet of things (IoT) for 
real-time data. IoT sensors embedded in physical objects 
continuously feed data to create and update digital represen-
tations [11]. The vast network of interconnected devices in 
IoT acts as the nervous system for digital twins.
The applications of IoT extend far beyond digital twins. It 
seamlessly integrates everyday objects like cameras, appli-
ances, vehicles, and sensors into a network, enabling them 
to communicate and share data [73]. Using tools such as 
microcontrollers and communication protocols, IoT gathers 
vast amounts of data from diverse sources, fueling innova-
tion in healthcare, smart homes, renewable energy, traffic 
management, and more [74]. The focus on solar PV in IoT 
applications stems from its widespread use in distributed 
generation. Integrating IoT solutions with solar PV systems 
offers benefits for both service providers and customers, 
leading to improved functionality, efficiency, and new busi-
ness opportunities within the IoT field [75].

As demonstrated in these studies, IoT dramatically 
improves monitoring and control capabilities beyond what 
manual oversight can achieve. Integrating IoT technology 
into solar PV systems enhances our understanding of system 
behavior over time, opening doors for sophisticated business 
analytics and development of promotional strategies. There-
fore, promoting the implementation of IoT in the solar PV 
industry is crucial to optimize system efficiency, minimize

In IoT systems, the effective gathering and utilization 
of data are paramount to their success. This is particularly 
true in photovoltaic (PV) systems, where precise monitor -
ing and control can significantly enhance performance and 
efficiency. The strategic integration of various sensors is 
essential for capturing real-time data on environmental and 
operational conditions. By leveraging this data, IoT-ena-
bled PV systems can achieve optimal performance, proac-
tive maintenance, and seamless operation. Here are some 
concrete examples of sensor integration strategies for IoT 
applications:
Temperature and irradiance sensors:
In PV systems, temperature and irradiance sensors are 
crucial for monitoring environmental conditions that affect 
the performance of solar panels [59]. These sensors can be 
placed on the surface of the PV panels and in the surround-
ing environment to provide real-time data on solar radiation 
and ambient temperature. For instance, a temperature sen-
sor such as DS18B20, GY-BME280 can be integrated with 
an Arduino microcontroller to measure panel temperature, 
while a pyranometer can be used to measure solar irradi-
ance [80]. The data collected from these sensors helps in 
optimizing the PV system's performance by allowing for 
adjustments based on temperature fluctuations and sunlight 
availability.
Current and voltage sensors:
To monitor the electrical output of PV systems, current 
and voltage sensors are integrated. Sensors, such as ACS712 
current sensor and voltage dividers, can be connected to 
an Arduino to continuously measure the current and volt -
age output of the solar panels [81]. This data is crucial for 
assessing the performance and efficiency of the PV system. 
It helps in identifying issues such as shading or panel degra-
dation early on, allowing for timely maintenance and ensur-
ing the system operates at its optimal capacity.
Humidity sensors:
Humidity sensors, such as the DHT22, can be utilized to 
monitor the moisture levels in the environment surrounding 
the PV system. High humidity can affect the performance 
of solar panels and related electronics [82]. By integrating 
these sensors, real-time humidity data can be collected and 
used to adjust operational parameters or alert maintenance 
teams if adverse conditions are detected. This proactive 
approach helps in maintaining the efficiency and longevity 
of the PV system.

2.3.4  Cloud computing
Cloud computing, as defined by the National Institute of 
Standards and Technology (NIST), is a model that allows 
convenient and immediate access to a shared collection of 
digital computing resources, including networks, servers, 
storage, applications, and services [114]. These resources 
can be efficiently allocated and released with minimal 
supervision or interaction with service providers. Cloud sys-
tems provide a collective IT infrastructure with the goal of 
improving operational efficiency, scalability, maintainability, 
and reliability while also reducing costs. The services are 
provided through three primary models: Software as a Ser -
vice (SaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS), and Infrastructure 
as a Service (IaaS) [115]. A solitary cloud data center could 
possess storage and computing capabilities that far exceed 
the combined capacity of all supercomputing facilities 
worldwide. Cloud providers possess a substantial quantity of 
interconnected servers at a minimal cost. This infrastructure 
comprises interconnected, large-scale pooled systems that 
utilize virtualization techniques to deliver high-performance 
data storage capabilities [116]. It operates in conjunction 
with a local network connection, capable of executing any -
where from a few to hundreds of billions of computations 
per second, depending on the level of demand.
The infrastructure as a service (IaaS) model in cloud 
computing provides sufficient storage options, which is con-
tributing to the growing adoption of cloud-based data stor -
age due to its large capacity [117]. Virtualization mitigates 
storage difficulties in IoT applications by furnishing funda-
mental resources, such as storage, processing, and network-
ing [118]. Cloud computing improves mission development 
by maximizing central processing unit (CPU) utilization 
and storage efficiency, resulting in decreased requirements 
for local storage and reduced associated expenses [119]. It 
additionally enables the organization of tasks in a way that 
minimizes the costs associated with computation and com-
munication, as well as reducing the duplication of data. The 
increasing demand for rapid and secure storage solutions is 
driven by the rapid advancement in cloud storage techniques 
[120]. Cisco and the international data corporation (IDC) 
have made predictions that emphasize the substantial growth 
of cloud computing and data generation in future [121]. It 
underscores the importance of cloud providers to enhance 
and improve their services.
Cloud computing uses virtualization technology to offer 
users computational resources that are readily available, 
flexible, reliable, dynamic, scalable, and widely accessi-
ble [122]. An essential characteristic of cloud computing 
is its elasticity, which enables systems to adjust to varying 
workloads efficiently [123]. The extensive utilization of 
cloud services in diverse internet applications highlights 
the significance of efficient resource scheduling to prevent 
problems such as prolonged computation durations, reduced 
profitability, suboptimal data processing capacity, esca-
lated expenses, and ineffective resource utilization [124 ]. 
The strong relationship between the use of resources and 
the amount of power consumed in cloud computing, espe -
cially in cases of excessive or insufficient use, emphasizes 
the increasing emphasis on incorporating energy-efficient 
solutions in cloud infrastructures [125].
Guamán et al. [126] developed the "Solar Manager," a 
monitoring system for photovoltaic (PV) systems. The sys-
tem utilizes an Arduino Mega 2560 board to collect data 
on parameters such as ambient temperature, humidity, and 
PV output. The data are sent to the cloud through a nearby 
router, enabling effective remote monitoring of independent 
PV systems. The cost-effectiveness and feasibility of the sys-
tem are improved using cloud computing and open-source 
software, providing a cost-efficient solution for managing 
PV systems. In their study, Vignesh and Samydurai [127] 
employed IoT technology to oversee the lifespan of PV pan-
els. They accomplished this by incorporating a PIC 16F877 
microcontroller equipped with sensors to gage temperature 
and energy production accurately. The transmission of data 
to a base station was enabled by a ZigBee transceiver, which 
subsequently forwarded it to a host computer operating a 
customized visual basic application. This application trans-
mitted the gathered data to a server for distribution through 
cloud-based technology, thereby improving the accessibility 
and management of the performance data for PV panels.

3.4  Fault detection and diagnostics
Fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) is crucial in diverse 
industries, such as engineering [131], manufacturing [132], 
and maintenance [133]. This procedure is of utmost impor -
tance in the identification and analysis of abnormalities, as 
well as the isolation of malfunctions in systems, processes, 
or machinery. The main goal of FDD is to ensure these com-
ponents' secure, effective, and dependable operation. The 
FDD process consists of two primary phases: fault detection 
and diagnosis.
• Fault Detection: This refers to identifying anomalies, 
deviations, or unforeseen behaviors through real-time 
monitoring or analysis of historical data. It frequently 
uses techniques, such as threshold-based detection and 
statistical methods. When applied to photovoltaic power 
plants, digital twin technology can facilitate fault detec-
tion by precisely identifying the type and location of 
problems within the plant, expediting troubleshooting, 
and decreasing downtime [134, 135].
• Fault Diagnosis: Involves thoroughly assessing faults to 
determine their type, size, location, time of detection, 
and behavior [136]. Fault diagnosis generally encom-
passes detection, isolation, identification, classification, 
and evaluation. However, in some cases, combining fault 
isolation and identification is called a fault diagnosis step 
[137]. The fault diagnosis process in photovoltaic power 
plants is multifaceted and aims to ensure the plant's effi-
cient, safe, and reliable operation [138].
3.5  Remote monitoring and control
Over the past twenty years, PV monitoring systems have 
gained significant interest because the lack of proper moni-
toring has made it difficult to identify and address operation 
and maintenance issues, significantly reducing the lifespan 
of PV systems [10, 139, 140]. A comprehensive understand-
ing of the difficulties in monitoring a PV system is crucial 
for developing an effective photovoltaic (PV) monitoring 
system and satisfactorily improving power generation [141]. 
Initially, the necessary commercial data acquisition in the 
market comprises a significant portion of the expenses asso-
ciated with a monitoring system. Automatic data acquisi-
tion systems monitor a system's performance and control 
its operation. The acquired data can be utilized to assess 
the effectiveness of the plant over extended durations and to 
enhance forthcoming systems in terms of their performance 
and dependability [6 , 142]. Various data acquisition sys-
tems have been created to be used in diverse applications, 
such as measuring, acquiring, and processing environmental 
variables [143], monitoring and assessing the performance 
of PV systems [144, 145], monitoring the condition of bat-
teries for water pumping PV systems [146], and measuring 
operational parameters of hybrid photovoltaic–diesel sys-
tems [147].
The conventional methods for monitoring a PV plant 
necessitate using a personal computer (PC) as a computa-
tional system to execute specialized software in real-time. 
Furthermore, the electrical power generated by a PV system 
can fluctuate due to the impact of different malfunctions and 
imperfections on the PV modules [60]. Consequently, it is 
imperative for a human operator to promptly recognize and 
rectify any identified malfunction on any PV panel, irrespec-
tive of the system they are working with [148]. Therefore, 
to tackle the challenges mentioned above, it is necessary 
to implement an intelligent monitoring system and a pre-
cise prediction model. Integrating remote monitoring and 
control is an essential aspect of digital twin technology in 
PV power plants [149]. Operators can utilize this technol-
ogy to effectively oversee and enhance energy production, 
promptly detect and address problems, and make well-
informed decisions using up-to-the-minute data [90]. This

of PV plants. Transient environmental factors, such as dust 
storms and unexpected shading, caused by nearby structures 
or vegetation growth can cause distortions in these measure-
ments by obstructing the readings of solar irradiance. These 
interferences have the potential to introduce irregularities 
in the data patterns, which can mislead the predictive and 
analytical algorithms of the digital twin [170]. Hence, the 
dependence of DTs on data of superior quality poses a com-
plex challenge for PV energy systems. The convergence of 
adverse environmental conditions, equipment degradation, 
and the logistical challenges of operating in remote areas 
gives rise to an intricate network of factors that can under -
mine data integrity. In order to reduce errors and tackle 
these difficulties, it is crucial to prioritize strengthening 
data collection systems, enhancing maintenance procedures, 
and optimizing data processing algorithms [159]. Directly 
addressing these challenges is critical to fully realizing the 
potential of digital twins in optimizing PV energy systems, 
paving the way for more efficient and reliable renewable 
energy sources.
5.2  Complexities in data integration
Integrating data into PV energy systems to create an efficient 
digital replica presents a substantial technical obstacle stem-
ming from these systems' inherent variety and distribution. 
PV installations are more complex because of their multi-
faceted nature, with each component generating its own data 
stream for the overall design. Integrating this diverse data 
into a unified and logical digital twin model poses a compli-
cated challenge that requires careful and thorough resolution 
to fully exploit the capabilities of digital twin technology in 
the solar energy industry [ 171]. Photovoltaic (PV) energy 
systems consist of complex arrangements of various ele-
ments, such as solar panels, inverters, meters, and environ-
mental sensors, among other components. Each component 
functions within its distinct area, producing data represent-
ing its efficiency, condition, and environmental interactions. 
The challenge stems from using diverse data sources that 
employ different formats for data representation, governed 
by various manufacturers' proprietary protocols and the 
components' technical specifications [172].
In order to ensure data compatibility within the digital 
twin framework, it is necessary to employ complex transla-
tion and normalization processes due to diverse data. Adding 
to the difficulty of dealing with diverse data is the inconsist-
ency in the time intervals at which various components pro-
duce data. For example, certain sensors can offer real-time 
updates at short intervals, capturing quick environmental 
changes. On the other hand, energy meters may record data 
at longer intervals, reflecting the total energy produced or 
consumed over time. Intricate synchronization mechanisms 
are required to guarantee that the digital twin model appro-
priately represents the concurrent states of these components 
to the disparate data generation rates from various compo-
nents in PV installations [173]. Numerous photovoltaic 
(PV) installations are not limited to one specific area. Still, 
they are distributed across various geographic regions, each 
with unique environmental conditions and operating within 
different time zones. The dispersion of geographical loca-
tions introduces a level of intricacy to integrating data, as 
it requires considering the variations in local time and the 
distinct environmental factors specific to each site. Accu-
rately contextualizing and synchronizing data from different 
locations within the digital twin model is a challenging task 
that requires advanced data management strategies capable 
of handling spatial and temporal differences [174].

5.3  Data security and privacy
Integrating DTs in various industries, such as PV energy 
systems, presents intricate security and privacy obstacles. 
The susceptibility of DTs to various cybersecurity risks 
stems directly from their need for seamless data trans-
mission across an interconnected system network. These 
threats endanger the trustworthiness and dependability of 
DT models and present substantial hazards to the sensitive 
data they handle and store. The core of DT technology lies 
in its capacity to combine and harmonize data from various 
origins, generating a comprehensive virtual representation of 
physical systems. The interconnected nature of DT is essen-
tial for their functionality and effectiveness, but it also cre-
ates various avenues for cyber threats. Each element in the 
network, such as sensors, IoT devices, cloud storage solu-
tions, and user interfaces, can be vulnerable to exploitation 
as an entry point for malicious activities. The difficulty lies 
in protecting these numerous weak points from unauthorized 
entry and guaranteeing the integrity of data transmission 
across the network [177].
DTs require a large amount of data as they depend on 
constantly gathering, handling, and examining extensive 
amounts of information. This data frequently encompasses 
technical and operational specifics and conceivably sensitive 
personal or proprietary information. The privacy implica-
tions are substantial, especially in sectors where DT may 
handle personal data subject to strict privacy regulations. 
Implementing stringent data protection measures is cru-
cial to ensure compliance with regulations and effectively 
safeguard personal and sensitive data from breaches. The 
cybersecurity environment is continuously changing as 
malicious actors utilize progressively advanced methods to 
exploit weaknesses in digital systems. Due to their intricate 
interconnections and dependence on software-based models, 
digital twins are highly vulnerable to various cyber threats 
[178]. These encompass various cyber threats, such as mal-
ware attacks, phishing, ransomware, and advanced persistent 
threats (APTs), which can secretly penetrate systems and 
evade detection for prolonged durations.
To effectively protect against these sophisticated threats, 
it is necessary to have cutting-edge security technologies 
and a proactive, adaptable security strategy to adjust to new 
and emerging risks. In addition to unauthorized access and 
data breaches, another major obstacle in safeguarding digi-
tal twins is guaranteeing the integrity of the data inputted 
into and produced by these models. Manipulating or tam-
pering with data can cause digital twin simulations to be 
inaccurate, which can lead to flawed decision-making and 
operational inefficiencies. Ensuring the authenticity and reli-
ability of data sources is a challenge, especially in complex 
ecosystems where data may come from multiple third-party 
providers. Securing digital twins requires a comprehensive 
strategy, including physical, network, application, and data 
security. Every layer poses distinct challenges, ranging from 
ensuring the security of physical devices and network com-
munications to incorporating secure coding techniques and 
data encryption methodologies. Coordinating security meas-
ures across multiple layers, each potentially utilizing distinct 
technologies, standards, and protocols, contributes to the 
overall difficulty of protecting digital twins from cyber risks. 
The security and privacy challenges related to digital twins 
are intricate and multifaceted due to these systems' intercon-
nected and data-intensive nature and the advanced nature 
of potential cyber threats [179]. In order to address these 
challenges, a comprehensive and intricate security strategy 
is required. The strategy should encompass technological 
measures, organizational policies, user education, and regu-
lation adherence. With the increasing popularity of digital 
twins in different industries, it is crucial to prioritize their 
security and safeguard the privacy of the data they manage, 
and this requires constant vigilance and flexible cybersecu-
rity strategies.

5.4  Implementation and maintenance costs
The introduction of DT technology in the photovoltaic (PV) 
energy industry signifies the beginning of a new period 
characterized by improved operational efficiency and sys-
tem optimization. However, the progress in implementing 
this revolutionary technology is impeded by financial bar -
riers, primarily due to the costs associated with its adop-
tion and ongoing maintenance. The expenses related to DT 
in PV energy systems, which include various investments, 
such as technological infrastructure and human expertise, 
pose a significant obstacle to their widespread adoption 
[180]. The fundamental basis of a DT system lies in its 
technological infrastructure, which necessitates a significant 
upfront financial commitment. It encompasses acquiring 
cutting-edge sensors and IoT devices crucial for collecting 
data in real time, high-capacity computing resources for 
data processing and simulation, and resilient networking 
equipment to guarantee smooth data transmission. Further -
more, the requirement for secure and scalable cloud storage 
solutions or on-premises data centers to accommodate the 
digital twin platform significantly increases the initial setup 
expenses.
Integrating these technological components into a cohe-
sive system requires financial investment and a strategic 
approach to infrastructure development. It involves ensur -
ing compatibility and scalability to accommodate future 
growth and technological advancements. Acquiring the 
human expertise required for developing, deploying, and 
managing digital twin systems is a challenge beyond 
the hardware and software. This expertise encompasses 
various fields, including data science and software engi-
neering, as well as specialized knowledge in PV energy 
systems. The scarcity of professionals with the requisite 
proficiency and background in digital twin technology 
often leads to increased costs for recruiting, educating, 
and retaining such individuals. Allocating resources to 
talent development and ongoing education is crucial for 
cultivating and sustaining a proficient workforce capable 
of effectively navigating the intricacies of DT systems, 
thereby contributing to the financial burden. Once a digi-
tal twin system functions, it involves ongoing expenses 
beyond simple upkeep. These expenses encompass costs 
associated with system updates and upgrades, essential for 
maintaining the DT in line with the most recent technolog-
ical advancements and operational needs. Data manage-
ment costs, including data storage, processing, analytics, 
data integrity, and security, comprise a substantial portion 
of ongoing expenses.
Hence, the financial obstacles linked to the imple-
mentation and upkeep of digital twin systems in the PV 
energy industry are complex, involving upfront invest-
ments in technology and infrastructure, expenses related 
to acquiring and cultivating expertise, continuous opera -
tional costs, and the necessity for regular system updates 
to address technological obsolescence. In order to address 
these challenges, it is imperative to implement a strategic 
approach to financial planning and allocation of resources. 
It involves weighing the immediate expenses against the 
long-term advantages of improved operational efficiency 
and system optimization provided by digital twin tech-
nology. To fully harness the potential of digital twins in 
advancing sustainable energy solutions, it is crucial to 
effectively navigate the financial obstacles that arise as the 
PV sector progresses.

7  Conclusion
This review has extensively explored the critical role that 
DT technology plays in improving the sustainability and 
efficiency of PV energy systems. As the world's energy 
demands grow and the need for sustainable solutions 
becomes ever more pressing, PV power plants are increas-
ingly important players in the renewable energy market. 
However, traditional methodologies struggle to address the 
significant challenges associated with effective management 
and optimization. In this context, DT technology emerges 
as an innovative solution for monitoring, maintaining, and 
optimizing PV systems.
DTs can create accurate replicas of physical assets, 
offering a platform for real-time monitoring and predictive 
maintenance needs. They provide operators with exceptional 
insights into the current condition and future productivity 
of PV installations, ultimately improving decision-making 
and operational efficiency. Despite the promising benefits 
of DT technology, its adoption faces challenges, such as 
high implementation costs and the need for industry-wide 
standardization. Nevertheless, the expected expansion of the 
DT market indicates a wider recognition of its potential to 
transform not only the photovoltaic sector but the entire field 
of renewable energy. Overcoming current challenges will be 
crucial to optimizing the potential of digital transformation 
technology during this technological revolution.
The advancement of sustainable energy, especially in the 
photovoltaic sector, will be heavily influenced by develop-
ments in DT technology. DTs have the potential to revo-
lutionize how we handle and enhance renewable energy 
sources by addressing current challenges and capitalizing 
on new opportunities. This review emphasizes the necessity 
of ongoing research and development in this field to fully 
exploit the potential of digital twins in advancing the shift 
toward a more efficient, dependable, and sustainable energy 
future.
Therefore, integrating DT technology into PV energy 
systems is essential to solving the complex issues facing 
the renewable energy industry today. By overcoming current 
challenges and harnessing the full potential of DTs, we can 
unlock a cleaner, greener energy future and make significant 
progress toward achieving global sustainable development 
goals.
