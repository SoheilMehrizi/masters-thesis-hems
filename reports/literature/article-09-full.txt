Abstract
The increasing penetration of distributed renewable energy sources and electric vehicles
(EVs) introduces significant operational challenges for residential energy management
systems (HEMS), including stochastic renewable generation, uncertain load demand, device
coupling, and physical safety constraints. This paper proposes a digital-twin-aided safe
multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for coordinated energy management in
renewable-integrated residential systems. The proposed approach models the battery
energy storage system and the EV as independent agents and employs a multi-agent soft
actor–critic (MASAC) algorithm with a centralised critic to capture the interactions among
distributed energy resources. To improve decision quality under uncertainty, a digital twin
module is developed to maintain a virtual representation of the residential energy system,
synchronise operational states, update degradation-sensitive parameters, and generate
short-term predictive information on photovoltaic (PV) generation and household load.
The updated digital twin states and forecasts are incorporated into the observations of
the reinforcement learning agents. In addition, a safety projection layer is incorporated to
improve operational feasibility during both training and deployment. The environment
considers realistic residential characteristics, including time-of-use electricity prices, battery
degradation, EV mobility patterns, and grid energy trading. Simulation results show that
the proposed framework reduces daily energy costs compared with rule-based baselines
while maintaining EV charging reliability and operational feasibility. These results highlight
the potential of combining predictive information, safety-constrained action execution, and
multi-agent reinforcement learning for intelligent residential energy management.
Keywords: residential energy management; digital twin; multi-agent reinforcement
learning; soft actor-critic; electric vehicles; battery energy storage; photovoltaic generation;
safety constraints
1. Introduction
The rapid deployment of distributed renewable energy resources, residential battery
energy storage systems (BESS), and electric vehicles (EVs) is fundamentally transform-
ing residential energy systems [1]. In recent years, rooftop photovoltaic (PV) generation,

household battery storage, and EV charging infrastructures have been increasingly inte-
grated into domestic energy systems to improve renewable energy utilisation, enhance
self-consumption, and reduce carbon emissions [1]. Although these technologies provide
significant opportunities for more sustainable residential energy operation, they also intro-
duce substantial operational complexity for home energy management systems (HEMS) [2].
In particular, the intermittency of PV generation, stochastic household load demand, time-
varying electricity prices, and uncertain EV arrival and departure patterns create a highly
dynamic and uncertain decision-making environment [3]. As a result, intelligent energy
management strategies are required to coordinate multiple distributed energy resources
and achieve efficient, reliable, and cost-effective operation of residential energy systems [4].
Traditional residential energy management strategies are typically based on rule-
based control or optimisation-based approaches [5]. Rule-based strategies are simple to
implement and easy to interpret, but they often lack flexibility under varying operating
conditions and may fail to fully exploit the coordination potential among PV generation,
BESS operation, and EV charging [4]. Optimisation-based methods, such as mixed-integer
linear programming (MILP) and model predictive control (MPC), have been widely used to
address residential energy scheduling problems by explicitly modelling system dynamics,
user preferences, and operational constraints [2]. These approaches can achieve high-quality
scheduling solutions when accurate system models, forecasts, and problem formulations are
available [3]. However, their performance strongly depends on the accuracy of forecasts and
models, while their computational burden may increase significantly as more distributed
energy resources, constraints, and uncertainty sources are considered [2]. Therefore, more
adaptive and scalable control strategies are needed for residential energy management in
complex and uncertain environments.
In recent years, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising approach
for energy management problems due to its ability to learn control policies directly from
interactions with the environment without requiring an explicit optimisation model for
every operating condition [4]. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques have been
investigated for residential energy management, EV charging coordination, and the joint
operation of PV , BESS, and household demand under uncertainty [5]. By learning from
historical data or simulated environments, RL-based controllers can adapt to stochastic user
behaviour, renewable generation fluctuations, and dynamic system states [5]. Among exist-
ing RL algorithms, soft actor–critic (SAC) has attracted considerable attention because of its
stability, sample efficiency, and suitability for continuous control problems [6]. Recent work
has further demonstrated the potential of SAC-based residential energy management for
coordinating household energy resources under stochastic EV behaviour and operational
constraints [5].
Related Work and Research Gap
Recent studies have extended residential energy management research from conven-
tional optimisation and single-agent learning methods toward more integrated digital-twin-
assisted and multi-agent learning frameworks. One important research direction is the
integration of digital twins with reinforcement learning and predictive energy management.
Digital twins have been used to construct virtual representations of physical energy systems,
support online monitoring, update system states, provide short-term forecasting, and assist
intelligent decision-making. For example, Zhou et al. investigated the application of digital
twins for reinforcement-learning-based optimal scheduling and reliability management
enhancement of energy systems [7]. Sado et al. proposed a digital-twin-based forecasting
framework for power flow management in DC microgrids, showing the value of digital
twins in real-time monitoring and predictive operation [8]. In battery-related applications,

recent studies have further combined digital twins with state estimation, transfer learning,
and AI-assisted modelling for lithium-ion battery management [9,10]. These studies indi-
cate that a digital twin should not be regarded as a simple forecasting block only; rather,
it can provide virtual representation, state synchronisation, parameter updating, predic-
tive support, and controller interaction. However, the integration of digital-twin-based
state updating and forecasting with safe multi-agent reinforcement learning for residential
PV-BESS-EV energy management remains insufficiently studied.
A second relevant direction is safe reinforcement learning for constrained energy
management. In practical residential energy systems, reinforcement learning policies
may generate actions that violate physical constraints, such as battery SOC limits, EV
availability constraints, charging power limits, or grid exchange limits. Therefore, recent
safe RL studies have moved beyond purely penalty-based constraint handling and have
introduced safety layers, action projection, physical-informed correction, and constrained
policy optimisation. Wang et al. developed a physical-informed safe reinforcement learning
approach for secure energy management of multi-energy microgrids [11]. More general
safe RL studies have also demonstrated that action projection and runtime safety filtering
can explicitly map unsafe actions into feasible action spaces [ 12]. In power and energy
systems, projection-based deep reinforcement learning has been applied to safe distribution
grid control to improve hard-constraint satisfaction during online operation [13]. These
studies support the idea that operational constraints should be embedded directly into the
action execution process rather than being handled only by soft penalty terms in the reward
function. Nevertheless, detailed safety projection mechanisms for residential systems with
coupled BESS and EV control remain underdeveloped.
A third research direction is multi-agent reinforcement learning for distributed energy
storage, EV scheduling, and smart community energy management. Compared with single-
agent control, multi-agent reinforcement learning can better represent the distributed nature
of residential energy resources and the coupling among different controllable devices.
Kaewdornhan et al. proposed a real-time multi-home energy management method with
EV charging scheduling using multi-agent deep reinforcement learning [14]. Wang et al.
developed a scalable multi-agent deep reinforcement learning approach for residential
hybrid energy systems, highlighting the value of centralised training and decentralised
execution for scalable coordination [15]. Saberi et al. further investigated a multi-agent deep
constrained Q-learning method for smart building energy management under uncertainties,
demonstrating the relevance of combining multi-agent learning with constraint handling
in building-level energy systems [ 16]. These studies show that MARL is suitable for
coordinating distributed energy resources under uncertainty. However, existing MARL-
based energy management methods often focus on community-level coordination, EV
scheduling, or economic dispatch separately, while the combined use of digital twin
updating, safety-constrained action projection, and MASAC-based BESS–EV coordination
in a residential PV-integrated system has not been fully addressed.
Battery health condition estimation is also closely related to long-term energy man-
agement because battery ageing affects available capacity, charging/discharging efficiency,
degradation cost, and the economic value of storage scheduling. Recent battery digital
twin and prognostics studies have investigated advanced SOH/RUL estimation methods,
including digital-twin-supported battery state estimation using TCN-LSTM networks and
transfer learning [9], physics-guided TL-LSTM networks for early-stage lithium-ion battery
degradation trajectory prediction [17], and knowledge-distillation-based network compres-
sion frameworks for lithium-ion battery lifecycle management [18]. These studies indicate
that battery health estimation can provide useful information for degradation-aware digital
twin updating. In the present work, the digital twin framework incorporates degradation-

Based on the above review, the research gap can be summarised as follows. Exist-
ing studies have separately investigated digital twin-assisted forecasting and decision
support, safe reinforcement learning for constrained energy management, multi-agent
reinforcement learning for distributed energy scheduling, and battery health estimation for
digital twin applications. However, there is still a lack of an integrated residential energy
management framework that combines digital-twin-based state updating and prediction,
explicit safety-constrained continuous action projection, and MASAC-based coordination
between stationary battery storage and EV charging flexibility. This gap motivates the
digital-twin-aided safe MASAC framework proposed in this paper.
Based on the above research gaps, the main contributions of this paper are summarised
as follows:
• A digital-twin-aided safe MASAC framework is developed for coordinated residen-
tial energy management with PV generation, battery storage, and electric vehicles,
integrating virtual system representation, state synchronisation, degradation-aware
model updating, learning-based decision-making, safety projection, and reward-based
policy learning within a closed-loop architecture.
• A MASAC-based control strategy is adopted to learn continuous charging and dis-
charging decisions for residential energy resources under stochastic PV generation,
load demand, electricity prices, and EV availability.
• A safety projection layer is designed to enforce physical constraints, including battery
SOC limits, EV charging availability, and grid power constraints, thereby ensuring the
system’s safe operation.
• A digital twin-based virtual–physical interaction mechanism is introduced to support
state synchronisation, degradation-sensitive parameter updating, short-term PV/load
forecasting, and augmented observation construction for the MASAC controller.
• Extensive simulation experiments demonstrate that the proposed method improves
operational efficiency and economic performance compared to TD3 and conventional
rule-based strategies.

2. System Model and Problem Formulation
2.1. Residential Energy System Architecture
The residential energy management system considered in this study comprises several
key components: rooftop PV generation, a household electrical load, a BESS, an EV , and a
bidirectional connection to the utility grid [19]. Together, these components form a typical
integrated renewable residential energy system that enables flexible energy exchange and
intelligent energy scheduling within the household [19].
The photovoltaic generation unit provides renewable electricity that can be used
directly to supply the household load or charge local storage devices. However, due to the
intermittent, weather-dependent nature of solar power generation, PV output is inherently
uncertain and varies throughout the day [19]. As a result, additional flexible resources are
required to balance the mismatch between generation and consumption.

The battery energy storage system is used to improve the flexibility of the household
energy system. The battery can store surplus electricity when PV generation exceeds
load demand and discharge energy when household demand exceeds available renewable
generation. Through appropriate charging and discharge scheduling, the battery helps
reduce electricity purchase from the grid, smooth power fluctuations, and improve overall
system efficiency [20].
In addition to stationary battery storage, an electric vehicle is also considered a mobile
energy storage device. When the EV is connected to the residential charging point, it can
participate in the household energy management by charging from the grid or local PV
generation [21]. Depending on the system configuration, the EV battery may also provide
energy back to the household via vehicle-to-home (V2H) [21]. However, the availability of
the electric vehicle is limited by its arrival and departure patterns. Specifically, the EV is
only available for charging or discharging when it is parked at home. Furthermore, the
EV battery typically needs to reach a target SOC before departure to meet the user’s travel
requirements [19].
The residential energy system is also connected to the external power grid through a
bidirectional power exchange interface. When local generation and storage are insufficient
to meet the household’s electricity demand, electricity can be purchased from the grid.
In contrast, if local photovoltaic generation exceeds household consumption and storage
capacity, surplus energy can be exported back to the grid, depending on electricity pricing
policy. In this work, the time-of-use electricity price is considered, in which the electricity
purchase price varies across time periods throughout the day. This pricing mechanism
provides economic incentives to shift energy consumption and optimise energy storage
operations [20].
Within this energy management framework, the battery charging or discharging power
and the EV charging or discharging power are treated as controllable decision variables.
At each decision time step, the energy management controller determines the charging
and discharging actions for the battery and the EV to coordinate energy flows among PV
generation, household load demand, and grid power exchange. These decisions must
satisfy various physical and operational constraints, including battery power limits, SOC
limits, EV availability constraints, and grid power exchange limits [19].
The objective of the residential energy management system is to minimise overall
operational costs while maintaining reliable, safe system operation. The operational cost
typically includes the purchase cost of the electricity from the network, potential revenue
from exporting electricity, the battery degradation costs associated with the charging and
discharging cycles, and penalties for operational constraints such as electric vehicle charg-
ing requirements. Due to the stochastic nature of photovoltaic generation, household
load demand and EV usage patterns, determining an optimal control strategy is a chal-
lenging sequential decision-making problem that requires intelligent and adaptive energy
management approaches [22].

To formulate the residential energy management problem, this subsection introduces
mathematical models for the key components of the system. These models describe the
energy balance of the household system, as well as the operational dynamics of the battery
energy storage system and the electric vehicle [20].

The above problem is difficult to solve using conventional optimisation methods
because the residential energy management environment is characterised by uncertainty
in PV generation, load demand, electricity prices, and EV usage patterns. In addition,
the problem involves coupled device dynamics, time-varying constraints, and sequential
decision-making over the entire scheduling horizon. Therefore, instead of relying on an
exact model-based optimisation solver, this work addresses the problem using a reinforce-
ment learning framework that learns a control policy directly from interactions with the
environment while implicitly optimising the above objective under operational constraints.
3. Methodology
3.1. Overview of the Proposed Energy Management Framework
To address the residential energy management problem formulated in Section 2.3, this
paper proposes a digital-twin-aided safe multi-agent reinforcement learning framework
for coordinated control of distributed energy resources in residential energy systems.
The proposed framework integrates three main components: a reinforcement-learning-
based control module, a digital twin module, and a safety-constrained action projection
mechanism. The digital twin module is not treated as a standalone forecasting model.
Instead, it is designed as a virtual representation of the residential energy system that
supports state synchronisation, degradation-sensitive parameter updating, short-term
prediction, and interaction with the MASAC controller. Based on the updated digital twin
states and predictive information, the MASAC controller learns coordinated charging and
discharging strategies for the battery and EV , while the safety projection layer ensures that
the executed actions satisfy physical and operational constraints. These components jointly
enable adaptive, predictive, and safe residential energy management under renewable
generation and load uncertainty.
The overall architecture of the proposed framework is illustrated in Figure 1. As
shown in Figure 1, the residential energy environment provides the current operating
states, including the battery SOC, EV SOC, EV availability, time-of-use electricity price, PV
generation, and household load demand. These state variables are first used to update the
virtual states and degradation-sensitive parameters of the digital twin. The updated digital

3.2. Digital Twin Architecture and Synchronisation Mechanism
In the proposed framework, the digital twin is not treated merely as a forecasting
model, but as a virtual representation of the residential energy system that is continuously
updated using operational data from the physical or simulated household environment.
The digital twin consists of five functional layers: the data acquisition layer, the virtual
system model layer, the adaptive calibration layer, the forecasting layer, and the decision
interaction layer.
The data acquisition layer collects time-indexed operating information from the res-
idential energy system, including PV generation, household load demand, battery SOC,
EV SOC, EV availability, electricity price, and grid power exchange. At each decision time
step, these measurements are transferred from the physical system to the virtual model to
update the digital representation of the household energy system. In the simulation-based
implementation adopted in this study, this physical-to-virtual synchronisation is realised
through the simulated environment, which provides the same categories of measurements
that would be available from smart meters, battery management systems, EV chargers, and
PV inverters in practical deployment.
The virtual system model layer maintains mathematical models of the PV generation
unit, household load, battery energy storage system, EV battery, and grid interface. These
models describe the system states, operational constraints, and energy balance relationships
used by the MASAC controller. To improve consistency between the virtual model and the
operating system, an adaptive calibration layer is included. This layer updates the virtual
state and degradation-sensitive model parameters according to the mismatch between
measured and model-predicted states.

3.3. Multi-Agent Soft Actor–Critic Energy Management Algorithm
The residential energy management problem defined in Section 2.3 is inherently a
sequential decision-making problem under uncertainty. The stochastic nature of photo-
voltaic generation, household load demand, and EV arrival and departure patterns makes
it difficult to derive an optimal control policy using conventional deterministic optimisation
methods. To address these challenges, this work adopts a reinforcement learning approach
to learn optimal energy management strategies through interaction with the environment.
In the proposed framework, the battery energy storage system and the electric vehicle
are modelled as two independent agents that learn their control policies simultaneously.
Each agent determines its own charging or discharging action based on its local observa-
tions, while a centralised critic is used during training to capture the interactions among the
agents. This structure enables coordinated control of multiple distributed energy resources
while maintaining decentralised decision-making.

3.4.4. Projection Procedure
The complete safety projection procedure is summarised as follows. First, the raw
battery and EV actions generated by the MASAC actors are clipped to their device-level
signed power ranges. Second, the signed actions are decomposed into charging and
discharging components, which guarantees mutual exclusivity. Third, SOC-based feasible
charging and discharging limits are computed for the battery and EV . Fourth, the EV
availability constraint is enforced so that the EV cannot charge or discharge when it is not
connected. Fifth, the grid power exchange is checked, and flexible charging or discharging
actions are corrected if the grid import/export limit is violated. Finally, the corrected
feasible actions are executed in the residential energy environment.
This explicit projection procedure ensures that the reinforcement learning policy does
not rely solely on soft reward penalties to learn feasibility. Instead, infeasible actions are
corrected before execution, which improves training stability and deployment safety. The
reward function still encourages economically efficient behaviour, but physical constraints
are enforced directly by the projection layer.
It should be noted that the proposed framework does not rely on the cost function to
avoid simultaneous charging and discharging. Since each storage device is controlled by a
single signed power command and the charging/discharging components are obtained
from its positive and negative parts, the mutual exclusivity condition Pchi (t)Pdisi (t) = 0,
i ∈ {b, ev}, is guaranteed by construction at every decision time step. Therefore, no
additional binary exclusivity variable is required.

4.2. Training Configuration and Implementation Details
The proposed energy management framework is trained using the multi-agent soft
actor–critic (MASAC) algorithm described in Section 3. The training process is conducted
in the simulated residential energy environment introduced in Section 4.1, where each
episode corresponds to one full-day energy management cycle.
The overall training procedure of the proposed MASAC controller is shown in Figure 3.
At the beginning of each episode, the residential environment is initialised with the daily
PV profile, load profile, EV availability pattern, battery SOC, and EV SOC. The battery
and EV actor networks generate raw continuous actions according to their observations.
These actions are processed by the safety projection layer before being executed in the envi-
ronment. The environment then returns the reward, next state, and updated observations.
The transition is stored in the replay buffer, and mini-batches are sampled to update the
centralised critic and decentralised actor networks.
Figure 3. Training procedure of the proposed safe MASAC controller.
https://doi.org/10.3390/en19133098
=== PDF PAGE 23 ===
Energies 2026, 19, 3098 23 of 34
The reinforcement learning controller consists of two independent actor networks that
represent the battery agent and the EV agent, respectively. Each actor network outputs
continuous charging or discharging power commands for the corresponding device. A cen-
tralised critic network is used during training to evaluate the agents’ joint actions based on
the global system state. This centralised training strategy enables the learning process to
capture the coupling relationships among different distributed energy resources.
Both the actor and critic networks are implemented using fully connected multi-layer
perceptrons. The actor networks output the parameters of Gaussian action distributions,
which are subsequently transformed into feasible charging and discharging power com-
mands through a bounded activation function. The critic network estimates the joint
action-value function over the system state and the joint actions of the battery and EV
agents. To improve training stability and mitigate overestimation bias, two critic networks
are used following the twin-Q learning mechanism.
Experience replay is used to improve the sample efficiency of the learning process.
During training, the actor networks first generate raw continuous actions for the battery
and EV agents. These raw actions are then processed by the safety projection layer to obtain
feasible actions that satisfy power, SOC, EV availability, charging/discharging exclusivity,
and grid exchange constraints. The feasible projected actions are executed in the residential
energy environment and are stored in the replay buffer together with the corresponding
states, observations, rewards, and next states. Therefore, each stored transition contains
the actually executed projected action rather than the original raw action. Mini-batches
of transitions are randomly sampled from the replay buffer to update the actor and critic
networks, thereby breaking temporal correlations between consecutive samples while
maintaining consistency between stored actions and actual environment transitions.
To further stabilise training, target critic networks are maintained and updated using
Polyak averaging. The policy networks are optimised using stochastic gradient descent
with the Adam optimiser. The entropy temperature parameter in the MASAC algorithm is
automatically adjusted during training to balance exploration and exploitation. Through
repeated interactions with the environment, the agents gradually learn coordinated control
policies that minimise the long-term operational cost of the residential energy system.

summarised in Table 3. These parameters were selected based on commonly used config-
urations in deep reinforcement learning applications for energy management and were
empirically validated to ensure stable training performance.

4.3. Benchmark Strategies and Evaluation Metrics
To assess the effectiveness of the proposed multi-agent reinforcement learning frame-
work, several benchmark control strategies are implemented for comparison. These baseline
methods represent commonly used heuristic approaches for residential energy management
and provide reference performance levels under identical operating conditions.
4.3.1. Benchmark Energy Management Strategies
Two rule-based energy management strategies are adopted as benchmark methods.
The first baseline is a price-greedy strategy, which adjusts charging and discharging
actions according to the current electricity price. When the electricity price is lower than
a predefined reference level, the battery is charged to store energy for future use. When
electricity prices are relatively high, the battery discharges to meet household demand and
reduce grid electricity purchases. Similarly, the electric vehicle is charged when electricity
prices are low, provided that the vehicle is available at home. This strategy is a typical
price-driven scheduling rule widely used in residential energy management.
The second baseline is a fixed-night charging strategy. In this strategy, the battery and
electric vehicle are charged primarily at night, when electricity prices are generally lower.
During the daytime hours, the battery may discharge to support the household load. This
type of strategy reflects simple time-based scheduling rules commonly used in residential
charging systems and EV home charging devices.
These rule-based strategies are designed without learning or prediction capabilities
and therefore serve as reference methods for evaluating the advantages of the proposed
reinforcement learning-based approach.
4.3.2. Evaluation Metrics
The performance of different energy management strategies is evaluated using several
quantitative metrics that reflect both economic performance and operational reliability.
The primary evaluation metric is the total daily operational cost. To avoid repeating the
objective function, the total cost used for evaluation is calculated according to Equation(20).
It includes the electricity transaction cost, stationary battery degradation cost, EV battery
degradation cost, battery ramping penalty, battery terminal SOC penalty, and EV departure
requirement penalty. A lower total operational cost indicates a more economically efficient
energy management strategy.
In addition to economic performance, the reliability of EV charging is evaluated using
the EV charging success rate. This metric measures the proportion of simulation episodes
in which the EV battery reaches the required target SOC before departure. A higher EV
charging success rate indicates greater satisfaction with the user’s mobility requirements.
To evaluate battery safety, thebattery SOC violation count is also recorded. This metric
counts the number of time steps during which the battery’s SOC exceeds its allowable op-
erating range. A well-designed energy management strategy should avoid such violations
and maintain the battery SOC within its safe operating limits.

Furthermore, the equivalent full cycle (EFC) metric is used to estimate the battery’s
cycling intensity. The EFC value is calculated from the cumulative variation in the battery’s
SOC over the simulation horizon, providing an approximate measure of battery usage and
degradation. Excessive cycling can accelerate battery wear and reduce its useful life.
By combining these evaluation metrics, the performance of different energy man-
agement strategies can be comprehensively assessed in terms of economic efficiency,
operational reliability, and battery usage characteristics. The proposed reinforcement
learning-based method is compared with benchmark strategies in the same simulation
scenarios to demonstrate its effectiveness in coordinating distributed energy resources
within residential energy systems.

5.10. Discussion
The case study results demonstrate that the proposed digital-twin-aided safe MASAC
framework achieves the best overall economic performance while maintaining full opera-
tional feasibility. Over the 35-day testing horizon, the proposed method reduces the total
operating cost from 391.4694 to 365.8245 compared with TD3, corresponding to a 6.55%
reduction. Compared with the rule-based baseline, the cost is reduced from 374.6889 to
365.8245, corresponding to a 2.37% reduction. At the same time, the proposed method
maintains 100% EV departure success, 100% battery terminal SOC success, and 0% grid
violation rate.
The superior performance of the proposed method can be explained by its more
effective use of local renewable energy and battery flexibility. Compared with TD3 and the
rule-based baseline, the proposed controller achieves the lowest grid import and the lowest
grid export. This indicates that it is better at consuming local PV generation within the
household rather than exporting surplus energy to the grid. The higher self-consumption
ratio and self-sufficiency ratio further confirm this advantage.
The comparison with TD3 shows that satisfying constraints alone is not sufficient
to achieve the best economic performance. Although TD3 also achieves 100% constraint
satisfaction, it tends to maintain a high battery SOC and exports more energy to the grid.
This conservative storage behaviour reduces its ability to shift energy across time and leads
to a higher total cost. In contrast, the proposed safe MASAC controller uses the battery
more actively and economically.
The rule-based baseline provides stable and feasible operation, but its fixed control
logic limits its flexibility. It has a lower peak import than the learning-based methods, but it
imports more total electricity and exports more PV energy. This shows that the rule-based
strategy is conservative and easy to implement, but it cannot fully exploit the economic
value of battery storage, EV flexibility, and PV self-consumption.
Overall, the results confirm that the proposed safe MASAC framework can achieve a
better balance between cost minimisation, EV charging reliability, battery utilisation, and
operational safety. The method is particularly effective because it combines learning-based
decision-making, safety-constrained action execution, and adaptive use of distributed
energy resources in a unified residential energy management framework.
6. Conclusions
This paper proposed a digital-twin-aided safe MASAC framework for renewable-
integrated residential energy management. The proposed method coordinates the operation
of PV generation, household load, battery storage, EV charging, and grid power exchange
under time-varying electricity prices and uncertain operating conditions. The digital twin
module was used to maintain an updated virtual representation of the residential energy
system by synchronising operating states, updating degradation-sensitive parameters, and
generating short-term PV/load forecasts for augmented agent observations. Meanwhile, a
safety projection layer was introduced to ensure that the executed control actions satisfy
key physical and operational constraints, including battery SOC limits, EV availability
requirements, and grid power exchange limits.
A 35-day residential case study was conducted to compare the proposed MASAC
controller with TD3 and a rule-based baseline. The results show that the proposed method
achieved the lowest total operating cost while maintaining full operational feasibility.
Specifically, MASAC reduced the total cost by 6.55% compared with TD3 and by 2.37%

compared with the rule-based baseline. All three methods achieved 100% EV departure
success, 100% battery terminal success, and 0% grid violation rate, indicating that the cost
improvement of MASAC was obtained under the same feasibility conditions rather than
through constraint relaxation.
The detailed operational analysis further showed that the proposed MASAC controller
used the battery more actively and improved local renewable energy utilisation. Compared
with the benchmark methods, MASAC achieved lower grid import, lower grid export, a
higher PV self-consumption ratio, and a higher self-sufficiency ratio. These results indicate
that the proposed framework can better exploit the flexibility of residential storage and EV
charging to reduce grid dependence and improve economic performance.
Future work will extend the proposed framework in several directions. First, more
realistic forecasting models, real-world residential datasets, and advanced battery health
estimation models will be incorporated to evaluate the robustness of the controller under
practical uncertainty and long-term degradation. In particular, physics-guided degradation
trajectory prediction and lightweight SOH/RUL estimation models can be integrated into
the digital twin to support more accurate degradation-aware energy management.
