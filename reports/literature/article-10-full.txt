The increasing integration of renewable energy sources into modern power systems has ampliﬁed the need for 
eﬃcient, real-time energy management solutions at the household level. Smart homes, equipped with distributed 
generation units, energy storage systems, electric vehicles and ﬂexible loads, are emerging as active participants 
in decentralized energy networks. However, the intermittent nature of renewable generation, dynamic energy 
pricing, and real-time uncertainties in user behavior pose signiﬁcant challenges for conventional home energy 
management systems. This paper presents a digital twin-assisted, edge-based real-time home energy manage-
ment system designed to optimize energy consumption and operational costs in smart homes. The proposed 
system integrates a real-time digital twin of the home’s energy ecosystem with machine learning-based fore-
casting models and edge computing infrastructure. Long short-term memory networks are employed to predict 
key parameters such as photovoltaic generation, EV availability, and appliance demand, while the digital twin 
continuously updates forecasts and simulations based on real-time data. The proposed framework was evaluated 
through comprehensive simulations using 15-min and 1-min scheduling resolutions. Results demonstrate signif-
icant improvements in forecasting accuracy, operational cost reduction, and system responsiveness compared to 
conventional methods. Speciﬁcally, the proposed framework achieved up to 10% cost savings under a 15-min 
resolution and further improvements with a 1-min resolution, highlighting the beneﬁts of ﬁne-grained, real-time 
control. The synergy between digital twins, edge computing, and machine learning oﬀers a scalable, privacy-
preserving, and responsive energy management framework for future smart homes, contributing to enhanced 
grid resilience and sustainable energy systems.
1.  Introduction
Global primary energy consumption has been steadily increasing, 
reaching approximately 620 exajoules (EJ) in 2023, with electricity de-
mand accounting for nearly one-ﬁfth of total energy use and growing at 
more than 2% annually. Fossil fuels-coal, oil, and natural gas-still sup-
ply about 80% of this energy, leading to escalating carbon emissions 
and environmental degradation. There is still persistence on fossil fuels 
demand, such as coal, in global energy portfolios, particularly in devel-
oping economies [1]. The global increase in energy demand has led to 
higher energy prices and increased environmental concerns, primarily 
due to the rise in carbon emissions from power generation. Integrat-
ing renewable energy sources (RES) with the power grid has emerged 
as a sustainable, environmentally friendly solution that can reduce en-
ergy costs and alleviate environmental pressures, promoting the devel-

In parallel with the rise of distributed energy resources, the con-

and utility providers. The advent of smart homes introduces new oppor-

On the consumer side, eﬀective energy storage and load schedul-
ing are crucial for managing energy consumption and minimizing costs. 
This approach, which involves active participation of consumers and 
utilities in the energy market, contributes to the development of smart 
power grids. A smart grid is an advanced power network that supports 
bidirectional power ﬂow and communication, accommodating renew-
able distributed generation, hybrid generation, distributed energy stor-
age, and controllable loads that function autonomously within a deﬁned 
area [4].
Within this context, home energy management systems (HEMS) 
have emerged as pivotal technologies, acting as the central intelli-
gence of smart homes. HEMS enable homeowners to monitor, sched-
ule, and optimize their energy consumption, helping to reduce oper-
ational costs, improve grid reliability, and maximize renewable energy 
self-consumption. Traditionally, HEMS architectures rely on centralized, 
cloud-based decision-making frameworks. While eﬀective, these sys-
tems are often constrained by latency, bandwidth limitations, and data 
privacy concerns. Furthermore, they struggle to handle the real-time 
uncertainties introduced by renewable generation variability, dynamic 
pricing, and evolving user behavior [5].
The modern energy system is increasingly incorporating innovative 
technologies, which, while enhancing capabilities, also make its struc-
ture more complex. Emerging smart technologies bring advanced com-
puting and communication capacities that cannot be represented in the 
physical layer alone. This complexity requires new approaches to im-
prove information ﬂow and system management. One promising solu-
tion is the digital twin (DT), a concept introduced by Grieves in 2002 for 
product lifecycle management [6]. A DT is a digital model of a physi-
cal entity that mirrors its behavior in real-time through continuous data 
interaction. This technology is particularly beneﬁcial for optimizing the 
management, operation, control, and security of energy systems.
In parallel, edge computing has gained traction as a complemen-
tary technology, enhancing data processing speed, reducing latency, and 
strengthening data privacy by enabling localized decision-making. The 
synergy between DTs and edge computing can signiﬁcantly enhance 
HEMS capabilities, facilitating real-time optimization of energy ﬂows 
within homes equipped with rooftop PV panels, battery storage sys-
tems, electric vehicles (EV), and ﬂexible appliances [7]. Furthermore, 
advancements in artiﬁcial intelligence, IoT, and cyber-physical systems 
have made DTs more relevant, allowing for the real-time collection, pro-
cessing, and analysis of data to create an accurate virtual representation 
of physical systems [8]. This virtual model can be used for real-time 
analysis, control, and decision-making in various industrial sectors, es-
pecially in power systems. Although still in its early stages, DT tech-
nology holds potential in numerous areas of power systems, including 
HEMS.
1.1.  Literature review and research motivation
Traditional HEMS frameworks often rely on centralized cloud-based 
architectures for data processing and decision-making which have been 
the core of many studies [5,9]. While these systems reduce energy costs 
by leveraging dynamic pricing and load scheduling, they face limita-
tions such as latency, bandwidth constraints, and privacy risks due to 
dependency on external servers [10]. Furthermore, conventional HEMS 
struggle to handle real-time uncertainties in RES generation and user 
behavior, which are critical for eﬀective demand-side management.
Driven by advances in IoT, artiﬁcial intelligence, and high-speed 
communication, energy systems are undergoing signiﬁcant digital trans-
formation. Digitalization enables enhanced data acquisition, real-time 
monitoring, and predictive analytics, promoting more resilient and ef-
ﬁcient grid operation. Many researchers have been studying digitaliza-
tion in modern energy sector [11]. Authors in [12–14] have proposed 
deep reinforcement learning approaches for real-time HEMS, achieving 
energy cost reductions through appliance scheduling, but without DT 
integration for scenario testing. Mirshekali et al. [15] review large lan-
guage models (LLMs) in energy systems, including an LLM interface for 
HEMS that enhances user interaction and decision-making, representing 
a novel AI-driven trend in 2025.
Within the digitalization landscape, the integration of DT technol-
ogy with HEMS represents a pivotal advancement in optimizing energy 
usage within smart homes, particularly those equipped with renewable 
energy sources like PV panels, battery storage, and electric vehicles. 
This section reviews recent literature to understand the current state of 
DT applications in HEMS, identify research gaps. The reviewed stud-
ies demonstrate signiﬁcant progress in applying DTs to energy manage-
ment, particularly in smart buildings and grids. DT technology, initially 
conceptualized for industrial applications [16], has gained traction in 
energy systems for its ability to create real-time virtual replicas of physi-
cal assets. DTs enable predictive analytics, scenario testing, and adaptive 
control by integrating IoT data, machine learning (ML), and simulation 
tools [17].
DTs oﬀer versatile applications in energy systems, particularly in en-
hancing forecasting accuracy for loads, system responses, and energy 
management. The eﬃcacy of these forecasts hinges on the availabil-
ity of real-time data, as the reliability of predictions directly correlates 
with the authenticity of input data. Unlike traditional methods reliant 
on historical datasets, DTs leverage real-time data streams, enabling 
more precise predictions. This capability is critical for optimizing long-
term microgrid development, especially in scenarios involving load ex-
pansion and infrastructure scalability. ML algorithms further augment
forecasting by addressing complex prediction and diagnostic challenges. 
For instance, diverse deep learning architectures-such as deep neu-
ral networks (DNNs) [18], Recurrent-DNNs [19], convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs) [20], and long short-term memory (LSTM) [21,22] 
networks-have been deployed across short-, mid-, and long-term fore-
casting horizons for the demand, generation and price in energy systems. 
Integrating these ML models with DT frameworks allows real-time data 
adaptation, signiﬁcantly improving prediction accuracy and operational 
eﬃciency in energy management.
Eﬀective energy management in energy systems necessitates robust 
control systems and precise data acquisition via distributed measure-
ment devices. However, inherent challenges arise from data estima-
tion inaccuracies, which can compromise decision-making. DTs miti-
gate these issues by providing high-ﬁdelity real-time data, thereby en-
hancing the reliability of energy management systems. Successful DT 
implementation requires seamless integration of information technol-
ogy and operational systems, forming a cohesive digital infrastructure. 
Case studies demonstrate DT applications in renewable energy mon-
itoring, wind farm optimization, and energy storage scheduling. Ad-
ditionally, ontologies and semantic web technologies facilitate struc-
tured DT modeling, enabling adaptable simulations for diverse energy 
systems. These advancements underscore the transformative potential 
of DTs in advancing smart grid adaptability and sustainability. Lama-
teou et al. [4] highlight the role of DTs in smart grids but do not ad-
dress their integration with household-level HEMS. In the context of 
smart grids, Saleem et al. [23] integrate IoT with cyber-physical sys-
tems for building energy management, incorporating DTs to improve 
eﬃciency. Carvalhosa et al. [24] assess digital transformation maturity 
in the energy sector using fuzzy logic, highlighting DT-enabled services 
for smart home energy management as a key adoption area. Aman et al. 
[25] discuss energy management strategies but overlook the potential 
of DTs to simulate future scenarios and update control policies dynam-
ically. Authors in [26] explore the integration of DTs with 6G technol-
ogy, highlighting the framework and connectivity requirements essen-
tial for this synergy, present two use cases: DT for home energy man-
agement and DT for open radio access network (O-RAN). Yet, current 
research lacks a holistic approach to embedding these technologies into 
HEMS.

Parallel to the rise of DTs, edge computing has been proposed to ad-
dress latency, privacy, and bandwidth issues in HEMS [27,28]. By shift-
ing computation closer to the household, edge-based HEMS enable faster 
response to local conditions, reduced dependency on cloud servers, and 
enhanced data security. While edge computing has been proposed to en-
hance data privacy and reduce latency, its synergy with DT frameworks 
remains underexplored. Recent advancements in edge computing and 
AI-driven forecasting, have improved the accuracy of load and renew-
able generation predictions [29].
These recent works highlight the rapid evolution toward AI-
enhanced, real-time energy systems but reveal gaps in fully integrated, 
edge-deployed HEMS at high resolutions. Existing studies often use 
coarse time resolutions (e.g., hourly intervals) for energy scheduling, 
which fail to capture rapid ﬂuctuations in PV generation or EV charging 
patterns [5]. Also the applications of DTs in smart homes still remain 
unexplored. This gap limits the ability of HEMS to exploit short-term 
price variations or respond to transient grid conditions. Besides, conven-
tional HEMS lack robust mechanisms to address real-time uncertainties 
in RES generation, EV usage, and dynamic pricing. While LSTM mod-
els improve forecasting, their integration with DTs for continuous error 
correction and adaptive scheduling is limited.
The convergence of DTs and HEMS presents unique opportunities for 
real-time energy optimization in the form of a digital twin-enabled home 
energy management system (DT-HEMS). This paper discusses the grow-
ing importance of DTs in managing energy of smart homes. Real-time 
data on components like home PVs, electric vehicles, and other home 
equipment is essential for active management, and DTs provide a frame-
work for eﬃcient decision-making. The scientiﬁc novelty of this study 
lies in the development of a tightly coupled, real-time DT-HEMS frame-
work that operates at high temporal resolution and is fully synchronized 
with the physical environment through continuous data exchange. Un-
like existing HEMS studies that rely on static forecasting models or of-
ﬂine optimization, our approach integrates a real-time DT deployed at 
the edge, a dynamic LSTM-based forecasting layer, and a dual-loop opti-
mization mechanism capable of responding to minute-level ﬂuctuations 
in renewable generation, load behavior, and user patterns. This combi-
nation enables a level of adaptiveness, accuracy, and responsiveness not 
previously achieved in the literature and demonstrates a practical path-
way for real-time energy management in next-generation smart homes. 
It highlights the importance of DTs in enhancing real-time decision-
making and optimizing the analysis, design, control, and development 
of HEMS. Table overviews a comparison between the current study and 
previous research in the ﬁled. In summary, the following items show the 
contributions of this study:
• Developing a real-time HEMS framework for a grid-connected smart 
home equipped with rooftop PV panels, a battery energy storage sys-
tem, an electric vehicle, and both ﬂexible and non-ﬂexible smart ap-
pliances.
• Employing an edge-computing architecture to enable low-latency 
data processing and real-time decision-making.
• Designing a DT of the smart home to support predictive analysis and 
enhance operational decision-making.
• Integrating an LSTM-based forecasting engine adapted through real-
time DT feedback, for prediction of PV generation, arrival/departure 
times and state of charge of EV, ﬁxed-load consumption, and house-
hold occupancy.

2.  System model
In this study, the focus is on a smart home that is connected to the 
grid for real-time energy optimization within a smart grid system, where 
two-way communication occurs between the user and the grid station. 
The smart home features a hybrid setup consisting of power generation 
sources, a battery as energy storage system (ESS) and an EV, and various 
smart appliances as loads, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The power generation 
sources include the utility grid, photovoltaic (PV) panels, and the ESS 
and EV. The loads are categorized into inﬂexible loads (such as TVs, 
computers, and lights) and ﬂexible loads (such as air conditioning sys-
tems, smart fridge, vacuum cleaner, dish washer, washing machine). The 
HEMS is installed within this grid-connected smart home at the edge of 
the network and is responsible for gathering the following data:
• User preferences regarding load (including operation duration, ar-
rival and departure times of EVs, maximum tolerable delay, etc.), 
home occupancy status from motion sensors, and thermal discom-
fort from thermal sensors;
• Power generation data including PV generation, energy transactions, 
and related constraints;
• The status and constraints of the ESS and EV;

4.  Digital twin implementation
A DT is often seen as an interaction between a physical and a vir-
tual twin, but it actually comprises ﬁve core components as shown in 
Fig. 2: the physical environment, the data environment, the analytical 
environment, the virtual environment, and the communication environ-
ment. The physical twin is measured through the physical environment, 
which is the physical home in our DT-HEMS, while raw data is stored 
in the data environment, i.e. edge of network in our platform. The ana-
lytical environment provides simulations, services, and automation. In 
DT-HEMS, the forecasting and decision making is done in the analytical 
environment. Users typically perceive the DT as a model of the physical 
twin in the virtual environment, often unaware of the other components 
[17,26]. Key characteristics of DTs include [26]:
∙ A comprehensive source of data, including digital models and real-
time data, creating a digital reﬂection of a physical model.

The concept of DTs has evolved with the advent of real-time connec-

can have a virtual counterpart, it is logical to deﬁne, design, and imple-

By outlining these services, appropriate requirements and speciﬁcations 
are established, ultimately enabling the development of the DT.
Based on this approach and the general concept of a DT and its com-
ponents, a DT-HEMS consists of the following: i) physical twin: The 
smart home with its existing infrastructure and assets, including local 
generation, energy storage systems, EVs, grid connection, smart appli-
ances, sensors, and more. ii) digital twin: The virtual counterpart of the 
physical system, continuously adapting based on real-time and histori-
cal measurements through artiﬁcial intelligence or ML algorithms. iii) 
communication infrastructure: standardized connections and protocols 
(such as IEC 60870-5, IEC 61850, and IEEE C37.118-2) facilitate inter-

minimal latency is crucial due to the rapid dynamics of power systems. 
Technologies like cellular network and future 6G, will form the back-
bone of any DT-HEMS. The communication infrastructure is a key com-
ponent that requires thorough analysis and evaluation, which is beyond 
the scope of this document. However, in order to reduce the latency and 
enhance the privacy, it is assumed that the communications are done via 
a local private 6G platform.
The analytical environment in the DT-HEMS, is continuously simu-
lating the energy behavior of the home by predicting the energy demand 
of household appliances, including EVs, smart devices, and refrigerators, 
alongside the generation from rooftop PV panels and utility grid, and 
performs the decision making for the smart home, in order to reduce 
the operational costs.
ML techniques have gained widespread adoption in various fore-
casting applications, with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks 
emerging as a particularly eﬀective tool for time series prediction. LSTM, 
a specialized form of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), was introduced 
by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997 [34]. Unlike conventional 
RNNs, which struggle to retain long-term dependencies due to the van-
ishing gradient problem, LSTM incorporates memory cells that enable it 
to capture and utilize long-term temporal correlations eﬃciently. These 
characteristics make LSTM highly suitable for applications such as load 
forecasting in residential energy systems and the evaluation of smart 
grid parameters [29,35].
The fundamental distinction between LSTM and traditional RNNs 
lies in its ability to selectively store, update, and retrieve information 
over extended sequences, preventing the loss of critical past informa-
tion. Standard RNNs suﬀer from exponentially diminishing or growing 
gradients during backpropagation, which limits their eﬀectiveness in 
learning long-term dependencies. LSTM mitigates this issue through a 
structured memory mechanism consisting of three specialized gates: the 
input gate, forget gate, and output gate.
• Input Gate: Regulates the amount of new information stored in the 
memory cell by determining the relevance of incoming data.
• Forget Gate: Controls the retention of past information by selec-
tively discarding less relevant historical data, allowing the network 
to focus on signiﬁcant long-term dependencies.

• Output Gate: Determines the portion of the memory cell’s current

These gates collectively enable LSTM to process complex tempo-

ties. Unlike a basic RNN cell, which merely propagates the hidden state 
across time steps, an LSTM cell maintains and reﬁnes stored information 
through its gating mechanisms. This structured approach signiﬁcantly 
enhances forecasting accuracy in scenarios requiring real-time decision-
making, such as DT-HEMS. The LSTM-based DT models play a crucial 
role in forecasting energy demand patterns for various household appli-

renewable energy generation. By integrating these predictive insights, 
the DT-HEMS can optimize energy scheduling, reduce operational costs, 
and enhance grid interaction.
By leveraging the DT for proactive decision-making, this approach 
minimizes operational costs, ensures user comfort, and maximizes re-
newable energy utilization, contributing to the development of smarter, 
more resilient, and sustainable energy systems.
A comprehensive review of the LSTM structure and its recent ad-
vancements is provided by Kong et al. [29]. To introduce the core 
concepts of LSTM, we follow a similar notation as presented in [29].

At each time step, the forget gate 𝑓𝑡 determines which parts of the 
previous state 𝑠𝑡−1 should be discarded. The input gate 𝑖𝑡 and candidate

be added to the memory cell. The memory cell state 𝑠𝑡 is then updated 
by combining retained past information and newly processed inputs. 
Finally, the output gate 𝑜𝑡 regulates which parts of the updated cell state 
should be exposed as the hidden state ℎ𝑡, which is passed to the next time 
step. The inner design of the LSTM cell is shown in Fig. 3.
In sequential prediction tasks, autoregressive LSTM models are com-
monly employed to forecast multiple future time steps by recursively 
feeding each predicted value back into the model as input for the next 
prediction. While eﬀective for short-term horizons, this approach suf-
fers from the well-known issue of error accumulation-small prediction 
inaccuracies at each step can propagate and amplify across the fore-
casting window, resulting in a signiﬁcant divergence from actual val-
ues over time. To address this challenge, the integration of a DT oﬀers 
a compelling advantage. A DT serves as a dynamic, real-time replica 
of the physical system, enabling continuous synchronization between 
the virtual and real environments. By incorporating real-time measure-
ments and continuously updating the LSTM model parameters or inputs, 
the DT can correct forecast deviations and signiﬁcantly reduce cumula-
tive errors. This closed-loop feedback mechanism enhances the model’s 
robustness and reliability, particularly in scenarios requiring accurate 
multi-step forecasting.
In this study, an LSTM-based forecasting model is employed for pre-
dicting PV generation, household occupancy, ﬁxed-load demand, and 
EV arrival time and state-of-charge. The choice of LSTM is motivated by 
its well-established ability to capture nonlinear temporal dependencies, 
long-range correlations, and sequential patterns in multivariate time-
series data [30]. These characteristics are essential for the short-term 
forecasting tasks required by the HEMS, where user behavior, weather 
conditions, and appliance usage exhibit strong temporal patterns and 
nonlinear dynamics. Although alternative ML models such as Support 
Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), or feedforward neural 
networks are commonly used for load and generation forecasting, they 
generally lack internal temporal memory and are less eﬀective at model-
ing complex sequential dependencies. Since the goal of this manuscript 
is not to compare forecasting methods but to integrate a reliable predic-
tor into the DT-HEMS framework, LSTM was selected as a suitable and 
widely validated approach. This justiﬁcation has now been clariﬁed in

To evaluate the performance of the proposed DT-HEMS, a compre-
hensive simulation environment was developed. The simulated system 
represents a smart home equipped with various energy resources, in-
cluding a PV system, a battery energy storage system, and an EV charg-
ing system and utility connection. The home also includes a set of smart 
appliances such as a refrigerator, electric oven and stove, air condition-
ing unit (A/C), dishwasher, washing machine, motion sensors, and light-
ing, which dynamically contribute to the overall energy demand.
The DT-HEMS framework integrates real-time forecasting models 
to predict energy consumption, renewable energy generation, EV ar-
rival/departure times, and user comfort-based A/C demand. These pre-
dictions are then utilized to optimize scheduling decisions and minimize 
operational costs while ensuring user comfort.
The energy demand dataset used for training the predictive models 
was collected from historical residential load proﬁles, while renewable 
energy generation patterns were simulated based on real-world weather 
data. The decision-making process operates under 1-min, and 15-min 
scheduling intervals, leveraging DT-driven optimization to enhance sys-
tem eﬃciency beyond human capability. The electricity price is shown 
in Fig. 4 which is obtained from Nordpool data [36]. As the electricity 
price is known for next 24-h, there is no need to perform a forecast-
ing for that. Besides, the simulation parameters of the smart home is 
expressed in Table 2.

enabling ﬁne-grained decision-making for optimal energy scheduling.

energy sources, EV charging/discharging, and smart appliance usage, 
ensuring eﬀective real-time management of home energy resources. Ad-
ditionally, it accounts for the variable energy consumption of smart ap-
pliances, including ﬂexible loads (e.g., dishwasher, washing machine,

5.2.  1-minute time resolution
The proposed DT-HEMS was further evaluated using a 1-min time 
resolution to assess its performance in capturing rapid ﬂuctuations in 
energy dynamics. This ﬁner granularity enables the system to respond 
to minute-by-minute variations in renewable energy generation, and 
smart appliance energy consumption with exceptional precision. The 
1-min resolution is particularly advantageous for real-time energy man-
agement in smart homes, where swift and accurate decision-making is 
critical for optimizing energy usage under dynamic pricing and ﬂuctu-
ating environmental conditions.
The DT’s real-time data integration and continuous model updates 
ensure high forecasting accuracy, as demonstrated in Figs. 9–11 for PV 
generation, EV schedules and home appliances demand predictions. The 
DT enabled prediction, outperforms the non-DT forecasting with mean 
square error of 0.006 to 0.018 in PV prediction, 0.003 to 0.015 in EV 
prediction, and 0.005 to 0.015 in nonﬂexible demand prediction. Com-
paring the results with the Section 5.1, the mean square error in 1 min 
resolution even in the case of without DT, is better of the 15 min reso-
lution with the DT. However, it requires more computational hardware.
The simulation results, illustrated in Fig. 12, highlight the perfor-
mance of the DT-HEMS under a 1-min resolution. The objective func-
tion in this case becomes 3.31 e, which shows about 7% decrease in the 
objective function value of the DT-HEMS under the 15-min resolution 
discussed in Section 5.1. The ﬁner time granularity allows the system to 
exploit short-term variations in PV generation, and load demand with 
greater eﬀectiveness. This improvement stems from the system’s ability 
to leverage short-term ﬂuctuations in energy supply and demand, which 
are often smoothed out in coarser time resolutions. Additionally, the 1-
min resolution enhances user comfort by maintaining tighter control 
over thermal conditions, as the heat pump adjusts its output more fre-
quently to keep indoor temperatures within the desired range. Besides,

6.  Conclusion and future work
This study presented a digital twin-assisted home energy manage-
ment system designed to optimize real-time energy usage in smart 
homes equipped with rooftop PV panels, battery energy storage sys-
tems, electric vehicles, and a mix of ﬂexible and inﬂexible smart appli-
ances. By integrating a digital twin, the proposed system eﬀectively ad-
dresses the challenges posed by the intermittent nature of renewable en-
ergy sources and dynamic energy pricing. The proposed DT-HEMS lever-
ages real-time data processing and edge-computing principles to enable 
precise energy scheduling, cost minimization, and enhanced user com-
