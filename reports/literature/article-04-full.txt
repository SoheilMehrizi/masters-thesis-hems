ABSTRACT
This study focuses on medium‑sized office buildings (8000–12000 m2) that are already in operation and 
equipped with partial sensor data. With the advancement of decarbonization goals in the building sector 
worldwide, there is an urgent need for Energy Performance Certification (EPC) tools to transform from “static 
compliance documents” to “dynamic decision-support platforms.” Traditional EPC systems suffer from three core 
limitations: incomplete whole-building performance evaluation due to data sparsity, inaccurate energy efficiency 
anomaly diagnosis caused by causal ambiguity, and lack of dynamic adaptability in retrofitting optimization 
resulting from static strategies. To address these gaps, this study proposes a data-driven iterative digital twin 
framework—the Self-adaptive, Trustworthy, and Actionable Reality-synchronization (STAR) framework—which 
establishes an iterative technical system of “perception-diagnosis-optimization-data update.” The framework 
integrates three innovative engines: 1) A physics-informed dual-channel spatiotemporal graph sensing engine 
that achieves comprehensive performance virtual metering under sparse data by constructing building spatio-
temporal heterogeneous graphs and embedding thermodynamic constraints; 2) An anomaly root-cause diagnosis 
engine based on graph random walk, which enables precise localization from symptoms to root causes by 
quantifying the contribution of individual nodes to observed anomalies; and 3) A dual-agent collaborative 
evolutionary optimization engine that generates compliant, efficient, and personalized dynamic retrofitting 
roadmaps through the competition and cooperation of exploration-validation agents. Experimental results 
demonstrate that the STAR framework’s spatiotemporal sensing engine achieves a mean absolute percentage 
error (MAPE) of 6.5% under a 60% sensor missing rate and an R2 of 0.92, with significantly mitigated perfor-
mance degradation under data-sparse conditions. Its anomaly diagnosis engine achieves an F1-score greater than 
0.88 across seven anomaly types, a recall rate of 92% for concealed anomalies, and a single-scenario diagnosis 
time of only 0.8 s. The collaborative optimization engine’s Pareto front coverage reaches 0.93, and its generated 
retrofitting schemes yield an average energy saving rate of 22.0% with a payback period of 3.2 years. After seven 
iterative learning cycles, the sensing MAPE further decreases to 5.2%, and the return on investment (ROI) for 
optimization increases to 10.2%. This research provides a technical paradigm for next-generation EPC tools, 
supporting the transition of building energy management from static evaluation to dynamic, interpretable, and 
continuously updated performance optimization, while offering scalable technical support for building

1. Introduction
Building energy consumption accounts for over 30% of global total 
energy use, making it a critical sector for achieving carbon peaking and 
carbon neutrality (dual carbon) goals[1,2]. Decarbonization roadmaps 
for the building sector released by international organizations explicitly 
mandate upgrading energy performance certification tools to drive the 
scaled implementation of energy-saving retrofits. Policy advancements 
in major economies further highlight the urgency of transforming 
Building Energy Performance Certification (EPC) systems[3,4]. As a core 
tool for building energy efficiency compliance, traditional EPC serves a 
foundational role in guiding retrofits and improving energy utilization 
efficiency. However, it suffers from inherent limitations including static 
evaluation, strong lag, and lack of diagnostic capabilities, failing to 
adapt to the dynamic changes in building operating conditions and the 
precise decision-making needs of building owners[5–7]. The industry 
urgently requires next-generation EPC tools characterized by dynamism, 
precision, and decision-enabling capabilities. Digital Twin (DT), through 
its closed-loop architecture integrating physical entities, virtual models, 
and data interaction, enables visualized monitoring, simulation, and 
decision optimization throughout the building’s full lifecycle. Its unique 
advantages in data integration, dynamic mapping, and accurate pre-
diction provide core technical support for overcoming the limitations of 
traditional EPC and constructing dynamic performance evaluation sys-
tems[8,9].
Current research on the integration of dynamic EPC and DT still faces 
three core technical bottlenecks, restricting the large-scale application 
and efficiency improvement of next-generation EPC tools. First, the data 
sparsity bottleneck: Advanced dynamic EPC studies rely on the 
deployment of high-density sensor networks, which incur high hardware 
procurement and operation/maintenance costs, making them difficult to 
popularize in small-to-medium-sized buildings[10]. Mainstream data- 
driven building energy modeling methods, lacking physical constraint 
integration, exhibit a sharp degradation in generalization performance 
under sparse data scenarios (sensor missing rate > 50%), with prediction 
errors exceeding 20%, failing to achieve accurate whole-building per-
formance evaluation[11,12]. Second, the diagnostic causality bottle-
neck: Cutting-edge building fault diagnosis research mostly remains at 
the phenomenon identification level, only capable of detecting anoma-
lies through energy consumption deviations from baseline values. It 
cannot quantify the contribution degree of potential root causes (e.g., 
equipment malfunctions, envelope failures)[13], and lacks causal 
interpretability, making it difficult to support targeted retrofitting de-
cisions and leading to suboptimal outcomes despite investment in 
energy-saving measures[14]. Third, the strategy staticity bottleneck: 
Existing building retrofitting optimization schemes are predominantly 
static, designed as fixed solutions based on current building conditions 
and climate parameters without considering the dynamic evolution of 
usage scenarios and climate conditions[15]. Additionally, most schemes 
focus on a single energy-saving objective, failing to balance multi- 
objective hard constraints such as cost, comfort, and compliance, 
resulting in low implementation feasibility and poor long-term energy- 
saving effects[16,17].
Notably, the concepts of data-driven iterative updating and adapt-
ability in DT and system control fields primarily focus on adaptive up-
dates of model parameters—optimizing model weights through real- 
time data iteration to improve prediction accuracy—without involving 
dynamic adjustments to decision-making strategies. The “ data-driven 
iterative updating” proposed in this study specifically refers to a two- 
dimensional iterative evolution encompassing data-driven model itera-
tive upgrading and scenario-adaptive strategy adjustment. Its core lies in 
realizing a transformation from passive response to proactive prediction 
and dynamic optimization in building energy management through a 
iterative process: sensing data update → model parameter optimization 
→ dynamic strategy adjustment → implementation effect feedback. This 
approach precisely adapts to the dynamic characteristics of the build-
ing’s full lifecycle.
To address the aforementioned research gaps, this study proposes a 
data-driven iterative digital twin framework integrating virtual sensing 
and anomaly diagnosis, aiming to overcome the three core limitations of 
traditional EPC (data sparsity, difficulty in root cause identification, and 
static strategies) and provide a practical technical solution for next- 
generation EPC. The core contributions of this paper are summarized 
as follows: 
• Theoretical contribution: Proposes the STAR data-driven iterative 
digital twin theoretical framework, defines the iterative logic of 
“perception-diagnosis-optimization-data update,” and establishes 
the core paradigm of dynamic perception, precise diagnosis, dy-
namic optimization, and continuous iteration for building energy 
performance, laying a theoretical foundation for the development of 
next-generation dynamic EPC.
• Technical contribution 1: Develops a physics-informed dual-channel 
spatiotemporal graph sensing engine. By constructing building 
spatiotemporal heterogeneous graphs and embedding thermody-
namic constraints, it solves the problem of high-precision virtual 
metering for comprehensive building performance under sparse data 
scenarios.
• Technical contribution 2: Designs an anomaly contribution 
quantification-based diagnosis mechanism using graph random 
walk. Through a reverse random walk algorithm that inverts causal 
directions, it quantifies the contribution degree of each root cause 
node to anomalies, enabling interpretable localization of energy ef-
ficiency anomalies from phenomena to root causes.
• Technical contribution 3: Proposes a dual-agent collaborative 
evolutionary optimization strategy. Leveraging the competition and 
cooperation mechanism between exploration and validation agents, 
it generates personalized dynamic retrofitting roadmaps under 
multi-objective constraints.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 re-
views research progress in relevant fields, including EPC evolution, DT 
applications, graph neural network modeling, building fault diagnosis, 
and optimization, to clarify research gaps. Section 3 elaborates on the 
formal definition, overall architecture of the STAR framework, and 
technical details of the three core engines. Section 4 introduces the 
experimental design, data preparation, benchmark models, and evalu-
ation metrics of the case study. Section 5 presents the experimental re-
sults, focusing on comparing the performance of the STAR framework 
with state-of-the-art methods, followed by in-depth discussion. Section 6
summarizes the research conclusions, analyzes limitations, and outlines 
future work.The application scope of this study is limited to typical of-
fice buildings with existing partial monitoring data (8000–12000 m2). 
The applicability to new buildings without historical data or to very 
large campuses requires additional investigation and is not claimed in 
this paper.

To overcome static limitations, dynamic EPC has emerged as a 
research hotspot in recent years, with its core objective of integrating 
real-time data to achieve dynamic updates of energy efficiency assess-
ments. Two cutting-edge approaches have been formed: First, deploying 
high-density sensor networks to collect real-time data and revising en-
ergy efficiency ratings using simplified physical models. Qu et al. [24]
achieved monthly updates with an 18% accuracy improvement but 
failed to address cost constraints. Second, introducing machine learning 
models for short-term energy consumption prediction to enable quar-
terly iterations. For instance,Jin et al. [25] proposed an LSTM model to 
dynamically update, which is prone to accuracy degradation under 
extreme climate conditions. Neither approach resolves the cost bottle-
neck of high-density sensing, making large-scale promotion impractical. 
Furthermore, they lack anomaly diagnosis and optimization capabilities, 
remaining at the “assessment” level without achieving the essential 
transformation to “decision empowerment.”.
To better position our research, it is essential to examine the distinct 
research trajectories across different regions. Europe, as a global leader 
in building energy policy, has conducted extensive work on improving 
the reliability, consistency, and comparability of EPCs, particularly 
under the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) frame-
work. For instance, Cozza et al. [26] systematically evaluated whether 
EPCs allow reliable building-to-building energy performance compari-
sons, revealing significant inconsistencies arising from different calcu-
lation methodologies and assessor behaviors. Pasichnyi et al. [27]
demonstrated new opportunities for data-enabled urban energy gover-
nance using EPC databases, yet highlighted the lack of dynamic feed-
back mechanisms. The latest EPBD revision (2024) has introduced 
requirements for smart readiness indicators and dynamic EPCs, pushing 
the agenda toward real-time, occupant-centric certification [28].
To further contextualize recent European developments, we 
reviewed the EU-funded next-generation EPC sister projects listed by the 
U-CERT initiative [29]. These projects indicate that EPC research in 
Europe is moving from static compliance certificates toward more dy-
namic, user-centred, data-informed, and renovation-oriented perfor-
mance assessment systems. As summarized in Table 1, QualDeEPC, U- 
CERT, X-tendo, D2EPC, E-DYCE, ePANACEA, EPC RECAST, crossCert, 
EUB SuperHub, iBRoad2EPC, and TIMEPAC have contributed to EPC 
reliability and comparability, operational data integration, digital EPC 
tools, building logbooks, smart readiness and comfort indicators, reno-
vation passports, and cross-country quality assurance. However, these 
initiatives mainly focus on certification design, data infrastructure, in-
dicator extension, user interaction, and renovation planning. The inte-
gration of sparse-data virtual sensing, causal anomaly diagnosis, and 
adaptive retrofit optimization remains insufficiently addressed, which 
motivates the STAR framework proposed in this study.

systematically evaluated the accuracy of automated M&V methods for 
commercial building energy savings, demonstrating the potential for 
real-time performance tracking. Kontokosta [32] proposed a knowledge

European EPC initiatives have substantially advanced certification

2.2. Applications of digital twin in building energy management
DT, through its closed-loop architecture of “physical entity-virtual 
model-data interaction,” provides a novel technical pathway for en-
ergy management[36,37]. The core of building DT construction lies in 
high-precision real-time mapping between physical and virtual models. 
The mainstream paradigm adopts “lightweight Building Information 
Modeling (BIM) + real-time data access + multi-physics coupling 
simulation”[38], with recent research focusing on cost reduction and 
precision improvement. For example, Zhang et al. [39] proposed a rapid 
BIM modeling method integrating point cloud scanning and deep 
learning, shortening the modeling cycle by 40%, but its accuracy is 
insufficient for complex irregular buildings. Baghalzadeh et al. [40]
addresses the issue of temporal asynchrony through BIM-DT-based 
construction process optimization, but it does not incorporate physical 
law constraints.
DT has been applied in scenarios such as energy consumption 
monitoring, load forecasting, and equipment control, with proven value: 
Zhang et al. [41] achieved real-time optimization of air conditioning 
systems in office buildings, yielding a 12% energy saving rate, but did 
not cover the entire building. de las Morenas et al. [42] developed a DT 
platform for commercial buildings, improving operation and mainte-
nance (O&M) efficiency by 25% yet lacking diagnostic and retrofitting 
scheme functions. Existing research still exhibits significant shortcom-
ings: Most studies remain at the “digital mirroring” level, lacking inte-
grated capabilities for “diagnosis-optimization-feedback”; The 
“lightweight BIM + simplified simulation” architecture results in 
insufficient model accuracy and physical consistency, reducing reli-
ability under complex operating conditions; The integration of data- 
driven approaches with physical mechanisms is inadequate, leading to 
a sharp decline in generalization performance under sparse data sce-
narios. Naeem et al. [43] have confirmed that when the sensor missing 
rate exceeds 30%, the prediction error of pure data-driven models rises 
to over 15%.
2.3. Applications of graph Neural Networks in building spatiotemporal 
modeling
Building systems feature complex topological correlations and 
exhibit typical non-Euclidean structures, making it difficult for tradi-
tional deep learning models to capture spatial dependencies. Graph 
Neural Networks (GNNs) are naturally suited for non-Euclidean struc-
tured data, as they can model topological relationships between ele-
ments through node and edge definitions. Combined with temporal 
modules, GNNs enable spatiotemporal joint modeling, achieving a 10%- 
20% accuracy improvement compared to Convolutional Neural Net-
works (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTMs)[44,45]. Zhuet al. 
[46] verifies its advantage in regional energy consumption prediction 
through experiments conducted on 10 different types of energy systems.
Recent GNN-based building modeling research focuses on two main 
directions: First, energy consumption prediction. Zhuang et al. [47]
proposed Spatiotemporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCNs), 
realizing accurate zonal energy consumption prediction via room to-
pology graphs with a 15% error reduction compared to LSTMs, but 
without considering thermodynamic constraints. Xu et al. [48] modeled 
energy transfer relationships between equipment using Graph Attention 
Networks (GATs) to achieve short-term overall energy consumption 
prediction, yet failed to dynamically adjust graph structures. Second, 
equipment fault detection. Wu et al. [49] constructed topological graphs 
of equipment parameters to identify early faults in core equipment but 
could not capture collaborative anomalies across multiple devices. Core 
limitations of existing research include: Pure data-driven approaches 
lack physical law constraints, resulting in poor interpretability and 
insufficient generalization under sparse data—prediction errors increase 
significantly when the sensor missing rate exceeds 50% Meng et al. [50]; 
The absence of multi-task collaboration capabilities for “perception- 
diagnosis”; Graph structures do not account for dynamic spatiotemporal 
correlations, leading to over 20% accuracy degradation during sudden 
scenario changes[51].
2.4. Summary of research gaps and research focus of This study
Synthesizing recent cutting-edge research, the current core technical 
gap lies in the fact that existing achievements can only address data 
sparsity, causal diagnosis, or static optimization individually. They fail 
to form a collaborative iterative process of “perception-diagnosis-opti-
mization-evolution,” thus unable to meet the “dynamism, precision, and 
empowerment” requirements of next-generation EPC. Specifically: Dy-
namic EPC has not resolved comprehensive perception under sparse 
data; Building DT remains at the mirroring and monitoring level; GNN 
modeling lacks physical integration and multi-task collaboration; En-
ergy efficiency diagnosis and optimization have not achieved causal 
root-cause identification and dynamic adaptation, nor deep integration 
with certification systems.

There is an urgent need in the field for a data-driven iterative 
framework capable of simultaneously overcoming the three major 
challenges of “sparse perception, causal diagnosis, and dynamic opti-
mization”—such a framework has not been reported to date. Targeting 
this gap, this study proposes a self-evolving digital twin framework 
integrating virtual sensing and anomaly diagnosis (STAR framework), 
with three core innovative breakthroughs: 
1. A physics-informed dual-channel spatiotemporal graph sensing en-
gine, which solves the problem of high-precision whole-building 
performance metering under sparse data and compensates for the 
insufficient integration of physical laws in existing research;
2. An anomaly root-cause diagnosis mechanism based on graph random 
walk, enabling causal root-cause localization of energy efficiency 
anomalies and breaking the black-box limitations of data-driven 
methods in existing research;
3. A dual-agent collaborative evolutionary optimization strategy, 
generating dynamically adaptive personalized retrofitting schemes 
to address the poor long-term effectiveness of static schemes in 
existing research.
In relation to the EU-funded next-generation EPC projects reviewed 
in Section 2.1, the STAR framework is not intended to replace existing 
EPC schemes or certification procedures. Rather, it is positioned as a 
methodological and algorithmic layer that can complement these ini-
tiatives. While projects such as U-CERT, X-tendo, D2EPC, ePANACEA, 
EPC RECAST, crossCert, EUB SuperHub, iBRoad2EPC, and TIMEPAC 
have advanced EPC reliability, digitalization, operational data integra-
tion, building logbooks, renovation passports, user-centered recom-
mendations, and cross-country quality assurance, the technical 
integration of sparse-data virtual sensing, causal root-cause diagnosis, 
and adaptive retrofit optimization remains insufficiently addressed. The 
STAR framework addresses this gap by providing an iterative 
perception-diagnosis-optimization-data update mechanism, thereby of-
fering technical support for dynamic, interpretable, and actionable EPC 
systems.

3.2. Overall framework architecture and iterative logic
The STAR framework is a dynamic iterative system integrating vir-
tual sensing and anomaly diagnosis. Its architecture comprises three 
core engines—spatiotemporal sensing, anomaly root-cause diagnosis, 
and collaborative evolutionary optimization—a data middle platform, 
and a BIM-based virtual twin.
The data middle platform integrates and preprocesses multi-source 
heterogeneous data (BIM, real-time sensors, meteorology), providing 
unified data support for the three engines. The virtual twin digitally 
maps the physical building and visualizes perception, diagnosis, and 
optimization results.
The operational logic follows a iterative cycle: perception → diag-
nosis → optimization → implementation → re-perception. The spatio-
temporal sensing engine generates a holographic energy performance 
profile. Based on this profile, the diagnosis engine identifies anomalies 
and quantifies root-cause contributions. The optimization engine then 
produces dynamic retrofitting roadmaps. After implementation, opera-
tional data feedback to the data middle platform, updating the virtual 
twin and driving iterative refinement of model parameters and strate-
gies. This iterative process ensures dynamic adaptability and sustained 
EPC accuracy.

3.4. Anomaly Root-Cause tracing and diagnosis Engine: Anomaly

Anomalies in building systems propagate along physical links.

3.5. Collaborative evolutionary optimization Engine: Dual-Agent Multi- 
Objective collaborative evolution strategy
To address high-dimensional solution space, multi-objective con-
flicts, and the decoupling of performance and feasibility in building 
retrofitting optimization, this section proposes a dual-agent collabora-
tive evolutionary optimization engine. The exploration agent ensures 
solution space coverage and diversity; the validation agent balances 
evaluation accuracy and efficiency. Through a shared database and 
Pareto front evolution, the engine achieves convergence with simulta-
neous improvements in solution performance and feasibility. The overall 
mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 5.
Before describing the dual-agent interaction, the space of possible 
retrofitting measures and how feasible combinations are represented as 
a graph is first defined.
The solution space is defined by a building retrofitting measure 
graph. Retrofitting measures are classified into four categories: envelope 
(e.g., insulation, windows), equipment (e.g., HVAC, lighting), intelligent 
control (e.g., smart thermostats), and renewable energy (e.g., PV, heat 
pumps). Each measure has multi-dimensional attributes: cost, energy 
saving rate, implementation cycle, service life, compliance, compati-
bility, and constraints.
Based on these, an undirected graph Gmeasure=(Vmeasure,Emeasure, 
Ameasure) is constructed, where Vmeasure are measure nodes, Emeasure =
Ecompatible∪ Econflict, and Ameasure the attribute matrix.A solution is a subset 
X={x1,x2,…,xm} with xi∈{0,1} indicating selection of measure i, subject 
to budget, cycle, compliance, and compatibility constraints.With the 
solution space defined, the two agents that work together to explore and 
evaluate candidate combinations are now introduced.
Exploration agent (based on PPO). Its goal is to efficiently search 
for novel, feasible measure combinations. The state space is S={Xcurrent, 
Ereal  time,Budget,Cycle,Policy}, where Ereal  time comes from the sensing 
engine. The action space includes add, replace, and remove. The reward 
function fuses multiple objectives: 
r= α⋅Energy_saving-β⋅Cost+ γ⋅Compliance-δ⋅Conflict (17) 
where the weight coefficients α,β,γ,δ are determined by AHP (see 
Appendix A). Training uses the PPO clipped surrogate objective. 
Hyperparameters (iterations, sample size, etc.) are listed in Appendix A.
Validation agent (lightweight digital twin simulator). It evalu-
ates schemes generated by the exploration agent. The simulator sim-
plifies the spatiotemporal sensing engine by: (1) freezing feature 
extraction and using low-dimensional mean features; (2) replacing 
finite-element simulations with empirical formulas; (3) employing GPU 
parallel computing. It assesses multi-objective performance (energy 
saving rate, ROI, payback period, PMV-based comfort), compliance 
(energy, environmental, safety standards), and feasibility (imple-
mentation difficulty, operational impact). Detailed evaluation criteria 
and thresholds are provided in Appendix A.
The exploration and validation agents do not operate in isolation; 
they continuously exchange information to drive the population toward 
better and more feasible solutions. This collaborative mechanism is 
detailed next. The collaboration involves competition (threshold-based

Screening by the validation agent: Only schemes meeting preset

Pareto optimal set screening: The final population is processed by 
NSGA-III to obtain the Pareto front, offering multiple non-dominated

following “easy first, fast first”: 
• Short-term (≤1 year): Low-investment, quick-return measures with

• Medium-term (1–3 years): Moderate investment, longer 
implementation.
• Long-term (3–5 years): High-investment, large-scale retrofits.
Each phase specifies measures, timing, cost, expected savings, 
responsible parties, and acceptance criteria. To suppress equipment 
oscillations such as frequent HVAC start‑stop or valve hunting that may 
arise from high‑frequency data‑driven strategy updates in real build-
ings, an interlock and dead‑zone module is explicitly placed between the 
optimization engine and the physical execution. The dead‑zone 
threshold is set to 1%, meaning that a new strategy is adopted only when 
its estimated absolute improvement in energy saving rate exceeds this 
threshold. The interlock conditions further require that the strategy does 
not violate equipment minimum run‑time, safety limits, or operational 
mode exclusivity, following ASHRAE Guideline 36. In the offline replay 
simulation, about 12% of minor strategy adjustments are filtered out for 
falling below the dead‑zone, while long‑term convergence remains 
unaffected. This design bridges data‑driven decisions and physical 
equipment execution, significantly enhancing the framework’s deploy-
ability in real buildings. Dynamic adjustment: The roadmap is updated 
when triggered by significant changes in building operation, external 
conditions, or failure to meet expected effects. The engine then re-
generates optimized schemes based on the latest perception, diagnosis, 
and input data.Thus, the optimization engine completes the iterative 
process: from perception and diagnosis to actionable, adaptive retrofit-
ting strategies.
4. Case studies and experimental design
To ensure the generality and practicality of the research, a dual-case 
model combining “public dataset + real building” is adopted for case 
selection, balancing the universality of method validation and the 
applicability of engineering applications. For the public dataset, the 
ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III (GEP III) is selected, with three office 
buildings of different scales as research objects (building areas: 8000 m2, 
10000 m2, and 12000 m2). The dataset covers hourly energy con-
sumption (electricity, water, natural gas), indoor temperature, and 
outdoor meteorological data from 2021 to 2024, with sensors only 
deployed in public areas and core equipment rooms—resulting in a 
sensor missing rate exceeding 60% in rooms, which aligns with the 
validation requirements of sparse data scenarios. For the real case, a 
central urban office building constructed in 2018 is chosen: it has a 
building area of 12000 m2, 10 above-ground floors, and adopts a central 
air-conditioning system, with sensors only installed on the 1st, 5th, and 
10th floors. The building exhibits significant excessive energy con-
sumption, and the owner has retrofitting demands. Multi-source data 
collection has been completed, including building physical parameters 
extracted from the BIM model, hourly sensor data from 2022 to 2023, 
synchronous meteorological data, occupancy density data, and energy 
bills—providing comprehensive data support for the practical applica-
tion verification of the framework.

comprehensive spatiotemporal and physical information for model

Dataset splitting strictly follows chronological order to avoid data

70%/15%/15% for the training, validation, and test

missing rate scenarios (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%) are constructed in the test

simulated by randomly removing sensor data, comprehensively evalu-

We designed an offline iterative data replay simulation using the 
ASHRAE GEP III dataset from 2021 to 2024 to verify performance 
improvement under accumulating data without physical retrofitting. As 
illustrated in Fig. 10, the simulation starts with 2021 data only: we train 
the spatiotemporal sensing engine to generate virtual metering, di-
agnose anomalies, and output an optimisation strategy such as HVAC 
setpoint adjustments or lighting schedules. That strategy is then applied 
as rule‑based modifications to the weather and load data of 2022, 
computing the resulting energy consumption and indoor conditions as if 
the strategy had been implemented. The modified 2022 data, which 
implicitly carry the strategy’s effect, are used to retrain the engine 
before moving to 2023. Repeating this process for 2023 and 2024 
completes seven rounds in total, with each year’s data used once. In each 
round we record perception MAPE, diagnostic F1‑score, and optimised 
ROI. This simulation is strictly data‑driven and does not represent a 
physical closed‑loop deployment. Real‑world factors such as imple-
mentation delays, measurement noise, and occupant behaviour changes 
are not captured and remain future work.

5.1. Performance analysis of the spatiotemporal sensing engine
To evaluate the spatiotemporal sensing engine under varying sensor 
missing rates, a comparative experiment was conducted against baseline 
models including EnergyPlus, CNN-LSTM, STGCN, GAT, and PINN. As 
shown in Table 4, the STAR engine consistently outperforms all baseline 
models, with significantly slower performance degradation as the 
missing rate increases. Under the representative sparse scenario of 60% 
missing rate, STAR achieves a MAPE of 6.5% (8.2% lower than PINN), a 
CV(RMSE) of 8.3% (12.6% lower than PINN), and an R2 of 0.92 (8.2% 
higher than PINN). Furthermore, the reduced CV(RMSE) directly im-
proves optimization decision accuracy. For instance, under the same 
60% missing rate, a 7.5% reduction in CV(RMSE) lowers the ROI 
calculation error of subsequent optimization schemes from 5.2% to 
3.1%, confirming the practical value of the engine’s performance.
To assess the reproducibility and significance of the results, all 
perception experiments were repeated 10 times using different random 
seeds or bootstrap resampling. Table 3 reports the mean ± standard 
deviation for each metric. At the representative sparse scenario of 60% 
sensor missing rate, STAR achieves a mean MAPE of 6.5% with a 
standard deviation of only 0.3%, while the best baseline (PINN) yields 
7.8 ± 0.5%. The improvement is consistent across all 10 runs, with STAR 
always outperforming PINN. Paired t‑tests between STAR and each

statistical significance. Fig. 11 shows the boxplot of MAPE at 60% 
missing rate, where STAR exhibits the lowest median, the narrowest 
interquartile range, and no outliers, whereas all baselines show broader 
distributions and higher medians. This robust statistical evidence sup-
ports the conclusion that STAR’s sensing engine provides not only 
higher accuracy but also superior stability under sparse data conditions.

5.2. Performance analysis of the anomaly Root-Cause tracing and 
diagnosis engine
To evaluate diagnostic accuracy, seven anomaly scenarios were 
constructed using controlled parameter injection. These included HVAC 
malfunction, external wall insulation damage, power fluctuation, 
insufficient ventilation, envelope heat leakage, lighting system over-
load, and fresh air system failure. All scenarios were based on real 
building normal operation data. For instance, HVAC malfunction was 
simulated by reducing compressor power to 60–70% of rated value. 
Envelope heat leakage was emulated by increasing door or window gap 
heat transfer coefficients by 50–60%. Fresh air system failure was 
implemented by interrupting supply or reducing heat exchange effi-
ciency below 40%. A mixed normal-anomaly test set was created, and 
diagnostic performance was quantified on a 1.0–5.0 hierarchical scale, 
where Level 5 represents optimal accurate diagnosis and Level 1 the 
worst or diagnostic failure.
Fig. 15 presents the confusion matrices. For STGCN (subfigure a) and 
GAT (b), most anomalies score below 3.0, with prominent misjudgment. 
PINN (c) improves diagonal performance to 3.5–4.2, but the concealed 
anomaly diagnostic level remains 4.6 with residual off‑diagonal inter-
ference. In contrast, STAR (d) shows darkest diagonal cells and 
near‑zero off‑diagonal values, indicating almost no misjudgment across 
the seven anomaly types. Quantitatively, STAR achieves an F1‑score >
0.88 for the seven idealised scenarios, with a recall of 92% for concealed 
anomalies (vs. 75% for STGCN). Under more challenging conditions 
(composite faults and gradual evolution), the F1‑score remains at 
0.84–0.85, as detailed in the sensitivity analysis below. The false alarm 
rate on normal data is 2.8%. These results, together with the paired 
t‑test (p < 0.01) and Cohen’s d = 1.23, confirm the statistical signifi-
cance and practical relevance of the diagnostic engine.

To evaluate whether the interlock and dead-zone module compro-
mises optimisation quality, we conducted a controlled comparison. Two 
configurations of the optimisation engine were tested. The first config-
uration included the module with a dead-zone threshold of 1% and 
interlock conditions following ASHRAE Guideline 36. The second 
configuration omitted the module entirely. Both were run on the same 
seven-round offline replay simulation using identical random seeds and 
input data. For each configuration we recorded the Pareto Coverage, the 
average energy saving rate, and the proportion of strategy updates 
filtered by the module.
As shown in Table 11, the module filtered out about 12% of the 
candidate strategy updates because their estimated net benefit fell below 
the 1% dead-zone threshold. However, the Pareto Coverage decreased 
only from 0.93 to 0.92 (a relative drop of 1.1%), and the average energy 
saving rate decreased from 22.1% to 21.9% (a relative drop of 0.9%). 
Both changes are well below 2%, confirming that the interlock and dead- 
zone module does not compromise optimisation quality. At the same 
time, it effectively suppresses meaningless high-frequency strategy ad-
justments, thereby enhancing the deployability of the framework in real 
buildings.

5.5. Comprehensive discussion
The STAR framework upgrades the core function of digital twins 
from traditional “physical entity mirror simulation” to “iterative deci-
sion evolution of perception-diagnosis-optimization,” realizing the 
collaborative integration of full-chain components for the first time and 
further enriching the theoretical system of dynamic EPC. In terms of 
physics-informed graph learning, the proposed “dual-channel + phys-
ical constraints” method primarily addresses the generalization bottle-
neck of pure data-driven Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in sparse and 
unbalanced building data scenarios, rather than merely improving 
model accuracy. Regarding the data-driven iterative updating mecha-
nism, the defined two-dimensional logic of “model iteration + strategy 
adjustment” is more aligned with the dynamic characteristics of the 
building’s full lifecycle, providing core theoretical support for the 
practical implementation of dynamic EPC. This contribution is also 
consistent with the evolution of EU-funded next-generation EPC initia-
tives discussed in Section 2.1. These initiatives have established 
important directions for digital, user-centered, and renovation-oriented 
EPCs, whereas STAR further contributes an operational technical loop 
that transforms incomplete monitoring data into virtual performance 
evidence, interpretable diagnostic outputs, and adaptive retrofit strate-
gies. In this sense, STAR can be regarded as a technical enabler for dy-
namic and actionable EPC implementation rather than as a separate 
certification scheme.
The practical value of the STAR framework is reflected in clear 
technical implementation paths and diverse industrial benefits: At the 
technical level, it can achieve data interoperability through integration 
with BIM platforms (e.g., Revit) and Building Energy Management 
Systems (BEMS), and complete the implementation connection from 
model to actual system through a “cloud-based training + edge-based

First, its adaptability needs to be improved in scenarios with insufficient

optimization process has not met practical requirements in super-large 
campus-scale building scenarios; third, current physical constraints do 
not cover the complex control logic of electromechanical systems, 
limiting model refinement. Corresponding future work will focus on 
these directions: First, explore the combination of transfer learning and 
meta-learning to reduce the initial data demand for new buildings by 
50%; second, develop hierarchical distributed optimization algorithms 
to control campus-scale optimization time within 30 min and improve 
real-time performance; third, integrate mechanistic models of electro-
mechanical systems to enhance the comprehensiveness of physical 
constraints, further improving model accuracy and applicability.
6. Conclusions
Addressing the core pain points of traditional EPC—data sparsity, 
difficulty in tracing anomaly root causes, and static rigidity of optimi-
zation strategies—this study proposes a data-driven iterative digital 
twin framework (STAR) integrating virtual sensing and anomaly diag-
nosis. It constructs a full-chain iterative technical system of “perception- 
diagnosis-optimization-data update,” breaking through the limitation of 
existing research focusing on single functional modules and realizing the 
leap from static assessment to dynamic data-driven iterative optimiza-
tion for EPC.
Experimental results show that: The STAR spatiotemporal sensing 
engine achieves a MAPE of 6.5% (8.2% lower than PINN), an R2 of 0.92, 
and a CV (RMSE) 12.6% lower than PINN under 60% sensor missing 
rate, effectively supporting subsequent optimization decisions; its 
anomaly diagnosis engine achieves an F1-score > 0.88 for 7 types of 
energy use anomalies, a recall rate of 92% for concealed anomalies, a 
single-scenario diagnosis time of 0.8 s (0.7 s shorter than GCN), and a 
cross-scenario F1-score coefficient of variation of only 3.2%; the 
collaborative optimization engine achieves a Pareto front Coverage of

The STAR framework provides a new technical paradigm of “accu-
rate perception, interpretable diagnosis, dynamic optimization, and 
continuous updating” for next-generation EPC, enriching the theoretical 
system and methodology at the intersection of building digital twins and 
physics-informed learning. Its standardized integration scheme with 
existing commercial systems such as BIM and BEMS enhances technical 
implementation feasibility, providing precise support for building 
energy-saving retrofitting decisions and facilitating the achievement of 
decarbonization goals in the global building industry. Meanwhile, the 
framework’s design concepts of “mechanism-data fusion” and “iterative 
self-updating” offer reusable theoretical references and technical in-
sights for performance optimization and dynamic certification of similar 
complex industrial systems.
Future research will focus on the integration of transfer learning and 
meta-learning, the development of hierarchical distributed optimization 
algorithms, and the construction of a mechanistic model library for 
electromechanical systems. This will further improve the framework’s 
adaptability to complex scenarios such as new buildings and super-large 
building campuses, promote its large-scale application in full-lifecycle 
building energy management, and provide more comprehensive tech-
nical support for achieving carbon neutrality in the building sector. This 
study did not deploy physical retrofitting in a real building or track the 
actual measurement results. The seven rounds of iteration described 
above are based on offline replay simulations using historical data and 
serve solely to verify the framework’s performance evolution trend 
under accumulating data. Practical factors such as strategy execution 
delays, measurement noise, and occupant behavior changes are not 
incorporated into the current model; these remain key issues to be 
addressed in future engineering deployment. The validation on ASHRAE 
RP-1043, while positive, also shows lower F1‑scores than on simulated 
building‑level anomalies. This discrepancy arises because the dataset 
lacks building‑scale spatial structure, forcing us to use a degenerate 
graph with a single node. Future work will focus on mapping devi-
ce‑level fault data onto full building graphs, possibly through transfer 
learning or feature alignment techniques.
Additional limitations and corresponding future work are discussed 
below. 
• Data dependency for new buildings: The framework requires several 
weeks of historical data for stable training. For new buildings 
without such data, performance degrades. As shown in Appendix B, 
Table B.1, a transfer learning experiment using two ASHRAE build-
ings reduced MAPE from 12.1% (direct application) to 9.0% after 
fine‑tuning with only one week of data, closing about half the gap to 
the full‑data baseline of 6.5%. Future work will integrate advanced 
transfer learning and meta‑learning to reduce the requirement to one 
week or less.
• Computational scaling for large campuses: For buildings up to 1000 
nodes, inference time is below 0.5 s, meeting real‑time requirements. 
For larger campuses, the O(N2F) complexity becomes a bottleneck. 
Appendix B, Fig. B.1 shows a hierarchical optimisation strategy on 
100–500 node graphs, reducing computation time by 45–68% with a 
relative loss in energy saving rate below 5%. For a 500‑node campus, 
time drops from about 78 s to 25 s, making online re‑optimisation 
feasible. Future work will develop scalable hierarchical distributed 
algorithms for ultra‑large scenarios.
• Electromechanical control logic modelling: Current physical con-
straints cover heat balance and energy conservation but not detailed 
control logic such as chiller sequencing or VAV minimum air flow. To 
demonstrate extensibility, a VAV flow bound constraint was added to 
the loss function. Appendix B, Fig. B.2 shows that this constraint 
reduces MAPE by 0.2–0.4 percentage points and cuts flow violation 
residuals by 50–60% across three floors with different VAV densities, 
confirming that additional domain‑specific physical constraints can 
be integrated. More complex logics, such as chiller start‑stop se-
quences, are left for future work.
• Real-World Deployment and Validation: This study did not deploy 
physical retrofitting in a real building or track post-implementation 
measurement. All iterative validation is based on offline historical 
data replay simulation. Practical factors such as strategy execution 
delays, measurement noise, and occupant behaviour changes are not 
incorporated. A long-term field test with a partner building is plan-
ned for future work. Additionally, the parameters of the interlock 
and dead-zone module (e.g., dead-zone threshold, interlock rule sets) 
need to be tuned for larger or more complex buildings; this will also 
be addressed in future studies.

Appendix B:. Supplementary experiments on transfer Learning, hierarchical Optimization, and VAV constraint integration
0B.1 Transfer learning for new buildings
The reduction in data requirement was evaluated using the ASHRAE dataset with two office buildings, designated A (8000  m2) and B (10000  m2). 
The spatiotemporal sensing engine was pre‑trained on Building A with a full year of data, then fine‑tuned on Building B using varying amounts of data: 
one week, two weeks, and four weeks. Baseline configurations included direct application without fine‑tuning and full training from scratch using one 
year of Building B data.

Table B.1 reports the virtual metering MAPE under these configurations. Fine‑tuning with only one week of data reduced the MAPE from 12.1% to 
9.0%, closing approximately half the gap to the full‑data baseline. Improvement saturated beyond four weeks, indicating diminishing returns. These 
results confirm that transfer learning substantially lowers the initial data requirement for new buildings.
0B.2 Hierarchical optimization for large campuses
Campus graphs with 100, 200, and 500 nodes were simulated. Full‑graph optimisation was compared against a hierarchical strategy that partitions 
the graph into sub‑graphs of about 50 nodes, executes independent optimisation, and then coordinates the sub‑solutions. Computation time and 
energy saving rate were recorded, with five repetitions per condition.

Fig. B1. Performance of full‑graph vs. hierarchical optimisation
Fig. B.1 compares the performance of full‑graph and hierarchical optimisation across the three campus sizes. Hierarchical optimisation reduced

approach scales effectively to large campuses.
B.3 Extending physical Constraints: VAV air flow Bounds
Fig. B2. Impact of VAV flow bound constraint on performance
To illustrate extensibility, a penalty term for VAV box air flow violations, covering minimum and maximum limits, was added to the loss function of 
the spatiotemporal sensing engine. Three typical floors with different VAV densities were tested. The evaluation metrics were MAPE and the physical 
constraint residual, defined as the root mean square error of flow violation. Each floor experiment was repeated five times. Fig. B.2 shows the impact of 
the VAV flow bound constraint on performance. Adding the VAV constraint reduced MAPE by 0.2 to 0.4 percentage points and decreased flow 
violation residuals by 50% to 60%. The improvement was consistent across floors with varying VAV densities. This confirms that the framework 
readily accommodates additional domain‑specific physical constraints, and that more complex control logics can be integrated in the same manner.
