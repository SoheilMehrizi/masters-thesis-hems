Jev versus Large Language Models for Energy-Audit
Recommendations: Accuracy, Calibration, and Cost
Eliseo Curcio
QuarluxAI • New York
Abstract. Decision models such as T ypeSafe’s Jev return probabilities for a fixed set of questions instead
of generating text, and are positioned as fast, inexpensive alternatives to large language models (LLMs).
We test this positioning on one task with auditor-recorded reference labels: predicting which of 19 energy-
conservation-measure categories a certified auditor recorded for a New Y ork City building under Local Law
87. In a pre-registered study of 7,296 buildings with a building-grouped split, we compare Jev 1.13 with
four LLMs, each with and without retrieval of five similar audited buildings, and with three local baselines;
two of the LLMs were added post hoc. In the measured configurations, Jev was far cheaper and faster than
every LLM, but its probability quality depended on the comparator: without retrieval it was worse than
Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol and better than Grok 4.7 and Kimi K3, and with retrieval no significant
difference from Grok and Kimi was detected. A gradient-boosted model trained on the labeled audits gave
the best probabilities, and an exploratory cascade that sent Jev’s uncertain buildings to an LLM showed
no consistent advantage over random routing. Jev offers substantial eﬀiciency advantages on this task; its
predictive competitiveness depends on the comparator and the metric.
Key findings
• Large efficiency gap. In the measured configurations, Jev was 29–90 × cheaper per prediction than
four LLMs and roughly 10–70 × faster at the median.
• Accuracy depends on the comparator. Without retrieval, Jev’s probabilities were significantly worse
than Claude’s and GPT’s but significantly better than Grok’s and Kimi’s Holm 𝑝 ≤ 0.0024 ).
• Simple baselines were strongest. No pretrained model without retrieval beat a constant base rate
estimated from the training labels, and a gradient-boosted model trained on the audits had the best
probabilities Brier 0.090 against 0.102–0.108 for every pretrained model with retrieval).
• Cascades: no consistent routing gain. Sending Jev’s uncertain buildings to an LLM showed no con-
sistent advantage over random routing with the tested uncertainty rule (exploratory).
1 Introduction
Automated workflows increasingly delegate small decisions to pretrained models: whether a document is
relevant, which tool to call, whether a record needs review. T wo kinds of models compete for these steps.
General-purpose large language models (LLMs) answer in text and must be asked to state a probability. A
newer class of decision models, of which T ypeSafe’s Jev is a prominent example, accepts a description of a
situation together with a set of typed questions and returns a probability for each answer directly [ 1, 2]. Jev
is priced at $0.042 per million input tokens with no charge for output [ 6], a small fraction of LLM list prices,
and is described as fast, inexpensive and accurate. Section 2 explains how it works and how it differs from
an LLM.

Speed and price are easy to verify. Accuracy is a property of a model on a task, and it matters most where
decisions carry money. We therefore test a decision model on a task with real stakes and auditor-recorded
reference labels: predicting which categories of energy-conservation measures a certified auditor recorded
for a New Y ork City building under Local Law 87 (LL87), the city’s energy-audit mandate. A model that
anticipated auditors’ recommendations from basic building data could help owners and programs prioritize
audits and retrofits. The task is also a demanding test for pretrained models. Its inputs are short and mostly
numeric, its labels are multiple and imbalanced, and labeled records exist, so a supervised model trained on
past audits is a natural competitor.
The study was pre-registered before any model was called and revised after two independent methodolog-
ical reviews. Jev has been benchmarked on general-purpose classification and reasoning datasets [ 20]. This
study adds a structured, domain-specific prediction task with a labeled history, where supervised models are
natural competitors. It makes five contributions. First, it compares a decision model with four LLMs and
three local baselines on identical buildings, with retrieval of similar audited buildings as a controlled factor;
two of the LLMs were added in a post hoc addendum. Second, it measures cost and latency from billed usage
rather than list prices alone. Third, it separates two sources of probabilistic error, scale and ranking, which
explains where the decision model falls short. Fourth, it tests, as an exploratory analysis, whether Jev can
reduce LLM spending as the first stage of a two-stage cascade. Fifth, it documents that the evaluation split
in prior work on this dataset allowed the same buildings into training and test sets, and reports results on a
corrected split.
2 Background: what Jev is and how it differs from an LLM
Decision models. Jev is the first model in what its vendor calls the System One class, a term borrowed from
Kahneman’s distinction between fast, intuitive judgments and slower, deliberate reasoning [ 3]. A System
One model “evaluates a state and returns typed answers and probabilities” rather than generated text; it does

not write replies, produce code or explain its reasoning [3]. A request has two parts (Figure 2, top). The state
describes the situation, here the 11 fields of one building record. The questions say what to decide. Three
question types exist: a Noul question returns the probability that a yes/no statement is true, a Choice question
returns one of several options, and a Score question returns a position on a spectrum, the last two with a
separate confidence value [ 4]. Each question carries an instruction and, for a Noul, criteria for “true” and
“false”. Jev reads the state once and evaluates every question against it in parallel, with up to 64,000 tokens
per request [ 1]. The vendor states that Jev is trained to return calibrated decisions, that calibration holds
across groups of predictions rather than for each answer, and that the same weights serve every customer,
with no fine-tuning on customer data [ 1, 3]. It charges $0.042 per million input tokens, and output is not
billed [1].
LLMs used for the same decision. A large language model is a general text generator. To obtain a probability
from it, the question and the data must be written into a prompt, the model must be asked to state a number,
and that number arrives as text that has to be parsed (Figure 2, bottom). Current models may first produce
hidden reasoning, which is billed as output; every generated token adds cost and time. The stated number is
a verbalized probability, which prior work finds to be informative but often overconfident and sensitive to the
prompt [14, 15]. In exchange, an LLM can answer any question that can be written down, explain its answer,
and handle tasks outside a fixed set of question types.
What this means for the comparison. In this study both kinds of model received identical information: the
same building fields, the same 19 category questions with equivalent wording, and, when retrieval was on,
the same five neighbors. Only the packaging differed. For Jev, each category became a Noul question;
for the LLMs, the 19 categories were listed in one prompt with a fixed JSON schema for the answer. The
comparison therefore isolates the model rather than the information it sees. It asks two questions that the
positioning raises: whether native probabilities are better than verbalized ones on this task, and whether a
decision model is faster and cheaper per decision. The vendor recommends gating actions on confidence and
handing uncertain cases to a person [ 5]; our exploratory cascade tests a variant in which uncertain cases go
to an LLM instead.
3 Related work
Evaluating probabilistic predictions. The Brier score [7] is the standard proper scoring rule for binary prob-
abilities. It rewards both calibration, the agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies,
and resolution, the ability to separate cases that turn out differently [ 8]. Ranking measures such as the area
under the ROC curve (AUC) isolate the second property. Classifiers are frequently miscalibrated, and post-
hoc methods such as Platt scaling, isotonic regression and temperature scaling correct much of the error
without changing a model’s ranking [ 9, 10, 11].
Confidence of pretrained language models. Pretrained transformers can be well calibrated in-domain but
less so out of domain [ 12]. LLMs can be prompted to state their confidence, and verbalized probabilities
are sometimes better calibrated than token probabilities, but they are often overconfident and sensitive to the
elicitation method [13, 14, 15]. Decision models instead return probabilities as their native output (Section 2).
Deußer et al. [ 20] evaluated the same Jev version zero-shot on 37 text datasets: its choice probabilities were
well calibrated and supported selective prediction, while its binary probabilities ranked well but were poorly
placed relative to a 0.5 threshold. Our task uses binary probabilities on short numeric records, and we find
the misplacement but not the strong ranking.

Retrieval and nearest neighbors. Supplying a model with retrieved examples improves performance on
knowledge-intensive tasks [ 16]. When the retrieved examples carry labels, a simple vote among them is
the classical nearest-neighbor rule [ 17]. It is therefore the natural control for any claim that a model adds
value beyond its retrieved context.
Learning from tabular records. Gradient-boosted trees remain among the strongest methods for medium-
sized tabular data [ 18, 19]. A prior benchmark on LL87 audits found that a supervised tree model outper-
formed all LLM variants, and that retrieval improved every LLM while explicit reasoning did not [ 21]. The
present study extends that work to decision models, adds a calibration analysis, and corrects its evaluation
split (Section 7).
4 Study design
Figure 3 summarizes the design. The protocol fixed the data handling, arms, metrics, primary contrasts,
sample-size rule and statistical tests before any model was called.

4.2 Task and arms
Each building is posed as 19 yes/no questions (“Did the auditor record at least one measure in category
𝑋?”), sent together in one request so that every model receives identical packaging, and every arm returns a
probability per category. The pretrained arms are Jev 1.13.0, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, which
were asked to state a probability per category in a fixed JSON schema. All versions were pinned. Both LLM
providers rejected requests for deterministic output with reasoning disabled. Under a protocol amendment
approved before the main runs, each LLM therefore ran at its lowest accepted reasoning setting and default
temperature; no reasoning output was observed for Claude, and GPT used a mean of 7.6 reasoning tokens
per call.
Grok 4.7 (xAI) and Kimi K3 (Moonshot) were added after the main study was complete (addendum
A1), because the author’s original arm list included them. They received the frozen prompt, rebuilt byte for
byte and checked by hash, the same JSON schema and parsing rules, and the same 300 calibration and 200
common test buildings. Request identifiers were taken from each provider’s live model list, and the lowest
accepted reasoning setting was established by live probes as in the amendment: Grok accepted reasoning
effort “low” and used a mean of 880–1,014 reasoning tokens per call, which are billed as output. Kimi ac-
cepted reasoning effort “none”, a value its API does not document, and no Kimi response contained reasoning
output.

The local arms require no API calls. XGBoost fits one classifier per category with hyperparameters
fixed in advance and Platt calibration on out-of-fold predictions [ 18, 9]. Neighbor voting predicts the share
of a building’s five nearest training buildings whose auditor recorded the category. The frequency baseline

When it is on, the prompt lists the same five neighbors used by neighbor voting, with their recorded categories.
4.3 Statistical analysis
The primary metric is the Brier score of raw probabilities, averaged over buildings and categories. Three
contrasts were pre-registered: Jev against Claude (P1a) and against GPT (P1b), both without retrieval, and
Jev with retrieval against neighbor voting (P2). Each uses a paired sign-flip permutation test on per-building
differences (10,000 permutations) with Holm correction across the three contrasts; confidence intervals are
paired bootstrap percentiles over buildings (10,000 resamples). Jev and the local baselines were evaluated
on all 1,460 test buildings and the LLMs on a stratified subset of 200, after a pre-registered sample-size
rule based only on the variability of pilot differences required 129. Analyses other than the three primary
contrasts are exploratory. The addendum compares Jev with Grok and Kimi at matched retrieval settings in
a family of four contrasts with the same tests and Holm correction within the family.
4.4 Execution and quality control
The study was run by an autonomous pipeline: an executor agent (Claude Opus 5.5) wrote and ran all code,
and each stage was approved by an independent reviewer model (GPT-6 Astra) before the next began. Head-
line metrics were recomputed by a separate verifier agent without access to the scoring code, and again for
this paper, with no mismatches. T wo procedural deviations, one extra review round in the scoring stage and
an addendum run interrupted when provider accounts ran out of funds, did not change any reported metric.
The total cost of the study was $72.16, of which $34.34 was model API charges.
5 Results
5.1 Primary comparisons
Without retrieval, Jev’s probabilities were significantly worse than those of Claude and GPT (Figure 4): its
Brier score exceeded Claude’s by 0.022 and GPT’s by 0.008. With retrieval, Jev’s Brier score was 0.0008
lower than that of neighbor voting, with a confidence interval spanning zero. The study therefore provides
no evidence that Jev adds value beyond its retrieved neighbors, and it does not establish equivalence either.

5.2 Accuracy and cost
Table 1 and Figure 1 compare all arms on the same 200 buildings, and Figure 5 breaks down tokens and cost
per model. Cost is computed from billed tokens at documented list prices and expressed per 1,000 category
predictions, so one building corresponds to 19 predictions. At matched retrieval settings Jev was 29–46 times
cheaper than Claude and GPT and 40–90 times cheaper than Grok and Kimi. Its median latency was 0.18–
0.20 s against 1.8–4.3 s for Claude and GPT and 6.4–13.2 s for Kimi and Grok, although its 95th-percentile
latency (3.3 s without retrieval, 7.2 s with it) was no better than Claude’s (3.3–3.5 s; Appendix Figure 10).
Jev’s requests were the longest, about 2,170 input tokens without retrieval and 4,150 with it, because each of
the 19 questions is sent as a structured object; its price of $0.042 per million input tokens with unbilled output
nevertheless made it the cheapest arm, at about $0.0001–0.0002 per building. Grok was the most expensive,
because it used 880–1,014 reasoning tokens per request, billed as output, at $0.008–0.011 per building.
T wo patterns stand out. Without retrieval, all five pretrained models had a higher Brier score than the
constant frequency baseline (0.103), so none of them improved on simply predicting each category’s base
rate. Retrieval lowered every pretrained model’s Brier score. Claude and GPT moved marginally below the
frequency baseline; Grok, Jev and Kimi remained just above it. XGBoost had the lowest Brier score of all
arms. At the pre-registered 0.5 cutoff, Jev’s micro-F1 (0.060 without retrieval, 0.175 with) was the lowest of
all arms, below even the frequency baseline (0.246).

5.4 Calibration
Reliability diagrams (Figure 6) show why the pretrained models trail the base rate without retrieval: all five
overstate the probability that a measure was recommended. Grok and Kimi overstate it most, with mean
probabilities of 0.35 and 0.38 against a prevalence of 0.146 on these buildings. Jev’s probabilities are also
compressed. Across the test set they average 0.33, more than twice the true prevalence of 0.146, yet only
3.3% of them reach 0.5, which explains its very low micro-F1 at that cutoff. With retrieval, Claude, GPT
and Jev track observed frequencies closely below a predicted probability of about 0.4, while Grok and Kimi
remain below the diagonal over most of the range.

5.8 Jev as the first stage of a cascade (exploratory)
A decision model need not replace an LLM to be useful: it could answer the cases it is confident about and
pass the rest to an LLM. We tested this with saved outputs only, without new model calls. The first stage
was Jev with retrieval or, for comparison, XGBoost; the second stage was Claude or GPT with retrieval. A
building was escalated when the first stage’s mean uncertainty over the 19 categories, ̄ 𝑢 = 119 ∑𝑐 𝑝𝑐(1 −
𝑝𝑐), exceeded a threshold. Thresholds for target escalation rates of 0–100% were set as quantiles of ̄ 𝑢on
the 300 calibration buildings and then applied unchanged to the 200 common buildings. Cost counts the
first stage on every building and the LLM on escalated buildings, from each building’s billed usage. The
comparison for routing is random escalation of the same number of buildings, whose expected Brier score is
the escalation-weighted average of the two stages. Intervals are paired bootstrap intervals over buildings with
routing decisions held fixed. A feasibility check had already examined these test buildings, so this analysis
is exploratory and was not pre-registered.
Figure 9 and Table 3 show the results. Uncertainty routing from Jev showed no consistent advantage
over random routing: with Claude, the interval excluded zero at 2 of 9 intermediate rates, by at most 0.0014,
without correction for the number of rates examined; with GPT, at none. The reason is visible at the level of
buildings. The LLM improved on Jev for 61–64% of buildings, but Jev’s uncertainty was almost unrelated
to where it did (Spearman correlation between ̄ 𝑢and the per-building Brier gain of −0.10 to 0.09 on the
calibration and test sets). No Jev–LLM operating point was significantly better than the frequency baseline,
and all were significantly worse than XGBoost alone, by 0.012–0.017. Escalating from XGBoost only made
results worse than XGBoost alone, significantly so at most rates from about 30%.
One operating point is descriptively attractive. Escalating 72.5% of buildings from Jev to Claude gave
a Brier score of 0.1016 against 0.1017 for Claude alone, at $0.292 rather than $0.389 per 1,000 predictions,
about 25% less. Equivalence was not tested, the point was selected after examining several, and so the result
shows only that no loss was detected. A cascade also costs more than the LLM alone when every building is
escalated ($0.399 against $0.389 for Claude), because Jev is paid on every building. Replayed from logged
call times, the cascade’s median latency at that point was 2.25 s, against 2.11 s for Claude alone; the cascade
was therefore slightly slower than Claude alone in this replay, which is an estimate rather than a measurement
of a live cascade.

6 Discussion
6.1 Efficiency and predictive competitiveness
In the configurations we measured, Jev’s eﬀiciency advantage held against every LLM. Its predictive standing
did not hold uniformly. On probability quality without retrieval, it was worse than Claude and GPT and better
than Grok and Kimi, so the answer to “is a decision model as good as an LLM?” depends on which LLM
is meant. On classification at the 0.5 cutoff it was worse than all four LLMs, because its probabilities rarely
reach 0.5. And it was worse than a constant base-rate predictor and a supervised model on Brier score and on
F1 at the 0.5 cutoff. On this task, then, a decision model can be preferable to some LLMs where probabilities
are consumed directly and cost or latency matters, but it was not the most accurate option available.
6.2 Probability scale and ranking
The Brier score penalizes two different errors: probabilities on the wrong scale, and a failure to rank buildings
that received a measure above buildings that did not [ 8]. In an imbalanced multi-label task, the frequency
baseline is a demanding reference: it has no ranking ability at all, but its probabilities are correct on average
for every category. A model beats it only if its ranking gain exceeds its scale error. Without retrieval, all five
pretrained models failed that test. Jev’s probabilities sat far above the base rate, and its ranking was close to
chance; Grok’s and Kimi’s sat further still above the base rate, which is why their Brier scores were worse
than Jev’s despite similar ranking (macro AUC 0.574–0.577 against Jev’s 0.559). The two problems call for
different remedies, and our analysis tests only some of them. A monotone recalibration fitted on labeled data
could reduce the scale error without changing the ranking; we did not fit one, so its effect on Jev’s Brier
score is untested. Threshold tuning, which we did test, changes decisions but not the probabilities or their
Brier score. With a tuned cutoff, Jev without retrieval remained below the frequency baseline in micro-F1,
which is consistent with its weak ranking (macro AUC 0.55–0.56). Deußer et al. [ 20] also found Jev’s binary
probabilities poorly placed relative to 0.5 but well ranked on text tasks; on these numeric records the ranking
was weak as well. The practical consequence is that Jev’s raw probabilities should not be taken at face value

6.3 The role of retrieval
Retrieval lowered every pretrained model’s Brier score and raised its macro AUC and tuned F1, and its effect
was larger than the differences between models. (The one exception is Claude’s F1 at the 0.5 cutoff, which
was essentially unchanged: 0.368 without retrieval, 0.362 with it.) With retrieval, the five pretrained models’
Brier scores fell within 0.007 of each other (0.102–0.108), and neighbor voting (0.109) was close behind,
and Jev’s difference from neighbor voting was not significant either at the primary metric or at tuned cutoffs.
A plausible interpretation is that, with retrieval, the pretrained models mostly aggregate the labels of the
retrieved neighbors. Their value then depends on whether they aggregate better than a vote. For Jev, this
study finds no evidence that it does. Retrieval also changes the nature of the comparison: a model that reads
labeled neighbors is drawing on the same labeled data a supervised model would use.
6.4 A trained model was strongest on this task
The supervised baseline was the strongest arm on probability quality and was competitive on every other
metric, with no API charge, although training and serving it have costs of their own that we did not measure.
At the pre-registered cutoff it outperformed Jev with as few as 50 training buildings. This agrees with the
prior benchmark on the same records [ 21] and with the broader evidence that tree ensembles remain strong
on tabular data [ 19]. It also changes how the cost advantage should be read. Measured per building, the
differences between pretrained models are small: with retrieval, Claude cost about $0.0074 and Jev about
$0.0002 per building, or about $10.80 and $0.25 for the full test set. At the scale of a building portfolio, these
amounts are small enough that accuracy, rather than inference price, is likely to decide which approach is
preferable.
Table 4 summarizes what this evidence supports. It comes from one task with short, structured, mostly
numeric inputs and a labeled history, and may not transfer to others.
6.5 Scope
These results concern one kind of task: short, mostly numeric records with a labeled history. They do not
speak to decision models on free text or to multi-step agentic decisions, the uses their designers emphasize.

A fair assessment of those uses requires tasks with reliable reference labels in which inputs are textual and
labels are scarce, which we leave to follow-up work.
7 Implication for the prior benchmark
The prior LL87 benchmark [ 21] split records rather than buildings, and 44.6% of its test records share a
BBL with a training record. Its tree model reproduces a micro-F1 of 0.577 on that split (0.571 reported),
and the same code scores 0.481 on the building-grouped split used here. The difference combines grouping,
deduplication to one record per building, and the resulting change in training and test populations, so we
do not attribute it to leakage alone. The overlap is nevertheless large enough that the prior headline results
should be regarded as optimistic and re-evaluated on a grouped split. All results in this paper use the grouped
split.
8 Limitations
The task predicts auditor-recorded categories, not the effectiveness or savings of the measures, and an un-
recorded category is not evidence that a measure is unsuitable. Grok and Kimi were added after the main
results were known; their comparisons are exploratory, and the robustness checks were not repeated for
them. Kimi’s reasoning setting is an undocumented value that its API accepted. Other providers, including
open-weight models, were not evaluated. The cascade analysis is exploratory: the test buildings had been
examined in a feasibility check, several operating points were inspected, and its latencies are replayed from
logged call times rather than measured in a live cascade. The LLM comparisons use 200 buildings; this
was suﬀicient for the primary contrasts under the pre-registered rule but leaves wide intervals for exploratory
LLM comparisons. The LLMs could not be run deterministically, and GPT used some reasoning tokens. The
tuned-cutoff analysis is post hoc and relies on 300 labeled calibration buildings. The reviewer model and
one evaluated model come from the same vendor; the reviewer assessed methods and arithmetic, not model
outputs. The verifier and executor agents are the same model. Recorded savings in the LL87 file include
at least one implausible value (about $963 billion per year for one building), so savings-weighted analyses
were omitted.
9 Conclusion
On the prediction of energy-audit recommendations, Jev delivered large speed and price advantages in the
configurations we measured, and its predictive competitiveness depended on the comparator and the metric.
Without retrieval, its probabilities were worse than those of Claude and GPT and better than those of Grok
and Kimi, at 1–4% of the cost of any of them; with retrieval, no significant difference from Grok, Kimi
or a vote among the same neighbors was detected. Its decisions at the 0.5 cutoff were the weakest of all
arms, and no pretrained model without retrieval improved on a constant base-rate predictor. Using Jev’s
uncertainty to decide which buildings to send to an LLM showed no consistent advantage over random routing.
A supervised model trained on the labeled audits gave the best probabilities and, at the pre-registered cutoff,
outperformed Jev with as few as 50 training buildings. Jev offers substantial eﬀiciency advantages, but its
predictive competitiveness depends on the comparator and the metric; on this task, a model trained on the
labeled audits and retrieval of similar buildings were the stronger choices.
