ABSTRACT
Modern smart grids increasingly rely on intelligent decision-support systems to coordinate distributed energy 
resources, renewable generation, and complex cyber–physical infrastructures. Although recent advances in 
artificial intelligence (AI) have substantially improved forecasting, optimization, and operational analytics, 
existing solutions remain largely fragmented, with limited system-level integration of perception, reasoning, 
coordination, and execution.
To address this gap, this study conducts a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 
(PRISMA)-guided systematic review of 61 peer-reviewed papers published between 2019 and 2026. Based on the 
extracted evidence, an evidence-informed six-layer architectural framework is proposed that integrates physical 
data acquisition, semantic context abstraction, large language model reasoning, multi-agent coordination, tool- 
augmented execution, digital twin validation, and cross-layer governance into a unified smart grid intelligence 
architecture.
The review shows that recent research has progressively evolved from isolated predictive models toward 
cognitive and agent-based decision-support systems; however, no existing framework provides an integrated 
architecture that simultaneously combines semantic reasoning, engineering tool orchestration, digital twin 
verification, governance mechanisms, and human oversight. To bridge this gap, the proposed framework es-
tablishes a perception–reasoning–action workflow and is further illustrated through an operational use-case 
scenario demonstrating the interaction between heterogeneous data sources, specialized agents, engineering 
tools, validation mechanisms, and decision support.
The analysis indicates that large language models can significantly enhance contextual reasoning, coordina-
tion, explainability, and operational adaptability when deployed as cognitive orchestrators rather than auton-
omous controllers. Nevertheless, practical deployment remains constrained by challenges including numerical 
reliability, inference latency, cybersecurity, operational safety, and regulatory compliance, requiring bounded 
autonomy supported by engineering verification, digital twin simulation, governance policies, and human-in-the- 
loop supervision.
This work provides an evidence-informed architectural foundation for trustworthy next-generation smart grid 
intelligence and offers a structured roadmap for future research on integrating large language models into real- 
world energy system operation.

1. Introduction
The rapid evolution of modern power systems toward decentralized, 
data-intensive, and cyber-physical infrastructures has fundamentally 
reshaped smart grids. The integration of renewable energy, distributed 
energy resources (DERs), electric vehicles, and intelligent demand-side 
management has transformed smart grids into dynamic systems 
requiring real-time, uncertainty-aware decision-making. In this context, 
AI and machine learning (ML) have become key enablers of forecasting, 
optimization, and system resilience [1,2].
Over the past decade, AI-driven approaches have been widely 
applied in smart grids, including load forecasting, anomaly detection, 
demand response, and predictive maintenance. Deep learning models 
such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs), convolutional neural net-
works (CNNs), and reinforcement learning (RL) have demonstrated 
strong performance in modeling nonlinear spatio-temporal dynamics [3,
4]. More recently, paradigms such as federated learning (FL) and digital 
twins (DTs) have been introduced to address data privacy, scalability, 
and cyber-physical integration challenges [5,6]. However, most existing 
approaches remain task-specific and lack cross-domain reasoning and 
system-level coordination capabilities.
Recent comprehensive reviews have further emphasized that the 
transition toward renewable-dominated power systems introduces sig-
nificant planning and operational challenges, including distributed 
resource coordination, uncertainty management, multi-objective opti-
mization, and resilient microgrid operation. These challenges require 
integrated decision-support mechanisms capable of combining plan-
ning, optimization, and operational intelligence across multiple tem-
poral and spatial scales. Although existing reviews primarily focus on 
optimization algorithms and energy management strategies, they pro-
vide important context for understanding why more advanced cognitive 
architectures based on LLMs and agentic AI are emerging as comple-
mentary decision-support technologies for future smart grids [7].
In parallel, large language models (LLMs), enabled by transformer- 
based architectures [8], have introduced new capabilities in contex-
tual reasoning, planning, and cross-task generalization. Foundation 
models such as GPT-style architectures have demonstrated strong per-
formance in multi-step reasoning, tool utilization, and decision support 
with minimal task-specific training [9]. These capabilities position LLMs 
as promising candidates for bridging the gap between data-driven ana-
lytics and high-level decision intelligence in complex cyber-physical 
systems.
However, the application of LLMs in smart grids remains in its early 
stages. Existing studies primarily employ LLMs as natural language in-
terfaces for querying data, generating reports, or supporting operator 
interaction, rather than as integral components of decision-making 
pipelines [10]. This limited perspective underutilizes their potential as 
cognitive engines capable of orchestrating heterogeneous models and 
coordinating distributed energy systems.
A key limitation of current smart grid intelligence is the absence of a 
unified architectural framework that integrates perception, reasoning, 
and action. Existing ML pipelines typically operate in isolation, opti-
mizing individual tasks without capturing system-level interactions. As 
smart grids evolve into decentralized and multi-agent environments, 
there is a growing need for holistic, context-aware intelligence that 
enables coordinated and adaptive decision-making across heteroge-
neous components.
Recent advances in agentic AI and tool-augmented LLMs introduce a 
new paradigm in which systems can decompose tasks, invoke external 
tools, and iteratively refine decisions through feedback loops [11,12]. 
This paradigm enables LLMs to function not merely as predictive 
models, but as orchestrators of multi-step decision processes across 
interconnected subsystems.
To address this gap, this study introduces a structured taxonomy of 
LLM roles in smart grids across three functional levels: interface, 
reasoner, and agent. These roles capture the progression from natural 
language interaction to context-aware reasoning and, ultimately, to 
autonomous coordination and tool-augmented decision-making.
Building on this taxonomy, the paper proposes a six-layer architec-
ture for LLM-driven smart grids, in which LLMs function as central 
reasoning and orchestration components within a cyber-physical sys-
tem. The framework integrates data acquisition, context abstraction, 
LLM-based reasoning, multi-agent coordination, tool-augmented 
execution, and cross-layer governance, enabling a coherent percep-
tion–reasoning–action pipeline. In this design, LLMs transform raw grid 
data into structured representations, generate context-aware decisions, 
and coordinate domain-specific tools such as forecasting and optimiza-
tion models. Integration with digital twins further supports closed-loop 
feedback, allowing decisions to be simulated and refined prior to 
deployment.
Methodologically, the study adopts a dual approach combining 
PRISMA-guided systematic review with conceptual architectural syn-
thesis. A total of 61 papers are analyzed to identify recurring patterns, 
capability gaps, and emerging design principles in LLM-enabled smart 
grid intelligence. The resulting contributions include: (i) a systematic 
evidence synthesis of LLM and AI applications in smart grids; (ii) a 
domain-specific taxonomy of LLM roles; (iii) an evidence-informed 
architectural framework; and (iv) a structured analysis of key chal-
lenges, including scalability, safety, latency, and trustworthiness.
Importantly, the proposed taxonomy and architecture are not pre-
sented as direct outputs of individual studies, but as higher-order con-
ceptual syntheses derived from structured evidence aggregation. 
Accordingly, the architecture should be interpreted as a design-oriented 
abstraction that organizes fragmented literature into a coherent system- 
level perspective, rather than as a fully validated end-to-end 
implementation.
This work does not introduce a new standalone algorithm, but pro-
vides the first domain-specific integration of LLM role taxonomy with 
cyber-physical smart grid architectures, bridging semantic reasoning, 
agentic coordination, and energy system constraints.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 pre-
sents the research methodology. Section 3 introduces the taxonomy of 
LLM roles. Section 4 describes the proposed architecture. Section 5
provides the thematic synthesis of the reviewed papers. Section 6 dis-
cusses challenges and future directions. Section 7 presents the limita-
tions of the study. Section 8 concludes the paper.
2. Methodology
This study was designed as a systematic literature review aimed at 
identifying, screening, and synthesizing the body of research related to 
artificial intelligence, machine learning, and more specifically LLM- 
driven intelligence in smart grids. In addition to summarizing prior 
studies, the review was structured to support the development of a 
conceptual taxonomy and a multi-layer architectural framework for 
LLM-driven autonomous smart grids. Accordingly, the methodology 
combined a PRISMA-guided screening process with structured evidence 
extraction and thematic synthesis.
The review protocol was designed to answer three interrelated 
research questions: 
(1) How have AI- and ML-based approaches evolved in smart grid 
research toward LLM-enabled intelligence?
(2) What functional roles are currently assigned to LLMs in energy 
systems, and where are the major conceptual and practical gaps?
(3) What architectural patterns, enabling technologies, and unre-
solved challenges can inform the design of autonomous, agentic, 
and trustworthy smart grid systems?
To address these questions, the review followed a stepwise pipeline 
involving search strategy design, eligibility definition, PRISMA-based 
study selection, and structured data extraction.

2.1. Search strategy
A comprehensive search strategy was designed to retrieve relevant 
publications at the intersection of smart grids, artificial intelligence, 
machine learning, digital twins, multi-agent systems, and large language 
models. The search was conducted across four major scientific sources: 
Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and ScienceDirect. These data-
bases were selected because together they provide strong coverage of 
energy systems, power engineering, artificial intelligence, cyber- 
physical systems, and intelligent infrastructure research.
The search period covered publications from 2019 to 2026, reflect-
ing the recent acceleration of research on foundation models, autono-
mous AI, and their integration with cyber-physical energy systems. 
Search strings were constructed using Boolean operators and keyword 
combinations to capture both established and emerging terminology in 
the field. To improve retrieval quality, the search was restricted to 
English-language journal articles, review papers, conference papers, and 
book chapters that provided substantive technical, methodological, or 
conceptual contributions. Editorials, notes, abstracts without full text, 
patents, and purely non-technical opinion pieces were not considered.
Some of the included papers from 2025 to 2026 consist of preprints 
and early-access publications. These were included when they provided 
sufficient methodological detail and relevance to emerging LLM-based 
paradigms, although their peer-review status may still be pending.
After merging results from all four databases and removing obvious 
non-relevant entries during the initial sorting stage, a corpus of 123 
records was retained as the basis for systematic screening.
To ensure reproducibility, the final search strings and database- 
specific query configurations are summarized in Table 1.
Review papers were initially retained to preserve search sensitivity, 
but were excluded during eligibility assessment to ensure that the final 
synthesis is grounded in primary evidence.
Duplicate records across databases were identified and removed 
using a combination of automated filtering (based on title, DOI, and 
metadata matching) and manual verification to ensure accuracy. When 
multiple versions of the same study were found (e.g., conference and 
extended journal versions), the most complete and recent version was 
retained.
2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The study selection criteria were defined a priori to ensure meth-
odological consistency and to maintain a clear focus on the review 
objective.
Inclusion criteria
Papers were included if they met all of the following conditions: 
1. The study addressed smart grids, intelligent energy systems, or 
closely related power-system applications.
2. The study involved AI, ML, deep learning, digital twin, agent-based, 
or LLM-related methods relevant to the operation, planning, opti-
mization, monitoring, forecasting, cybersecurity, or automation of 
smart grids.
3. The study contained sufficient methodological or conceptual detail 
to support evidence extraction and comparative analysis.
4. The full text was accessible in English.
5. The publication fell within the 2019–2026 time window.
Exclusion criteria
Papers were excluded if they met one or more of the following 
conditions: 
1. They were outside the topical scope of smart grids or energy systems.
2. They mentioned AI or LLMs only tangentially, without a substantive 
link to energy-system functionality or architecture.
3. They were duplicated records across databases.
4. They lacked sufficient methodological clarity, technical depth, or 
conceptual relevance.
5. They were short abstracts, editorials, announcements, or non-peer- 
reviewed commentary without analyzable content.
These criteria were particularly important because the review was 
not limited to cataloging applications; it was intended to support a 
taxonomy of LLM roles and the synthesis of a layered architecture for 
autonomous smart grids. Therefore, Papers were favored when they 
provided information relevant to reasoning, orchestration, agentic 
behavior, tool use, digital twin integration, or governance-oriented AI in 
energy contexts. This is consistent with the paper’s current emphasis on 
LLM roles, agentic coordination, and cross-layer feedback and 
governance.

To enhance the methodological rigor of the screening process, an

research contributions to construct an evidence-to-architecture mapping 
grounded in original methodological insights. While this approach may 
reduce coverage breadth, it enhances the granularity and traceability of 
extracted evidence, which is essential for the development of the pro-

the emerging nature of LLM applications in smart grids, a substantial 
portion of the broader AI literature was excluded due to insufficient 
relevance to reasoning, orchestration, or agentic intelligence.

2.5. Quality assessment and coding strategy
To improve the transparency, reproducibility, and methodological 
rigor of the systematic review, a structured quality assessment and 
coding strategy was applied after full-text eligibility screening. This step 
was designed to ensure that the final corpus of 61 papers was not only 
topically relevant but also sufficiently informative for evidence extrac-
tion, thematic synthesis, and architectural mapping.
2.5.1. Quality assessment criteria
Each full-text article retained after eligibility screening was assessed 
using predefined quality and relevance criteria. The assessment focused 
on whether the study provided sufficient methodological, technical, or 
architectural information to support inclusion in the synthesis. The 
criteria were not intended to assign a formal numerical quality score, but 
rather to ensure consistency in determining whether each study could 
contribute meaningful evidence to the review questions.
Before performing evidence extraction, all eligible papers were 
evaluated using a predefined set of quality and relevance criteria. Rather 
than assigning a numerical quality score, the assessment ensured that 
each included study provided sufficient methodological, technical, and 
architectural information to contribute meaningfully to the evidence 
synthesis. The criteria used during this quality assessment are summa-
rized in Table 2. papers were excluded from the final synthesis if they 
were only marginally related to smart grids, mentioned AI or LLMs 
without substantive technical relevance, lacked methodological clarity, 
or did not provide sufficient information to support architectural 
interpretation. Review papers were used for contextual understanding 
where necessary but were not treated as primary evidence in the final 
synthesis to avoid duplication of secondary interpretations.

2.5.2. Coding protocol and evidence extraction
After quality assessment, the included papers were coded using a 
structured evidence-extraction protocol. The purpose of coding was to 
transform the reviewed literature from a descriptive list of studies into a 
traceable evidence base for taxonomy development and architectural 
synthesis.
Following quality assessment, each included study was systemati-
cally coded using a structured evidence-extraction protocol. The coding 
framework enabled consistent identification of architectural character-
istics, methodological features, validation evidence, and functional ca-
pabilities across the reviewed literature, thereby supporting the 
subsequent taxonomy development and architectural synthesis. The 
extracted coding dimensions are summarized in Table 3.
This coding protocol enabled each study to be mapped to functional 
roles and architectural layers rather than summarized only narratively. 
Studies contributing to more than one function were assigned both a 
primary and, where appropriate, secondary architectural placement. 
The primary placement was determined according to the dominant 
technical role of the study, while secondary placements were used to 
preserve cross-layer contributions.
2.5.3. Evidence-to-architecture mapping
The coded evidence was aggregated through thematic synthesis. 
Recurrent patterns across the corpus were grouped into six architectural 
categories: physical infrastructure and data acquisition, data processing 
and context abstraction, LLM-based cognitive reasoning, multi-agent 
coordination, tool-augmented execution, and digital twin validation 
and governance.
This mapping process was essential for distinguishing evidence- 
driven findings from the authors’ conceptual synthesis. The evidence- 
driven component consists of recurring patterns, application domains, 
reported capabilities, and limitations extracted from the 61 reviewed 
papers. In contrast, the proposed six-layer architecture represents an 
author-level conceptual synthesis that integrates these recurring pat-
terns into a unified system-level framework. Therefore, the architecture 
should not be interpreted as a configuration directly implemented in any 
single reviewed study, but as an evidence-informed integration derived 
from cross-study synthesis.
2.5.4. Consistency checking and inter-rater validation
To improve consistency during study selection and coding, the 
screening and extraction process followed a consensus-oriented valida-
tion procedure. The inclusion and exclusion criteria, quality assessment 
dimensions, and coding categories were defined before final synthesis. 
Ambiguous cases were reviewed against the research questions and 
resolved through discussion among the authors.
Where papers could plausibly fit multiple categories, classification 
was based on the dominant contribution of the paper. For example, a 
study combining LLM-based reasoning with optimization solvers was 
coded under both the LLM cognitive reasoning layer and the tool- 
augmented execution layer, while its primary placement was deter-
mined by the main objective emphasized in the study.
To further reduce subjective interpretation, a subset of the screened 
papers was independently checked by more than one reviewer for 
eligibility and coding consistency. Disagreements were resolved through 
discussion until consensus was reached. This process helped ensure that 
the final classification of papers was consistent with the predefined 
criteria and that the architectural synthesis remained traceable to the 
underlying evidence.
Overall, this quality assessment and coding strategy strengthens the 
reproducibility of the review process and provides a transparent meth-
odological bridge between the PRISMA-guided study selection, the 
thematic synthesis of the 61 included papers, and the proposed six-layer 
architecture.

3. Taxonomy of LLM roles in smart grids
The rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs) has introduced 
a new paradigm in artificial intelligence, enabling systems to perform 
not only pattern recognition but also contextual reasoning, planning, 
and tool orchestration. While LLMs have been extensively explored in 
domains such as natural language processing, software engineering, and 
knowledge systems, their role in cyber-physical infrastructures partic-
ularly smart grids remains conceptually fragmented and insufficiently 
formalized. Existing papers often employ LLMs in an ad hoc manner, 
primarily as interfaces for querying data or generating reports, without a 
structured understanding of their functional capabilities within energy 
systems [1,10].
While similar conceptual progressions have been explored in prior 
agentic AI literature, the taxonomy proposed in this study contextualizes 
these roles specifically within smart grid environments, linking them to 
domain-specific functions such as grid operation, optimization, and 
cyber-physical coordination.
To address this gap, this paper proposes a unified taxonomy that 
conceptualizes the progressive roles of LLMs in smart grids across three 
levels of intelligence: LLM-as-interface, LLM-as-reasoner, and LLM-as- 
agent. This taxonomy reflects an evolutionary trajectory from passive 
interaction to active decision-making and provides a foundational 
framework for designing LLM-driven autonomous energy systems.
3.1. LLM-as-interface: natural language access to energy systems
At the most fundamental level, LLMs function as intelligent in-
terfaces that enable natural language interaction between human op-
erators and complex energy systems. In this role, LLMs act as semantic 
translators, converting user queries into structured representations and 
retrieving relevant information from underlying databases, dashboards, 
or knowledge bases.
This paradigm aligns with early applications of LLMs in smart grids, 
where models are primarily used for tasks such as report generation, 
question answering, and operator assistance [2]. For example, 
LLM-based systems can allow grid operators to query real-time system 
status, interpret sensor data, or generate summaries of operational 
events using natural language. Such capabilities significantly enhance 
usability and accessibility, especially in environments characterized by 
high data complexity and heterogeneous information sources.
Technically, this level is often implemented through retrieval- 
augmented generation (RAG) frameworks, where LLMs are coupled 
with structured data repositories or domain-specific knowledge graphs 
[13]. By grounding responses in external data sources, these systems 
improve factual accuracy and reduce hallucination risks, which is crit-
ical in safety-sensitive domains like energy systems.
Despite its utility, the LLM-as-interface paradigm remains limited in 
scope. It primarily supports human-in-the-loop interaction and does not 
directly contribute to autonomous decision-making. The model does not 
reason deeply about system dynamics nor does it initiate actions. As 
such, it represents an entry-level integration of LLMs into smart grids, 
focusing on communication rather than intelligence.
3.2. LLM-as-reasoner: context-aware intelligence and decision support
Moving beyond simple interaction, the second level of the taxonomy 
conceptualizes LLMs as reasoning engines capable of contextual un-
derstanding, multi-step inference, and cross-domain analysis. In this 
role, LLMs operate as cognitive layers that integrate heterogeneous data 
sources—including time-series sensor data, historical records, and 
domain knowledge—to generate informed insights and support 
decision-making processes.
Recent advances in LLM capabilities, such as chain-of-thought 
reasoning, tool-augmented inference, and few-shot generalization, 
have significantly enhanced their ability to perform complex analytical

For instance, LLMs can interpret anomalies in grid behavior by

LLMs can bridge semantic reasoning with numerical computation by

advanced paradigm: LLM-as-agent.
3.3. LLM-as-agent: autonomous coordination and action
The highest level of the proposed taxonomy represents a paradigm 
shift in which LLMs function as autonomous agents capable of planning, 
coordinating, and executing actions within the smart grid ecosystem. In 
this role, LLMs are no longer passive components but active participants 
in system operation, interacting with other agents, invoking tools, and 
adapting to dynamic environments.
This paradigm is enabled by recent developments in agentic AI, 
where LLMs are integrated with planning modules, memory systems, 
and tool-calling mechanisms to form autonomous decision-making en-
tities [11,12]. Frameworks such as ReAct and generative agents 
demonstrate how LLMs can iteratively reason, act, and learn through 
feedback loops, effectively bridging cognition and execution.
In smart grids, LLM-as-agent enables a wide range of advanced ca-
pabilities, including autonomous grid control, distributed energy 
resource coordination, real-time demand response, and adaptive market 
participation. For example, an LLM-based agent can monitor grid con-
ditions, identify potential violations, formulate mitigation strategies, 
and execute control actions through APIs connected to energy man-
agement systems (EMS) or SCADA platforms.
Furthermore, when combined with multi-agent systems, LLM agents 
can coordinate with specialized entities such as EV agents, market 
agents, or security agents forming a distributed intelligence network 
that enhances scalability and resilience [18]. This aligns with the 
increasing decentralization of modern energy systems, where 
decision-making must occur across multiple nodes and stakeholders.
A key enabler of this paradigm is the integration with Digital Twin 
environments, which allow agents to simulate and validate decisions 
before real-world execution. This creates a closed-loop system where 
actions are continuously refined based on feedback, improving both 
performance and safety.
Despite its transformative potential, the LLM-as-agent paradigm in-
troduces significant challenges. Issues related to safety, reliability, la-
tency, interpretability, and governance become critical when 
autonomous systems operate in mission-critical infrastructures. 
Ensuring that agentic LLMs adhere to operational constraints, regulatory 
policies, and ethical standards remains an open research problem [19].
3.4. From interfaces to autonomous energy intelligence
The proposed taxonomy highlights a clear evolutionary trajectory in 
the role of LLMs within smart grids from interface-level interaction to 
reasoning-driven intelligence and ultimately to fully autonomous 
agents. This progression reflects a broader shift in artificial intelligence 
toward agentic, cognitive, and system-level intelligence, where models 
are not confined to isolated tasks but operate as integrated components 
of complex systems.
Importantly, these three levels are not mutually exclusive; rather, 
they coexist within a layered architecture. An effective LLM-driven 
smart grid system may simultaneously employ interface-level interac-
tion for human communication, reasoning capabilities for decision 
support, and agentic behavior for autonomous operation. This layered 
perspective directly informs the multi-layer architecture proposed in 
this paper, where each level of the taxonomy corresponds to specific 
functional layers within the system.
By formalizing the roles of LLMs in this manner, the taxonomy pro-
vides both a conceptual foundation and a practical design framework for 
advancing smart grids toward cognitive, adaptive, and autonomous 
energy systems.
3.5. Comparison with existing grid intelligence frameworks
The proposed three-level taxonomy (LLM-as-Interface, LLM-as- 
Reasoner, LLM-as-Agent) and the six-layer architecture build upon and 
extend several established frameworks in smart grid intelligence.
Traditional multi-agent systems (MAS) have long been applied to 
smart grid control. Mahela et al. [20] provide a comprehensive review of 
MAS architectures for energy management, market operations, and fault 
handling. These frameworks typically emphasize decentralized 
decision-making and agent communication via protocols such as FIPA, 
offering strong scalability and fault tolerance. However, they often lack 
advanced semantic reasoning and natural language grounding, relying 
primarily on rule-based or optimization-driven agents.
More recently, Kiasari et al. [21] proposed a formalized agentic AI 
framework for smart grids that decomposes agent behavior into five 
layers: perception, cognition (reasoning and planning), action, coordi-
nation, and explanation. This framework integrates reinforcement 
learning, multi-agent coordination, digital twins, and explainable AI 
(XAI). While highly relevant, it remains largely model-centric and does 
not fully incorporate the semantic abstraction, tool-augmented execu-
tion, and LLM-orchestrated reasoning that characterize the present 
taxonomy. Our proposed taxonomy explicitly positions LLMs as the 
central cognitive engine across all three functional levels, providing 
stronger support for contextual interpretation and human-AI collabo-
ration compared to the more traditional agent archetypes.
In the domain of digital twins, reference architectures such as ISO 
23247 [22] and RAMI 4.0 [23] offer structured layers for cyber-physical 
systems. These focus on data integration, simulation, and feedback loops 
but typically treat intelligence as modular add-ons rather than a unified 
cognitive orchestration layer. The six-layer architecture in this paper 
advances these models by placing LLM-based reasoning at the core, 
enabling dynamic task planning and semantic bridging between phys-
ical data and high-level governance.

3.6. Critical comparison with existing smart grid intelligence architectures
Before introducing the proposed six-layer architecture, it is necessary 
to position it against existing smart grid, multi-agent, digital twin, and 
LLM-based architectures. Prior research has produced several important 
architectural paradigms. Smart Grid Architecture Model (SGAM)-based 
frameworks provide a structured, technology-neutral reference model 
for describing smart grid interoperability across domains, zones, and 
layers. Traditional multi-agent system architectures support decentral-
ized coordination among grid actors and have been widely applied to 
virtual power plants, demand response, market operation, and distrib-
uted energy management. Digital twin architectures add cyber-physical 
simulation and feedback capabilities, enabling virtual representation, 
what-if analysis, and performance assessment of energy systems. More 
recently, agentic AI and LLM-based frameworks have introduced plan-
ning, reasoning, natural-language interaction, tool invocation, and 
multi-agent orchestration into power-system analysis and operation.
However, these architectural families remain limited in different 
ways. SGAM and related interoperability models provide strong struc-
tural representation but do not define a cognitive reasoning layer or 
LLM-mediated decision workflow. Classical multi-agent systems 
improve decentralization and scalability but usually rely on predefined 
rules, optimization logic, or communication protocols rather than se-
mantic reasoning and natural-language-grounded task decomposition. 
Digital twin frameworks enable simulation-based validation but often 
treat intelligence as an external analytical component rather than as an 
integrated cognitive orchestration layer. Recent LLM-based systems 
such as GAIA, GridMind, Grid-Agent, and X-GridAgent demonstrate 
promising capabilities in dispatch assistance, power-system analysis, 
solver invocation, and agent coordination, yet most remain task-specific 
and do not fully integrate semantic abstraction, multi-agent coordina-
tion, tool-augmented execution, digital twin validation, governance, 
and auditability within a single system-level architecture.

The reviewed literature demonstrates significant progress in indi-
vidual components such as LLM-assisted reasoning, multi-agent coor-
dination, and digital twin integration. However, these elements remain 
largely fragmented. To synthesize recurring design patterns identified 
across the 61 papers, we propose a six-layer conceptual architecture that 
organizes perception, reasoning, and action into a coherent framework. 
This architecture should be interpreted as an evidence-informed syn-
thesis rather than a fully implemented system.
The preceding sections synthesized the empirical evidence reported 
across the 61 reviewed papers. Building upon these evidence-driven 
findings, the following architecture represents the authors' conceptual 
synthesis rather than a direct extraction from any individual study. Each 
architectural layer is informed by recurring patterns identified in the 
literature; however, the overall six-layer integration constitutes the 
primary conceptual contribution of this work.
To address this gap, we propose an evidence-informed conceptual 
architecture that synthesizes recurring design patterns identified across 
the reviewed papers into a unified smart grid intelligence framework. 
The proposed architecture is not derived from any single publication and 
should not be interpreted as a fully validated deployment model. 
Instead, it represents a higher-level architectural synthesis that orga-
nizes the major technological capabilities reported in the literature into 
a coherent perception–reasoning–action structure.
The framework integrates six complementary layers: (1) Physical 
Infrastructure and Data Acquisition, (2) Data Processing and Context 
Abstraction, (3) LLM Cognitive Reasoning, (4) Multi-Agent 
Coordination, (5) Tool-Augmented Execution, and (6) Digital Twin 
Validation and Governance. Collectively, these layers establish a closed- 
loop intelligence cycle that combines contextual understanding, 
distributed decision-making, analytical execution, and continuous 
feedback.
A distinguishing characteristic of the proposed architecture is the 
integration of LLM-based reasoning, agentic coordination, digital twin 
validation, and governance mechanisms within a single system-level 
framework. Rather than replacing established engineering methods, 
the architecture positions LLMs as cognitive orchestration components 
that interact with specialized analytical tools, optimization engines, and 
operational control systems. This design supports bounded autonomy, 
explainability, safety, and human oversight, which are essential re-
quirements for future AI-enabled cyber-physical energy systems.

1. Physical Infrastructure and Data Acquisition Layer
This layer represents the foundational sensing and data generation 
infrastructure of the smart grid. It consists of heterogeneous data sources 
including smart meters, phasor measurement units (PMUs), IoT sensors, 
electric vehicles, and distributed renewable energy resources. These 
components continuously generate large volumes of time-series data, 
event logs, and operational signals reflecting the real-time state of the 
grid.
The primary role of this layer is to capture raw, high-frequency, and 
multi-modal data streams that serve as the basis for higher-level intel-
ligence. Given the distributed and dynamic nature of modern energy 
systems, this layer must ensure data reliability, temporal synchroniza-
tion, and secure transmission to upstream processing components.
2. Data Processing and Context Abstraction Layer
This layer transforms raw sensor data into structured and semanti-
cally enriched representations that can be effectively utilized by LLM- 
based reasoning systems. It includes data fusion, feature extraction, 
embedding generation, and knowledge graph construction to model 
relationships across heterogeneous energy assets.
A key contribution of this layer is context abstraction, where raw 
numerical signals are converted into meaningful representations such as 
grid states, anomalies, demand patterns, and operational contexts. By 
bridging the gap between low-level data and high-level reasoning, this 
layer enables LLMs to operate with structured, interpretable, and 
context-aware inputs.
3. LLM Cognitive and Reasoning Layer
At the core of the proposed architecture lies the LLM-based cognitive 
engine, which performs context understanding, multi-step reasoning, 
and decision generation. Unlike traditional AI models that operate on 
fixed pipelines, this layer enables flexible reasoning across multiple 
domains by leveraging large-scale pretrained knowledge combined with 
real-time contextual inputs.
The LLM operates as a reasoning orchestrator, capable of synthe-
sizing information from diverse sources, generating explanations, and 
formulating action plans. It supports advanced capabilities such as 
chain-of-thought reasoning, scenario analysis, and adaptive decision- 
making under uncertainty.
This layer is central to the transition from predictive analytics to 
cognitive intelligence, where decisions are not only data-driven but also 
context-aware and explainable.
4. Multi-Agent Coordination Layer
This layer operationalizes the agentic paradigm by decomposing 
complex grid operations into specialized agents, such as grid agents, EV 
agents, market agents, and security agents. Each agent is responsible for 
a specific functional domain and interacts with others through coordi-
nated protocols.
The LLM acts as a central coordinator, enabling communication, 
negotiation, and task allocation among agents. This distributed intelli-
gence framework enhances scalability, modularity, and robustness, 
allowing the system to manage complex, interdependent energy 
processes.
By enabling collaborative decision-making across multiple agents, 
this layer supports real-time adaptability and resilience in dynamic grid 
environments.
5. Tool-Augmented Execution Layer
This layer connects LLM-driven decisions to executable actions 
through domain-specific tools and control systems. It includes fore-
casting models, optimization engines, EMS/SCADA APIs, and control 
modules that directly interact with the physical grid.
The LLM dynamically selects and invokes appropriate tools based on

4.1. Illustrative use-case: LLM-driven voltage control in distribution 
networks
To illustrate the practical applicability of the proposed architecture, 
we consider a voltage control scenario in a distribution network with 
high penetration of distributed energy resources (DERs), including 
photovoltaic systems and electric vehicles. Maintaining voltage stability 
under dynamic generation and demand conditions remains a critical 
operational challenge.
Within the proposed framework, real-time data from sensors and 
grid components are processed in the data and context abstraction layer 
to generate structured representations of system states, such as voltage 
profiles and load patterns. These inputs are then analyzed by the LLM 
cognitive layer, which performs context-aware reasoning to detect po-
tential voltage violations and generate candidate corrective actions.
A key feature of this architecture is the integration of a multi-agentic 
AI layer, where specialized agents such as grid agents, EV agents, and 
demand response agents collaboratively negotiate and refine control 
strategies. The LLM acts as an orchestration engine, enabling coordi-
nated decision-making across distributed energy resources while 
resolving local conflicts and operational constraints.
The resulting decisions are validated and executed through optimi-
zation tools and control systems in the execution layer. Prior to 
deployment, actions are evaluated within a digital twin environment, 
enabling simulation-based validation and iterative refinement through a 
closed-loop feedback mechanism.
This use-case demonstrates how LLM-driven multi-agent coordina-
tion can support adaptive, interpretable, and system-level decision- 
making in energy systems. While illustrative, it highlights the potential 
of integrating LLM-based reasoning with multi-agent control to enhance 
operational efficiency and resilience in modern smart grids within a 
bounded and verifiable framework.

adaptive refinement and bounded autonomy. The architecture is 
designed to reflect the requirements of modern cyber-physical energy 
systems, including scalability, interoperability, resilience, explain-
ability, and operational safety.
Step 1: Physical Infrastructure and Data Acquisition
The workflow begins with the continuous collection of operational 
information from heterogeneous smart grid assets, including sensing 
devices, distributed energy resources, advanced metering infrastructure, 
electric vehicle ecosystems, renewable generation units, and supervisory 
control systems. These data streams represent the physical state of the 
grid and provide the informational foundation for all higher-level in-
telligence functions.
Given the distributed and dynamic nature of modern energy systems, 
this layer is responsible for ensuring data availability, synchronization, 
and quality before information is propagated to subsequent layers. The 
output of this stage consists of structured operational observations 
describing the current state of the cyber-physical environment.
Step 2: Data Processing and Context Abstraction
Raw measurements alone are insufficient for intelligent decision- 
making. Therefore, the second layer transforms heterogeneous numer-
ical and event-based data into semantically meaningful contextual 
representations.
This process may include data fusion, state estimation, feature 
extraction, semantic enrichment, and contextual interpretation. Opera-
tional conditions are represented as higher-level system states, such as 
congestion risks, voltage deviations, renewable variability, demand- 
response opportunities, or asset-health concerns. The resulting

contextual representation provides a grounded and interpretable view of 
the grid that can be processed by higher-level reasoning mechanisms.
Step 3: LLM Cognitive Reasoning and Decision Generation
Step 3: LLM Cognitive Reasoning and Decision Generation. The 
contextualized system state is forwarded to the LLM-based cognitive 
layer. Drawing on patterns observed in studies such as [15,29,16,26] the 
LLM performs contextual analysis, identifies operational challenges, and 
generates candidate decision plans using chain-of-thought reasoning 
and tool-calling interfaces. The output consists of explainable hypothe-
ses rather than direct control commands.
Step 4: Multi-Agent Coordination and Distributed Decision 
Refinement
Because smart grids comprise multiple interacting stakeholders and 
distributed resources, candidate decisions generated by the reasoning 
layer are further refined through multi-agent coordination mechanisms 
[30].
Specialized agents representing different functional domains such as 
distributed generation, demand response, energy storage, electric ve-
hicles, market participation, or system operations evaluate proposed 
actions according to local objectives and operational constraints. 
Through collaborative interaction, negotiation, and distributed 
decision-making processes, the agents reconcile local requirements with 
broader system-level goals [31–33].
This coordination layer enhances scalability and adaptability while 
reducing the limitations associated with centralized decision-making in 
highly distributed energy environments.
Step 5: Tool-Augmented Execution and Operational Planning
The coordinated decision plan is subsequently translated into 
executable operational actions through interaction with domain-specific 
analytical and optimization tools.
Examples include forecasting models, optimization engines, power- 
system analysis modules, scheduling frameworks, and energy manage-
ment platforms. Rather than directly executing actions, the LLM in-
teracts with these specialized tools to obtain numerically rigorous 
results, validate feasibility, and ensure compliance with operational 
constraints.
This hybrid reasoning paradigm combines the contextual intelli-
gence of LLMs with the reliability and precision of established engi-
neering methods, addressing a major limitation of purely language- 
based decision-making approaches [34,27,35,36].
To further clarify the execution mechanism, the output of this layer is 
not a direct autonomous command but a structured execution package. 
This package may include the selected analytical tool, required input 
variables, operational constraints, expected performance indicators, 
confidence level, fallback option, and the responsible human or auto-
mated control interface. Such a representation enables traceability be-
tween the LLM-generated reasoning, the agent-refined decision, the 
engineering tool output, and the final operational recommendation. In 
this way, the architecture separates cognitive planning from numerical 
optimization and physical actuation, reducing the risk of unsafe 
language-model-driven control.
Step 6: Digital Twin Validation and Governance
Before any action is considered for deployment, proposed decisions 
undergo verification within a digital twin and governance environment.
The digital twin provides a virtual representation of the physical 
grid, enabling simulation-based assessment of candidate actions under 
realistic operating conditions. Potential impacts on reliability, stability, 
resilience, and system performance can therefore be evaluated before 
implementation.
Simultaneously, governance mechanisms enforce operational pol-
icies, safety requirements, regulatory constraints, explainability re-
quirements, and trustworthiness criteria. This validation stage 
introduces a bounded-autonomy principle in which decision recom-
mendations must satisfy predefined operational and governance condi-
tions prior to execution as supported by works on closed-loop cyber- 
physical systems [37–39].
Step 7: Deployment, Monitoring, and Closed-Loop Feedback
Following successful validation, approved actions may be deployed 
through operational control and management systems. The resulting 
system behavior is continuously monitored and compared with expected 
outcomes generated during earlier stages of the workflow.
Observed outcomes are subsequently fed back into the architecture, 
enabling continuous adaptation, performance evaluation, and iterative 
refinement of future decisions. This closed-loop feedback process sup-
ports learning, resilience, and long-term system improvement while 
maintaining alignment with operational objectives and governance 
requirements.
Inter-Layer Interaction and System-Level Integration
Although presented sequentially for clarity, the proposed workflow 
should not be interpreted as a strictly linear process. Information ex-
change occurs across multiple layers through iterative feedback loops, 
enabling continuous interaction between perception, reasoning, coor-
dination, execution, and validation functions.
A defining characteristic of the architecture is the integration of 
cognitive intelligence, distributed agent coordination, tool-assisted 
analytical execution, and digital twin-based verification within a uni-
fied system-level framework. Collectively, these mechanisms transform 
fragmented AI functionalities into an integrated decision-support 
ecosystem capable of supporting future research on trustworthy, adap-
tive, and bounded-autonomy smart grid intelligence.
Importantly, the proposed workflow represents a conceptual opera-
tional blueprint synthesized from the current state of the literature. 
While the reviewed studies provide evidence for individual architectural 
components, comprehensive end-to-end validation of the complete 
framework remains an important direction for future research. This 
inter-layer design also establishes an audit trail across the full decision 
cycle. Each major output raw observation, contextual state, LLM- 
generated hypothesis, agent-refined plan, tool-derived result, digital- 
twin validation outcome, and final deployment decision can be logged 
and reviewed. This auditability is essential for trustworthy operation in 
critical energy infrastructures because it enables post-event analysis, 
accountability, human oversight, and regulatory inspection.
4.2.1. Illustrative operational use case
To further demonstrate how the proposed architecture can be oper-
ationalized, this section presents an illustrative use case involving the 
management of renewable generation variability during peak electricity 
demand. The objective is not to experimentally validate the framework 
but to demonstrate how the six architectural layers interact within a 
realistic smart grid decision-support scenario.
Assume that a utility operator observes an unexpected reduction in 
photovoltaic generation caused by rapidly changing weather conditions 
while electricity demand simultaneously increases during evening peak 
hours. Such situations require timely coordination among multiple grid 
resources while satisfying operational constraints and maintaining sys-
tem reliability.
The operational workflow begins at the Physical Infrastructure and 
Data Acquisition layer, where heterogeneous observations are continu-
ously collected from phasor measurement units (PMUs), smart meters, 
supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems, weather 
forecasting services, distributed energy resources, battery energy stor-
age systems, and electricity market feeds. These heterogeneous mea-
surements collectively represent the real-time operating state of the 
cyber-physical grid.
Within the Data Processing and Context Abstraction layer, the 
incoming data streams are synchronized, fused, and transformed into 
higher-level contextual representations. Rather than processing isolated 
sensor values, the system identifies a semantic operational situation 
characterized by renewable generation deficit, increasing system load, 
reduced operating reserve, and potential voltage stability concerns.
The contextual representation is subsequently forwarded to the LLM 
Cognitive Reasoning layer. Acting as a cognitive orchestration

4.3. Validation scope, technology maturity, and research readiness
The proposed six-layer architecture should be interpreted as an 
evidence-informed conceptual framework synthesized from recurring 
architectural patterns identified across the reviewed literature rather 
than as a fully validated deployment architecture. Although the indi-
vidual technological components underlying the framework have been 
investigated extensively in prior research, their integration into a uni-
fied LLM-driven, agentic, and governance-aware smart grid architecture 
remains an emerging research direction.
Importantly, the reviewed studies provide different levels of empir-
ical evidence for different architectural layers. Technologies associated 
with physical infrastructure, data acquisition, state estimation, optimi-
zation, and digital twin simulation have already demonstrated consid-
erable technical maturity through numerous experimental studies and 
industrial deployments. Likewise, multi-agent systems have been 
extensively investigated for distributed coordination, demand response, 
market operation, distributed energy resources, and virtual power plant 
management.
In contrast, recent advances in large language models, agentic arti-
ficial intelligence, and tool-augmented reasoning represent a rapidly 
evolving research frontier. Existing studies demonstrate promising ca-
pabilities in semantic reasoning, task decomposition, planning, natural- 
language interaction, and orchestration of engineering tools. However, 
these capabilities have primarily been evaluated within limited opera-
tional scenarios, prototype implementations, or domain-specific appli-
cations rather than fully integrated real-world power-system 
environments.
Consequently, the proposed framework should not be interpreted as 
evidence that fully autonomous smart grids have already been achieved. 
Instead, it illustrates a feasible architectural pathway toward bounded- 
autonomy, in which cognitive reasoning, engineering optimization, 
digital twin validation, governance constraints, and human supervision 
operate together to support trustworthy decision-making. Throughout 
the workflow, autonomy remains explicitly constrained by safety pol-
icies, regulatory requirements, engineering validation, explainability 
mechanisms, and human oversight before any operational deployment is 
considered.

4.4. Operational risks, trustworthiness, and safe deployment 
considerations
Although recent advances in large language models and agentic 
artificial intelligence have considerably expanded the capabilities of 
intelligent decision-support systems for smart grids, deploying these 
technologies within safety-critical cyber–physical infrastructures in-
troduces significant operational risks that cannot be overlooked. Unlike 
conventional ML models that perform narrowly defined prediction tasks, 
LLM-driven agentic systems integrate semantic reasoning, autonomous 
planning, external tool invocation, and multi-agent collaboration. 
Consequently, failures originating from the reasoning process may 
propagate through multiple architectural layers and eventually influ-
ence engineering decisions if appropriate safeguards are not enforced.
For this reason, the proposed architecture adopts the principle of 
bounded autonomy, whereby LLM-generated outputs are treated as 
decision hypotheses rather than executable control commands. 
Throughout the operational workflow, reasoning outputs are progres-
sively constrained through engineering optimization tools, digital twin 
verification, governance policies, and human supervision before any 
operational deployment is considered. This layered validation strategy 
aims to reduce operational risk while preserving the cognitive advan-
tages offered by LLMs.The principal risks associated with LLM-enabled 
smart grid intelligence and the corresponding mitigation mechanisms

Table 8.

4.4.2. Numerical reliability and engineering precision
Although LLMs demonstrate impressive reasoning capabilities, they 
remain unsuitable for precise numerical optimization tasks that require 
deterministic mathematical guarantees. Problems such as optimal power 
flow, unit commitment, state estimation, contingency analysis, and 
network optimization continue to require specialized engineering 
solvers [41,42]. Accordingly, the proposed architecture explicitly sep-
arates semantic reasoning from numerical computation. The LLM 
identifies candidate operational strategies, whereas external optimiza-
tion engines and analytical models perform all engineering calculations 
before recommendations proceed to subsequent validation stages. This 
separation significantly reduces the risk of numerical inconsistency 
while preserving the contextual reasoning capabilities of LLMs [43].
4.4.3. Latency and real-time operational constraints
Many smart grid applications require decision-making within strict 
temporal constraints. Cloud-based LLM inference may introduce 
communication delays that are incompatible with real-time protection 
or fast control functions [42,16]. Therefore, the proposed framework 
should primarily support supervisory decision-making, operational 
planning, and medium-timescale coordination rather than replacing 
low-level protective control. Hybrid deployment strategies combining 
edge intelligence, hierarchical control, and asynchronous reasoning 
provide a practical mechanism for reducing latency while maintaining 
operational responsiveness (Aggarwal and Irfan; [44]).
4.4.4. Cybersecurity, prompt injection, and unsafe tool execution
Integrating LLMs with operational technologies introduces new 
cybersecurity challenges beyond those encountered in conventional AI 
systems. Recent studies and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 
identify prompt injection, insecure output handling, excessive agency, 
and unsafe tool invocation as major risks for LLM-enabled systems [45,
46].
To mitigate these risks, the proposed architecture incorporates 
multiple defensive layers rather than relying solely on model-level 
safeguards. Retrieval sources should be trusted and authenticated, tool 
invocation should follow least-privilege principles, external actions 
should be constrained through policy engines and execution sandboxes, 
and all operational decisions should remain subject to governance 
validation and human authorization. Role-based access control, secure 
AI gateways, audit logging, and continuous monitoring further 
strengthen the trustworthiness of the overall architecture [45,47,43].
Collectively, these considerations reinforce an important principle of 
the proposed framework: LLMs should function as cognitive orchestra-
tors rather than autonomous controllers of physical infrastructure. Their 
primary role is to interpret heterogeneous operational contexts, coor-
dinate specialized analytical components, and support explainable 
decision-making. Engineering validation, digital twin simulation, 
governance mechanisms, and human supervision remain indispensable 
for ensuring operational safety. Consequently, the proposed six-layer 
architecture should be interpreted as an evidence-informed bounded- 
autonomy framework that integrates cognitive intelligence with deter-
ministic engineering verification rather than as a fully autonomous 
smart grid control system [21,43].
The proposed framework is intentionally designed as a bounded- 
autonomy decision-support architecture rather than a fully autono-
mous operational control system. Accordingly, deployment within 
safety-critical smart grid environments should be preceded by multiple 
assurance mechanisms. First, formal verification techniques should be 
employed wherever applicable to verify logical consistency, policy 
compliance, and safety constraints associated with automated decision 
workflows. Second, constraint-enforcement mechanisms should ensure 
that all candidate operational actions satisfy predefined engineering, 
regulatory, cybersecurity, and operational policies before execution is 
considered. Finally, human-in-the-loop supervision remains an indis-
pensable safeguard throughout the decision lifecycle. Human operators 
retain ultimate authority for approving, rejecting, or modifying opera-
tional recommendations after reviewing the outputs of engineering 
tools, digital twin simulations, and governance validation. Conse-
quently, the proposed architecture should be interpreted as supporting 
explainable and trustworthy decision-making rather than replacing 
human operators in safety-critical grid control.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs), agentic workflows, 
and tool-augmented reasoning introduces a qualitatively different 
paradigm. Rather than being limited to prediction or optimization, LLMs 
show potential to support interpretation, coordination, planning, and 
controlled action across grid subsystems [15,16,30,33]. The reviewed 
literature suggests that the primary value of LLMs in smart grids lies not 
in their linguistic capabilities per se, but in their capacity to serve as 
coordination layers linking data streams, domain-specific models, 
external tools, agents, and human operators. The six-layer architecture 
proposed in this paper represents an evidence-informed conceptual 
synthesis of these emerging patterns, not a claim that the literature has 
already produced a unified, empirically validated end-to-end system. 
The thematic synthesis in Tables 3–9 provides the empirical basis for this 
integration, while Tables 1 and 2 document the methodological rigor of 
the PRISMA-guided review.
A central insight from the evidence base is the functional progression 
of LLM roles in smart grids from interface, to reasoner, to agent. At the 
interface level, LLMs enhance usability through natural language 
interaction with grid data and operator queries [38,10]. At the reasoning 
level, they support contextual synthesis, scenario analysis, and solver 
integration [49]. At the agent level, they contribute to planning, 
multi-agent coordination, and tool-mediated execution [55,30,51]. 
Nevertheless, these roles exhibit clear maturity differences. Interface 
applications are already practical and low-risk, while reasoning-level 
hybrid systems are advancing rapidly. Fully agentic implementations, 
by contrast, remain emergent, with only limited evidence of robust 
performance under real-time, high-stakes, and safety-critical conditions. 
This asymmetry underscores that the field is currently in a transitional 
phase favoring bounded agency, supervised execution, and structured 
validation over unrestricted autonomy.
The thematic synthesis further reveals persistent fragmentation 
across technically interdependent layers. Studies on data acquisition 
focus on sensing and privacy-preserving flows [2,26], 
reasoning-oriented works emphasize dispatch and solver interaction 
[15,16,30,33], multi-agent research addresses coordination among 
DERs and prosumers [55,29,51], execution papers highlight tool invo-
cation [60,33,58], digital twin studies explore closed-loop validation [5,
37,38,79], and governance research examines explainability, compli-
ance, and cybersecurity [66,78,67,76]. While each cluster advances 
meaningful progress, few studies achieve full integration into a unified 
perception–reasoning–action pipeline. The primary conceptual contri-
bution of this review is therefore the synthesis of these dispersed ele-
ments into a coherent six-layer design logic that organizes existing 
capabilities and highlights necessary future developments for trust-
worthy system-level intelligence.
One of the strongest conclusions emerging from the corpus is that the 
most effective current applications of LLMs in smart grids are hybrid 
rather than standalone. Across multiple studies, LLMs demonstrate 
greatest utility when embedded within workflows that incorporate 
deterministic solvers, simulation environments, optimization engines, 
and human oversight [16,30,33,58]. In such configurations, LLMs 
function as semantic and procedural orchestrators that improve task 
decomposition, accessibility, and multi-step reasoning while relying on 
established engineering tools for numerical rigor and constraint satis-
faction. This pattern implies that future advances are more likely to arise 
from modular, hybrid architectures than from end-to-end black-box 
autonomy.
Semantic grounding emerges as another critical prerequisite. The 
quality of LLM-based reasoning depends heavily on transforming raw, 
heterogeneous sensor data, operational logs, policy rules, and engi-
neering constraints into structured, domain-grounded context [65,50]. 
Without dedicated context abstraction mechanisms such as ontologies, 
knowledge graphs, or retrieval-augmented generation LLMs risk pro-
ducing poorly grounded outputs that could lead to unsafe or 
non-compliant decisions. The proposed context abstraction layer is thus 
not merely theoretical but directly supported by the reviewed evidence 
as an essential mediator between physical infrastructure and cognitive 
processes.

a cross-layer function rather than an external overlay.
6.2. Challenges, risks, and deployment considerations
While the integration of LLMs into smart grid architectures offers 
promising capabilities for reasoning, coordination, and orchestration, 
several critical operational challenges must be addressed before wide-
spread deployment. The reviewed literature consistently highlights 
cybersecurity vulnerabilities, latency constraints, scalability limitations, 
and broader operational issues as key barriers to realizing bounded 
agentic intelligence in mission-critical energy systems.
Cybersecurity emerges as one of the most pressing concerns. Smart 
grids, as cyber-physical systems, are attractive targets for sophisticated 
attacks, including false data injection, denial-of-service, and adversarial 
manipulation of AI-driven control loops. Studies such as Zaboli et al. 
[77,76] demonstrate early applications of LLMs for anomaly detection in 
IEC 61850 digital substations and GOOSE/SV traffic analysis, showing 
improved adaptability compared to traditional methods. Sharshar et al. 
[78] reported 93% accuracy in identifying attack targets in Automatic 
Generation Control (AGC) systems using explainable LLM-based 
frameworks. However, LLMs themselves introduce new attack sur-
faces, including prompt injection, data poisoning, and 
hallucination-induced unsafe recommendations. Ibrahim and Kashef 
[80] emphasize that the “black-box” nature and resource-intensive 
operation of LLMs limit explainability and increase susceptibility to 
adversarial attacks in smart grid cybersecurity. Without robust verifi-
cation layers, RAG, knowledge graphs, or hybrid human-AI oversight, 
LLM-augmented systems risk amplifying rather than mitigating threats.
Latency presents a fundamental barrier for real-time grid operations. 
Power system applications such as voltage control, frequency regula-
tion, and protective relaying often require sub-second (or even milli-
second) response times. Several studies note that current LLM inference, 
even with optimization techniques, struggles to meet these re-
quirements. For instance, Jin et al. [16] highlighted performance con-
sistency across models but identified scalability and latency as open 
challenges in multi-agent power system analysis. Xiang and other [56] 
works on secure operation assistants similarly stress that real-time 
dispatch support remains constrained by inference delays. Edge 
deployment of smaller models or hybrid architectures (LLM + light-
weight surrogate models) is frequently proposed as a mitigation strat-
egy, yet quantitative evidence from large-scale utility environments 
remains limited.
Scalability challenges arise from the growing complexity and volume 
of data in modern grids with high DER penetration, EVs, and IoT devices. 
Many LLM-based frameworks tested on IEEE benchmark systems (e.g., 
118-bus) show promising results in controlled settings but face 
significant hurdles when extrapolated to utility-scale networks. Jung 
et al. [30] and Cheng et al. [15] acknowledge limitations in scaling to 
large grids, extreme-weather robustness, and cross-jurisdictional coor-
dination. Computational demands for training, fine-tuning, and infer-
ence further exacerbate scalability issues, particularly when integrating 
with existing SCADA/EMS infrastructures that were not designed for AI 
workloads.
Additional operational constraints include numerical reliability, 
interoperability with legacy systems, regulatory compliance, and energy 
consumption of AI infrastructure. Hybrid designs where LLMs orches-
trate rather than replace deterministic solvers and physics-based simu-
lators have proven most effective in addressing numerical stability 
concerns. Nevertheless, the literature underscores that full end-to-end 
autonomy remains premature. Bounded, supervised, and progressively 
validated implementations are necessary to align technological capa-
bilities with the stringent reliability, safety, and regulatory standards of 
power systems.
Addressing these challenges requires interdisciplinary efforts 
combining advances in efficient LLM inference (e.g., quantization, 
distillation, edge computing), domain-specific fine-tuning, formal veri-
fication methods, and integrated governance frameworks. Future 
research should prioritize large-scale pilot deployments, standardized 
benchmarks for latency and robustness, and co-design of cyber-resilient 
architectures that embed security, explainability, and constraint 
enforcement as first-class architectural principles.
From a deployment perspective, the literature supports a progres-
sive, staged pathway rather than abrupt transitions. The first phase 
operator assistance and natural-language interfaces is already observ-
able. The second phase reasoning systems integrated with solvers and 
simulators is emerging. The third phase bounded agentic coordination 
supported by digital twin validation, tool-mediated execution, and 
governance constraints remains early-stage and requires substantial 
further validation. Full autonomy in mission-critical grid operations is 
not a near-term objective. This phased perspective aligns technological 
maturity with operational risk management and helps avoid both 
excessive optimism and unwarranted skepticism.
Because the reviewed studies originate from multiple regions 
including North America, Europe, China, and the Middle East, the pro-
posed architecture is intended as a technology-neutral conceptual 
framework rather than a country-specific solution.
Overall, the future of intelligent smart grids will depend less on 
whether LLMs outperform specialized algorithms on isolated tasks and 
more on whether they can be effectively embedded within architectures 
that unify data, semantics, tools, agents, feedback, and governance into 
reliable, governable systems. The core contribution of this review is 
architectural: moving from model-centric toward system-level intelli-
gence while emphasizing safety, validation, and trustworthiness. The 
central question is no longer whether LLMs have a role in energy sys-
tems, but how to engineer bounded, trustworthy, and operationally 
meaningful implementations grounded in rigorous empirical validation.
6.3. Practical implications
Beyond its conceptual contribution, the proposed evidence-informed 
six-layer architecture provides a practical foundation for the next gen-
eration of intelligent energy management systems. By integrating se-
mantic reasoning, multi-agent coordination, engineering tool 
orchestration, digital twin validation, and governance mechanisms 
within a unified decision-support framework, the architecture offers a 
structured pathway for incorporating large language models into oper-
ational smart grid environments while maintaining safety, transparency, 
and human oversight.
One important application is the coordination of distributed 
renewable energy resources. As renewable penetration increases, power 
system operators must continuously balance intermittent generation, 
demand variability, storage assets, and network constraints. The

7. Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, although inter-rater reli-
ability was assessed on a subset of records (Cohen’s kappa = 0.82), the 
primary screening and data extraction were conducted by a single 
researcher, which may introduce some selection bias. Second, the final 
corpus of 61 studies reflects a deliberate focus on methodological depth 
and architectural relevance, potentially excluding broader but less 
directly relevant contributions. Third, the heterogeneity of methods and 
metrics across studies precluded quantitative meta-analysis. Finally, the 
proposed architecture constitutes a conceptual synthesis derived from 
thematic patterns in the literature rather than an empirically validated 
end-to-end system and therefore requires rigorous real-world testing, 
formal verification, and iterative refinement in operational smart grid 
environments.
8. Conclusion
This study provides a PRISMA-guided systematic review and 
evidence-informed architectural synthesis of the emerging role of large 
language models in smart grids. By analyzing 61 selected studies, the 
review documents a shift in smart grid intelligence from isolated, model- 
centric approaches toward more integrated, orchestration-driven 
systems.
A core contribution is the domain-specific taxonomy of LLM roles 
interface, reasoner, and agent that formalizes the progressive evolution 
of intelligence in energy systems. Building upon this taxonomy, the 
proposed six-layer architecture integrates physical data acquisition, se-
mantic context abstraction, LLM-based cognitive reasoning, multi-agent 
coordination, tool-augmented execution, and cross-layer digital twin 
validation with governance. This framework is presented as a concep-
tual synthesis grounded in the reviewed evidence, intended to organize 
fragmented research findings and guide future development toward 
bounded, validated, and trustworthy autonomy in cyber-physical energy 
systems.
The findings underscore that the near-term value of LLMs lies in 
hybrid orchestration rather than standalone decision-making. Critical 
gaps remain in scalability, latency, numerical reliability, semantic 
grounding, and standardized evaluation frameworks. Governance, trust, 
and safety mechanisms must be embedded as core architectural com-
ponents rather than added post hoc. Key future research directions 
include domain-specific efficient LLMs, federated reasoning architec-
tures, robust multi-agent protocols, large-scale digital twin integration, 
and unified governance frameworks.The proposed framework is 
particularly relevant for renewable-rich smart grids, distributed energy 
resource coordination, microgrid operation, and next-generation energy 
management systems.
In summary, the transition from model-centric AI to system-level 
agentic intelligence in smart grids represents a significant paradigm 
shift. This work offers a structured, evidence-based foundation for that 
transition and highlights the importance of rigorous validation, 
constraint enforcement, and institutional alignment to realize safe and 
effective deployment in critical energy infrastructure.
