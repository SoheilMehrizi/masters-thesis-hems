ABSTRACT
Intelligent operation and maintenance of energy equipment represents a critical component in ensuring the 
stable performance of new-generation power systems. Faced with complex operational conditions and nonlinear 
fault characteristics, conventional manual maintenance suffers from delayed perception and ambiguous cau-
sality. However, while digital twin technology can establish a virtual-real interaction space, its static modeling 
approach exhibits prediction failure in dynamic scenarios. To address these challenges, this study proposes an 
intelligent maintenance methodology based on cognitive agents and virtual-real co-evolution: constructing a 
dynamic environment representation model to achieve spatiotemporal feature correlation of equipment states 
and operational condition migration; designing a memory-planning-decision architecture to enhance causal 
reasoning capabilities for equipment faults and integrating with digital twin models for virtual-real interaction. 
The methodology is validated through an 18-month case study of a gas-steam boiler in a combined heat and 
power plant, utilizing 5.2 million historical operational records. Experimental results demonstrate that this 
approach achieves a 97.3 % accuracy rate in diagnosing non-stationary faults for gas-steam boiler equipment, 
realizes a 20-fold improvement in knowledge update time (from 48 to 2.3 h), and attains significant performance 
enhancements including 31.2 % cost efficiency improvement, 3-fold early warning lead time extension (from 24 
to 72 h), and 16.2 % overall collaborative performance improvement (from 82.2 % to 95.5 %). The research 
validates the engineering value of dynamic cognitive paradigms in intelligent maintenance of power equipment, 
providing a feasible solution for autonomous decision-making in high-real-time scenarios.
1. Introduction
Energy equipment constitutes the backbone of modern energy sys-
tems, with operational reliability directly impacting system stability and 
overall societal productivity [1]. As the integration of combined heat 
and power systems intensifies, the complexity and dynamism of power 
grids have escalated, presenting unprecedented challenges for the 
maintenance of critical assets such as gas-steam boilers, steam turbines, 
and heat recovery steam generators [2]. These high-value components 
routinely endure severe thermomechanical stresses, making equipment 
degradation and nonlinear fault evolution increasingly unavoidable. As 
failures occur, they not only disrupt energy supply—causing cascading 
effects on industrial production—but also pose significant risks to sys-
tem security through potential domino effects in interconnected in-
frastructures [3].
However, traditional maintenance strategies remain predominantly 
reliant on periodic inspections and reactive repairs. For instance, in gas- 
steam combined heat and power plants, maintenance still hinges on 
manual checks at fixed intervals, with technicians recording thermal- 
hydraulic parameters using paper logs or basic spreadsheets. This 
experience-driven approach faces critical limitations: 1) lack of sys-
tematic temporal correlation modeling in data analysis; 2) inflexible 
maintenance rules unable to adapt to equipment aging and variable load 
cycles; 3) fragmented human expertise, which cannot be formalized for 
broader knowledge transfer [4]. The consequences are severe: delayed 
fault detection, unplanned shutdowns for diagnosis, and increased 
operational costs, all of which threaten the reliability and safety of en-
ergy systems.
Amid these challenges, the modernization of maintenance method-
ologies has become an urgent priority. The widespread adoption of 
sensing technologies and industrial Internet platforms has enabled the 
real-time collection of vast volumes of multi-modal operational data. 
Yet, transforming this heterogeneous, high-velocity data into actionable 
knowledge for improving equipment reliability and lifecycle

enabling the construction of self-evolving operational paradigms. The

virtual-real interaction to address the core challenges of intelligent 
maintenance in energy equipment. The term agentic is used to empha-
size a higher level of cognitive autonomy and goal-oriented reasoning, 
distinguishing it from conventional multi-agent systems where indi-
vidual agents often operate based on predefined rules. Our agentic 
paradigm centralizes intelligence within the DT, enabling it to dynam-
ically plan, delegate, and coordinate tasks in a holistic manner. Specif-
ically, the proposed methodology overcomes the static modeling 
limitations of conventional digital twins by enabling dynamic state- 
space construction through cognitive agents and establishes a formal-
ized knowledge acquisition bridge between symbolic expert reasoning 
and sub-symbolic sensor data. Moreover, it is designed to meet the ur-
gent industrial need for self-evolving, real-time maintenance architec-
tures, aiming to resolve a significant majority of faults within boiler on- 
load maintenance windows, and is broadly applicable to key thermal 
power infrastructures such as heat exchangers and steam distribution 
systems [8]. This paradigm shift towards autonomous digital twin 
technology represents a significant advancement in thermal power 
system automation, aligning with the broader trends of Industry 4.0 and 
the Internet of Things (IoT) in combined heat and power applications. As 
illustrated in the proposed framework (Fig. 1b), the tool use capability 
acts as a key input to the agent’s Cognitive Core, demonstrating the

labor but also improves the timeliness and accuracy of maintenance 
actions, potentially leading to better asset performance and longer 
lifespans. Furthermore, intelligent systems can enhance their 
decision-making capabilities through continuous learning and adapta-
tion to data, providing dynamic and evolving maintenance strategies 
that traditional systems find hard to match. While the system is designed 
for a high degree of autonomy in diagnostics and planning, it operates 
within a HITL framework. Critical maintenance decisions and actions 
proposed by the agentic DT are presented to human operators for final 
verification and approval, ensuring ultimate safety, accountability, and 
control.

2. Related work
2.1. Digital twin and intelligent equipment maintenance
Recent years have witnessed a paradigm shift toward data-driven 
predictive maintenance, driven by advancements in the Artificial In-
telligence of Things (AIoT), big data analytics, and the Industrial 
Internet of Things (IIoT). Cutting-edge solutions now integrate multi- 
source sensor fusion, deep learning, and edge-cloud collaborative 
computing to enable comprehensive health monitoring and predictive 
maintenance in complex industrial environments. For instance, deep 
learning frameworks and ensemble learning algorithms for equipment 
health monitoring now enable early abnormality detection via hierar-
chical feature engineering and have demonstrated impressive capabil-
ities in remaining useful life estimation and adaptive maintenance 
scheduling across diverse sectors [10]. Concurrently, advanced state 
observers using extended Kalman networks demonstrate improved 
tracking accuracy under nonlinear system dynamics [11], while multi-
modal knowledge graphs consolidate structured troubleshooting pro-
tocols with unstructured maintenance logs for enhanced diagnostics 
[12].
The integration of emerging LLMs and autonomous intelligent agents 
further expands the potential of maintenance systems by enabling nat-
ural language understanding, intelligent reasoning, and knowledge- 
driven decision-making. The LLM-enabled system synthesis (LESS) 
framework demonstrates intelligent production planning through 
formalized procedure encoding [13]. Revolutionary RCAgent architec-
tures integrate LLMs with RESTful API agents, achieving 35 % faster 
root cause localization in cloud systems [14]. Notably, temporal 
semanticization algorithms combined with multi-agent reinforcement 
learning(MARL) show 40 % performance gains in industrial anomaly 
prediction through natural language-based collaboration protocols [7]. 
Recent studies have also explored LLMs for automated fault report 
generation and context-aware root cause analysis, significantly 
enhancing transparency and interpretability. Furthermore, MAS coor-
dinated by Reinforcement Learning(RL) or federated learning ap-
proaches have shown promise in distributed equipment monitoring and 
collaborative diagnosis, especially in scenarios requiring real-time 
adaptation and decentralized autonomy.
In this context, DT technologies have revolutionized equipment 
monitoring through high-fidelity virtual modeling. Yu et al. proposed a 
digital twin framework for tunnel operation and maintenance (O&M), 
using Semantic Web tech for multi-level data fusion and a rule-based 
engine for reasoning [15]. Li’s multidimensional DT architecture for 
hydraulic infrastructure enables closed-loop health management 
through sensor-cloud integration [16]. However, despite these ad-
vancements in creating high-fidelity models, key challenges persist in 
real-time heterogeneous data fusion and expert knowledge embedding 
within decision engines. Moreover, static DT implementations reveal 
fundamental limitations in nonlinear fault propagation scenarios due to 
three major constraints: 1) Parametric drift across equipment aging 
phases, 2) Epistemic uncertainty in multivariable state estimation, and 
3) Temporal misalignment between virtual representation and physical 
dynamics [5]. The relevant works have been illustrated in Table 1.

strate significant progress in leveraging multimodal data—spanning 
text, time series, images, and code—to enhance specific maintenance- 
related functions. For instance, in robotics and human-robot

However, a critical analysis of these studies reveals that they often

2.2. Research gap and motivation
Based on the above literature review, it is evident that while signif-
icant progress has been made in intelligent equipment maintenance, 
several research gaps persist: 
1) Lack of a Continuously Self-Evolving Maintenance Paradigm. Exist-
ing digital twin maintenance frameworks predominantly rely on 
static or semi-static models, which are insufficient for capturing the 
dynamic, nonlinear, and evolving nature of equipment degradation 
and fault propagation in real-world environments [31]. These 
models also typically lack the capability for closed-loop cognitive 
adaptation that leverages both symbolic and sub-symbolic reasoning 
to continuously refine their understanding and strategies [32].
2) Insufficient Interpretability and Adaptability in Fault Diagnosis. 
Most current predictive maintenance and diagnostics approaches are 
limited by the semantic gap between symbolic knowledge (e.g., 
expert rules, ontologies) and sub-symbolic models (e.g., deep 
learning) [9]. This restricts their ability to provide interpretable, 
transparent, and adaptive fault diagnosis, especially under 
non-stationary and variable operational conditions. Moreover, there 
is a lack of robust causal reasoning frameworks that can bridge this 
gap and enable explainable root-cause analysis [33].
3) Limited Autonomy and Optimization in Multi-Agent Coordination. 
The real-world deployment of intelligent maintenance systems often 
involves large-scale, distributed assets requiring collaborative diag-
nosis, resource allocation, and adaptive scheduling. However, 
traditional MAS often lack self-evolving optimizing coordination 
mechanisms, especially under the uncertainty, heterogeneity, and 
real-time constraints typical of energy infrastructure operations 
[34].
To solve the above research gaps, this work provides a universal, 
intelligent maintenance framework that advances the state-of-the-art in 
industrial asset management, offering new directions for the develop-
ment of autonomous, real-time operational paradigms in the era of In-
dustry 4.0 and IIoT.
3. Framework for agentic maintenance
3.1. Overall framework of the agentic maintenance
The proposed framework for agentic maintenance is built upon the 
foundational principles of a digital twin, establishing a dynamic and 
symbiotic relationship between physical and virtual systems. This 
duality is central to enhancing the operation and maintenance of energy 
equipment, such as gas-steam boilers and heat recovery systems. Our 
approach aligns with the conceptual architecture of digital twins as 
outlined in standards like ISO 23247, which emphasizes the integration 
of physical assets with their virtual counterparts through data exchange 
[35]. By combining real-time monitoring, predictive analytics, and 
autonomous decision-making, the framework ensures stable perfor-
mance in next-generation power systems. The overall architecture is 
illustrated in Fig. 2, which highlights the bidirectional data flow be-
tween the Physical System and Virtual System, emphasizing their dy-
namic and collaborative interaction.
The Virtual System’s components interact synergistically, with 
bidirectional data flows between the Environment Perception/Cognition 
Model and the Condition-Based Decision (CBD)/Predictive Model (rep-
resented as the Maintenance Algorithm/Policy in Fig. 2). This holistic 
integration addresses the limitations of static modeling approaches, such 
as prediction failures in dynamic scenarios, by enabling adaptive system 
analysis and real-time decision-making.
3.1.1. Physical system
The Physical System forms the foundation of the framework and 
comprises the following key elements:
1) Energy Assets. These are the core physical entities, such as gas-steam 
boilers, heat recovery steam generators, or other thermal power 
equipment, whose operational states are continuously monitored 
and maintained. They represent the primary focus of the framework, 
ensuring reliable performance and operational stability under com-
plex and dynamic conditions.
2) Sensors. Sensors are mounted on the Energy Assets to collect real- 
time data originating from them, capturing their current state, 
denoted as S(t). This data is then transmitted to the Virtual System 
for analysis. The bidirectional interaction with Energy Assets, 
depicted in Fig. 2, represents a continuous flow of information, 
enabling real-time insights into equipment’s performance and 
condition.
3) Actuators. Actuators are physical devices (e.g., valves, motors, ro-
botic systems) that execute commands received from the Virtual 
System, denoted as M(t), enabling physical adjustments or mainte-
nance actions on Energy Assets. The framework is designed to sup-
port varying levels of autonomy. For routine adjustments or well- 
defined corrective tasks, actions can be applied autonomously 
without direct human intervention. For more complex or critical 
maintenance activities, the system can operate in a HITL mode, 
where it provides recommendations for a human technician to vali-
date and execute. The bidirectional connection with Energy Assets 
establishes a feedback mechanism, where operational adjustments 
influence the system’s state, completing the control loop. This 
configuration ensures that the Physical System provides a robust 
foundation for data collection and action execution, addressing the 
challenges posed by nonlinear fault characteristics and complex 
operational conditions.

The Virtual System serves as the intelligent core of the framework 
and comprises several interconnected components designed to process 
data, predict outcomes, and make informed decisions:

3) Cognitive Maintenance Agent.
The Cognitive Maintenance Agent orchestrates higher-level decision-

reasoning and planning capabilities can be implemented using LLMs, 
which allow the agent to interpret complex instructions, reason about 
unseen situations, and generate coherent maintenance plans [36]. It 
comprises three interconnected modules: 
1) Memory. A repository of historical data and learned experiences, 
providing context for current and future decisions. Fig. 2 depicts 
Memory feeding into the Planning module via work memory 
extraction, emphasizing its role in leveraging past data for informed 
decision-making.
2) Planning. A module that formulates maintenance strategies based on 
Memory and predictive inputs from the CBD/Predictive Model. 
Iterative feedback loops, depicted in Fig. 2 as circular arrows with 
colored dots (orange, purple, and gray), refine plans dynamically.

The framework leverages cognitive agents and virtual-real co-evo-
lution to extend beyond static modeling approaches. By dynamically 
correlating spatiotemporal features of equipment states with operational 
condition migration, the framework addresses challenges such as 
nonlinear fault characteristics and enhances causal reasoning capabil-
ities. The bidirectional interaction between the Physical System and 
Virtual System exemplifies the engineering value of dynamic cognitive 
paradigms in intelligent maintenance. This provides a scalable and 
adaptive solution for next-generation power systems.
3.2. Interaction and dynamic feedback loop of virtual-physical systems
The Cognitive Maintenance Framework operates based on a closed- 
loop principle, ensuring seamless interaction between the Physical and 
Virtual Systems. This bidirectional feedback mechanism is designed to 
address complex operational conditions, such as nonlinear fault evolu-
tion and dynamic environmental changes, while enabling real-time 
responsiveness and autonomous decision-making. The interaction be-
tween the Physical System and Virtual System is characterized by the 
following steps:
Step 1: The Physical System continuously transmits its current state 
S(t) to the Virtual System via sensors. This establishes a data flow from 
the physical domain to the virtual domain, providing real-time insights 
into equipment performance and operational conditions. The state data 
includes multi-modal information such as temperature, pressure, vi-
bration, and flow rates, ensuring comprehensive monitoring.
Step 2: The Virtual System processes the received state data S(t) 
through predictive models, memory-driven analysis, and cognitive 
agents. It generates decision commands M(t) based on the equipment’s 
current state and anticipated operational changes. These commands 
reflect actions such as equipment adjustments, fault mitigation

The practical challenges of implementing a fully autonomous closed-

The system can operate in a HITL configuration where critical decisions

actions and feedback over time. This enables progressive codification of 
intrinsic expertise [37].
The closed-loop mechanism integrates advanced sensing, reasoning, 
and execution capabilities, offering several key features: 
1) Real-Time Responsiveness. Sensors and actuators ensure uninter-
rupted data collection and immediate action execution, critical for 
high-real-time scenarios. This capability minimizes delays in fault 
detection and response, addressing challenges such as ambiguous 
causality in conventional maintenance methods.
2) Predictive Maintenance. By leveraging predictive models and his-
torical data stored in the Memory module, the framework anticipates 
potential issues before they escalate. The efficacy of this predictive 
approach has been validated in case studies, showing the potential 
for significant error reduction in predictive maintenance tasks for 
energy equipment [8].
3) Adaptive Decision-Making. The Cognitive Maintenance Agent en-
hances the system’s ability to learn from past experiences and 
dynamically adapt to changing operational conditions. Iterative 
feedback loops in the Planning module refine maintenance strategies 
over time, contributing to high accuracy rates in diagnosing non- 
stationary faults [38], as will be demonstrated in the experimental 
section of this paper.
4) Integration of External Tools. The CBD/Predictive Model integrates 
external tools for advanced analytics, enhancing forecasting capa-
bilities and operational efficiency. This feature ensures that the 
framework remains versatile and scalable for diverse industrial 
applications.
In brief, this closed-loop interaction mechanism embodies the engi-
neering value of dynamic cognitive paradigms in intelligent mainte-
nance. By continuously correlating spatiotemporal features of 
equipment states and operational condition migration, the framework 
provides a feasible solution for autonomous decision-making in high- 
real-time scenarios. As will be highlighted in experimental results, the 
framework can achieve significant improvements in maintenance 
response efficiency, reducing downtime and operational costs while 
enhancing system reliability.
3.3. Definitions for core processes
The framework’s dynamics are formalized using a state-space rep-
resentation. This approach is conceptually analogous to established 
frameworks for modeling sequential decision-making under uncer-
tainty, such as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and RL, which pro-
vide a robust mathematical foundation for the interaction between an 
agent and its environment [39]. This Section details the core processes 
of our proposed system by expanding on the construction of its dynamic 
models. 
1) Dynamics of the Physical System 
The physical system’s state evolves based on its current state S(t) 
and the decision command M(t) imposed by the virtual system. This 
evolution is governed by the state transition function f: 
S(t+ 1)= f(S(t), M(t)) (1) 
Where S(t) represents the state vector of the system at time t (e.g., 
temperature, pressure), M(t) is the command executed at time t (e.g., 
adjust valve position), and f is the state transition function. The 
construction of f can follow two primary approaches. It can be a 
physics-based model, derived from first-principles equations such as 
thermodynamic and fluid dynamic laws governing energy equip-
ment. Alternatively, it can be a data-driven model, such as a 
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) or a Long Short-Term Memory 
(LSTM) network, trained on historical operational data to learn the 
complex, nonlinear dynamics of the equipment. For instance, in a 
gas-steam boiler, f would model how steam pressure Sp(t+ 1) changes 
based on the current pressure Sp(t), feed water temperature ST(t), and 
a fuel intake adjustment Mfuel(t).
2) Dynamics of the Virtual System 
The virtual system processes the observed state S(t) to update its 
internal state V(t) and generate a decision command M(t). 
The internal state update is formalized as: 
V(t+ 1)= h(V(t), S(t)) (2) 
Here, h represents the evolution function of the virtual system’s 
internal state. This function models the cognitive process of memory 
update. For example, the new state S(t) is stored in Short-Term 
Memory (STM), and h governs how this new information is used to 
retrieve relevant historical data from Long-Term Memory (LTM) and 
assemble the current Working Memory (WM), which forms part of V 
(t+ 1). 
The decision command generation is formalized as: 
M(t)= g(S(t), V(t)) (3) 
The function g is the core decision-making policy implemented by 
the cognitive maintenance agent. It takes the current physical state S 
(t) and the agent’s internal cognitive state V(t) (containing context 
from memory) as input to produce a maintenance action M(t). This 
function can be realized as a sophisticated planning algorithm, a 
rule-based inference engine, or a learned policy from a reinforcement 
learning model, as detailed in Section 3.5.2. It essentially translates 
cognitive understanding into actionable commands.
3) Dynamics of the Closed-Loop System 
The interaction between the physical and virtual systems forms a 
closed-loop system, ensuring continuous feedback and adaptation. 
The overall dynamics are thus described by coupling the above 
equations: 
S(t+ 1)= f(S(t), g(S(t), V(t))) (4) 
V(t+ 1)= h(V(t), S(t)) (5) 
This set of equations captures the essence of the feedback loop. Eq. 
(4) shows that the physical system’s next state S(t+ 1) is determined 
by its current state S(t) and the action g(S(t), V(t)) generated by the 
virtual system. Eq. (5) shows that the virtual system updates its own 
internal state V(t+ 1) based on its previous state V(t) and the new 
observation S(t) from the physical world, thus informing the next 
cycle of decision-making.

3.4. Data flow
The Cognitive Maintenance Framework is driven by a continuous, 
multi-modal stream of data originating from the physical system. The 
state data S(t) is not a monolithic entity but a vector comprising various 
sensor readings, each serving a distinct function within the digital twin 
system. For the gas-steam boiler case study detailed later, this data in-
cludes: 1) Temperature Data: Readings such as flue gas temperature, 
feed water temperature, and steam temperature are crucial. They 
function as primary indicators for monitoring the boiler’s thermal effi-
ciency, ensuring complete combustion, and detecting overheating risks 
that could lead to material fatigue or failure. 2) Pressure Data: Mea-
surements like steam pressure and control target pressure are essential 
for both safety and performance. They are used to maintain operational 
stability within safe limits, prevent over-pressurization events, and 
ensure the steam produced meets the requirements of the downstream 
turbine. 3) Flow Data: Data on cumulative and instantaneous inlet water 
flow and outlet steam flow are vital for assessing the boiler’s real-time 
output and efficiency. Discrepancies between inlet and outlet flows 
can indicate potential leaks or blockages, triggering diagnostic routines 
within the virtual system. 4) Operational Parameters: Other data points 
like unit load, thermal efficiency calculations, and conductivity provide 
higher-level context about the equipment’s operational mode and 
health, enabling the cognitive agent to make more informed, context- 
aware decisions.
This rich set of sensor data provides the foundation for the 
framework’s data flow, which is structured to ensure seamless interac-
tion and decision-making between its components. The framework 
correlates spatiotemporal features from these data streams with opera-
tional condition migration, addressing challenges such as nonlinear fault 
characteristics and enhancing causal reasoning capabilities. Table 3

the framework to continuously refine its strategies.

predictive, and adaptive maintenance, this framework addresses the 
challenges of complex operational conditions and nonlinear fault char-

of next-generation power systems.

3.5.2. Self-evolving operational maintenance
The agentic digital twin-embedded maintenance system implements 
a sophisticated operational maintenance architecture designed for 
autonomous, adaptive, and predictive maintenance. This architecture is 
built upon the real-time virtual model of the physical system (the digital 
twin) and is operationalized by a team of specialized agents. Their 
collaboration, governed by principles of closed-loop cognition and 
optimization, directly addresses the core challenges of dynamic 
operational environments and nonlinear fault characteristics.
The architecture revolves around the digital twin, which continu-
ously updates with sensor data to reflect the equipment’s current state. 
The specialized agents are assigned distinct roles to create a seamless 
workflow for anomaly detection, strategy optimization, task execution, 
and process refinement. The key agent roles within this architecture 
include: 
1) Monitoring Agents: Detect anomalies using real-time sensor data and 
environmental conditions.
2) Planning Agents: Devise optimal maintenance strategies through 
simulations, leveraging historical and predictive insights.
3) Execution Agents: Implement planned maintenance actions on the 
physical system.
4) Feedback Agents: Analyze outcomes to refine strategies and update 
the memory system.
5) Maintenance Task Agents: Coordinate tasks, assign sub-tasks to 
execution agents, and ensure system-wide synchronization.
This role-based agent collaboration enables a self-improving, closed- 
loop operation, where real-time data, simulations, and feedback 
collectively drive efficient and continuously optimized maintenance. 
Fig. 6 illustrates the mechanism of this self-evolving operational 
maintenance.
To orchestrate the collaboration among these agents and continu-
ously enhance their collective decision-making, the framework in-
tegrates a MARL algorithm. The MARL model is embedded primarily 
within the Planning and Feedback loops of the architecture. Specifically, 
Planning Agents use the MARL policy to determine the optimal joint 
maintenance strategy, considering inputs from Monitoring Agents and 
the Memory system. After Execution Agents perform the tasks, Feedback 
Agents collect the results and calculate a global reward. This reward is 
then used to update the MARL policy, allowing the agent team to learn 
from experience and adapt its collaborative strategies over time. This 
integration ensures that the maintenance decision-making process is not 
only reactive but also self-optimizing. The pseudo-code of the Q- 
learning-based MARL approach is described in Algorithm 3 [41].

Coordination optimization of decision-making agents achieves dy-
namic optimization allocation of maintenance resources through Q- 
learning enhanced resource allocation strategies. When facing multi- 
task concurrency and resource conflict scenarios, the collaborative 
decision-making mechanism can generate Pareto-optimal resource 
allocation schemes through game theory models and multi-objective 
optimization algorithms. Experimental results show that in terms of 
resource utilization, multi-agent collaboration achieves a 31 % 
improvement over traditional centralized scheduling, and a 24 % 
improvement in task completion timeliness. Particularly during high- 
load operation periods, when multiple maintenance requirements 
occur simultaneously, the collaborative mechanism can complete 
rescheduling within an average of 4.2 min, ensuring priority execution 
of critical tasks. Closed-loop control of execution feedback further en-
sures maintenance quality by real-time monitoring of execution pro-
cesses and result feedback, enabling the system to promptly detect and 
correct execution deviations, achieving a maintenance task success rate 
of 98.7 %. Long-term performance evolution of self-evolutionary opti-

Reinforcement learning-driven strategy optimization plays an 
important role in improving maintenance decision quality. As intro-
duced in Section 3.5.2, this study applied the Q-learning-based MARL 
approach (Algorithm 3) to train the decision-making agents. For the 
specific application on the boiler maintenance task, the MARL frame-
work was configured as follows:
State Space (S): The state was defined by a vector of discretized 
sensor readings, including key indicators for flue gas temperature, steam 
pressure, and water flow rate, representing the boiler’s operational 
condition.
Action Space (A): The agents’ collective action space consisted of a 
set of predefined maintenance commands, such as ’No Action’, ’Adjust 
Fuel-Air Ratio’, ’Schedule Soot Blowing’, and ’Initiate Emergency 
Shutdown Procedure’.
Reward Function (R): A composite reward function was engineered 
to guide the learning process. It provided positive rewards for main-
taining operation within optimal efficiency ranges and negative rewards 
(penalties) for actions leading to safety threshold breaches, unplanned 
downtime, or high operational costs.

5. Conclusions
This paper addresses the urgent need for intelligent, adaptive, and 
self-evolving maintenance strategies for energy equipment operating 
under complex and dynamic conditions. To tackle the limitations of 
conventional digital twin-based maintenance (particularly their static 
modeling and lack of cognitive autonomy), this paper proposes an 
agentic digital twin-embedded maintenance methodology. This meth-
odology is characterized by the integration of cognitive agents and DT- 
based virtual-real co-evolution, enabling dynamic state-space modeling, 
closed-loop reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. Experimental 
validation on gas-steam boiler equipment demonstrates the effective-
ness of the proposed methodology: achieving a 97.3 % accuracy rate in 
diagnosing non-stationary faults, 20-fold improvement in knowledge 
update responsiveness, 31.2 % enhancement in cost efficiency, and 
comprehensive performance improvements, including 16.2 % increase 
in overall collaborative effectiveness and 98.7 % maintenance task 
success rate. The system demonstrates remarkable adaptability with 
9.1 % continuous improvement in fault prediction accuracy over 18 
months (from 89.2 % to 97.3 %) and an 80.7 % reduction in system 
calibration time. These results substantiate the engineering value and 
practical applicability of dynamic cognitive paradigms for intelligent 
maintenance in energy systems.
The broader significance of this research lies in its potential to 
advance the field of industrial asset management by enabling autono-
mous, interpretable, and real-time maintenance operations. The meth-
odology supports enhanced system reliability, reduced operational 
costs, and extended asset lifespans—outcomes that are critically 
important for the sustainable development of next-generation power 
systems in the context of Industry 4.0 and the Industrial Internet of 
Things.
Despite its demonstrated success, the methodology has certain lim-
itations. First, the approach’s performance may be affected by the 
availability and quality of multi-modal data streams, as well as by the 
accuracy of the underlying digital twin models. Second, while the 
agentic coordination mechanism has shown robustness in tested sce-
narios, its scalability and generalization to highly heterogeneous or 
cross-domain equipment fleets require further investigation. Finally, the 
interpretability of hybrid symbolic-neural models, though improved, 
still presents challenges in highly complex or ambiguous fault cases.
Future research will focus on expanding the universality and scal-
ability of the agentic digital twin paradigm, including: 1) Developing 
more advanced data fusion and transfer learning techniques to handle 
sparse or imbalanced datasets; 2) Enhancing the explainability of causal 
reasoning models; 3) Integrating cross-domain knowledge for multi-type 
equipment ecosystems; These efforts aim to further strengthen the au-
tonomy, adaptability, and resilience of intelligent maintenance systems 
for energy and other high-value industrial assets.
